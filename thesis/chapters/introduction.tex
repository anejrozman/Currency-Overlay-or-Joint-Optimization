
\chapter{Introduction}\label{ch:introduction}

\section{Background and Motivation}
 
Portfolio optimization studies how an investor should divide their wealth among different assets. Markowitz
\cite{Markowitz1952} proposes to choose the portfolio weights by trading off the expected return of the portfolio
against its risk, which he measures by the variance of the portfolio return. A central insight of his work is that
combining assets whose returns do not move together lowers the risk of the portfolio. Since then, portfolio
optimization has become a standard tool in asset management. An important difficulty in practice is that the expected
returns and the risks of the assets are unknown and have to be estimated from historical data.

Investing abroad gives an investor access to assets from more economies and industries. Their returns depend in part
on risk factors that do not drive the domestic market, so adding them to the portfolio can improve its risk-return
profile. At the same time, these assets expose the portfolio to changes in exchange rates. If a foreign currency loses
value against the domestic currency, the investor can lose money on the assets in that currency, even if they earn a
positive return in their local currency. The investor can manage this currency risk with currency forward contracts.
An international portfolio is therefore described by two sets of decisions: how to allocate the assets and how to set
the currency exposures. In practice, many investors make these decisions in two steps. They first choose the asset
portfolio and then add a portfolio of currency positions, called a currency overlay, to manage its currency risk, often
through a separate overlay manager \cite{SparseAndStableInternationalPortfolioOptimization}.

The alternative to this sequential approach is joint optimization, which chooses the asset weights and the currency
exposures together in one step. Jorion \cite{Jorion1994} argues that the two-step approach is suboptimal, since it
ignores how the individual assets and the exchange rates move together. On the historical data used to choose the
portfolio, joint optimization can never do worse than the overlay, since it can always choose the
same portfolio. However, what matters for an investor is how the portfolio performs in the future, out of sample.
Because joint optimization fits all weights to the same data at once, it can also adapt more closely to random noise, 
and its advantage can disappear out of sample \cite{SparseAndStableInternationalPortfolioOptimization}.
Regularization, which keeps the portfolio weights from becoming too extreme, is a common way to reduce this problem.

Many investors use a currency overlay, often run by a separate manager. If joint optimization works better out of
sample, these investors could improve their portfolios by making the asset and the currency decisions together. The
existing evidence, however, does not give a clear answer. Burkhardt and Ulrych
\cite{SparseAndStableInternationalPortfolioOptimization} study regularized mean-variance portfolios, which include the
expected returns in the objective, and find that joint optimization achieves on average higher risk-adjusted returns
out of sample than the overlay. Lucescu and Ulrych \cite{RegularizedMultiCurrencyExpectedShortfallPortfolios} minimize
expected shortfall instead, which measures the average of the largest losses of a portfolio, and find that joint
optimization performs better in the smaller of their two investable universes. The two studies differ in the risk
measure and in whether expected returns are used in the optimization, and the second study shows that the answer can also depend
on the number of assets that make up the investable universe. This thesis builds on their frameworks and studies whether optimizing the asset weights and
the currency exposures jointly improves on the sequential currency overlay out of sample, and how the answer depends on
the risk measure, the size of the investable universe, regularization, and a mean term in the objective.

We study this question for a US investor, using daily stock and currency data for 14 economies from 1990 to 2023.
We form three investable universes from the 10, 50, and 100 largest stocks of each economy, up to $140$, $700$, and
$1{,}400$ stocks in total.


\section{Contributions}

In this thesis, we make four contributions. First, we compare joint and sequential optimization under five risk
measures, in three investable universes of increasing size, with and without regularization. Without a mean term,
the joint and the overlay optimizers achieve very similar risk-adjusted returns, and the main difference is that the
joint optimizers achieve a lower risk. Out of sample, their advantage in the risk measure they minimize 
is largest in the smallest universe, where the currency exposures make up a larger share of the decision 
variables. In the larger universes, the asset step of the overlay program has more weights to fit 
the objective on its own, and the advantage shrinks until it disappears in the largest universe.

Second, we add a mean term to the programs of all five risk measures, with expected asset returns that are pooled by
economy, and expected currency returns that are derived from the forward premia. The mean term raises the
risk-adjusted returns of almost all optimizers, and most for the variance, the semi-variance, and the mean absolute
deviation, which are unaffected by a shift of the portfolio return, whereas expected shortfall and second-order 
expected shortfall already reward a higher mean return implicitly. Once a mean term is added to the unregularized programs, the joint optimizers
achieve higher Sharpe ratios than the overlay optimizers for every risk measure in the two larger universes, since the
joint program can weigh the expected returns of the assets and the currencies against each other.

Third, we show that the joint program has an advantage over the overlay program in sample by construction, and we
measure how much of this advantage is realized out of sample. For the variance, the semi-variance, and the mean
absolute deviation, the in-sample advantage is small, and most of it is realized out of sample. For expected shortfall
and second-order expected shortfall, it is larger but mostly not realized. By varying the confidence level and by
de-meaning the two measures, we explain their in-sample advantage with two mechanisms, the fitting of the currency positions
to the specific worst days of the lookback window and an implicit risk-return tradeoff that rewards a high realized
mean, and neither of them generalizes out of sample.

Finally, we analyze how much the optimizers improve on the equally weighted portfolio and on two asset-only benchmarks,
which fully hedge or leave unhedged the currency exposure of their assets. All optimizers achieve a much lower
volatility and smaller drawdowns than the equally weighted portfolio. Without a mean term, they also earn lower excess
returns, but they outperform it on a risk-adjusted basis in the two larger universes, which offer more low-risk stocks
to choose from. Regularization improves the risk-adjusted returns,
most in the largest universe, since it limits the extreme positions that the programs fit to noise. The regularized
joint and overlay optimizers, which manage the currencies actively, realize a lower value of the risk measure they
minimize than the fully hedged benchmark at similar risk-adjusted returns, and they outperform the unhedged benchmark in
the two larger universes. 

Taken together, our results translate into practical advice for asset managers who invest internationally. For a
manager who uses a currency overlay, switching to joint optimization is beneficial in two situations. The first is
when the manager optimizes for both risk and return. In the two larger universes, the joint optimizers then achieve higher
Sharpe ratios than the overlay optimizers for every risk measure. The second is when the manager only minimizes risk but holds
few assets per currency, as in the smallest universe, where joint optimization lowers the risk of the portfolio at
similar risk-adjusted returns. For the choice of the risk measure, our results favor the variance, since the mean-variance joint optimizer performs
best overall. Whichever approach is chosen, regularization
raises the risk-adjusted returns, most in the largest universe. Managing the currencies actively 
lowers the risk relative to full hedging without giving up risk-adjusted returns, and in the 
two larger universes it also outperforms leaving
the currency exposure unhedged.


\section{Thesis Structure}

The remainder of the thesis is organized as follows. In \Cref{chap:literature-review} we review the literature on
currency risk management, on the joint optimization of assets and currencies, and on the risk measures and estimation
methods on which our programs build. In \Cref{chap:optimization-framework} we introduce the five risk measures, derive
the return of an international portfolio, and formulate the regularized and the mean-risk programs for sequential and
joint optimization. In \Cref{chap:data-and-implementation} we describe the dataset used and the implementation of the
backtests. In \Cref{chap:backtest-analysis} we analyze the out-of-sample performance of the joint and the overlay
optimizers and compare them with the benchmarks. In \Cref{chap:joint-vs-sequential} we compare how well the joint and
the overlay programs minimize their risk measure in sample and out of sample. In \Cref{chap:conclusion} we summarize our
findings and discuss the limitations of the analysis and directions for future research. Appendices~\ref{app:es_derivation},
\ref{app:mv_derivation}, and~\ref{app:mad_derivation} derive the optimization programs for the five risk measures considered in our analysis.
Appendix~\ref{app:demeaning} contains the results on coherent risk measures and deviation measures.
Appendices~\ref{app:optimization-implementation} and~\ref{app:transaction_costs} describe the numerical implementation of the optimizers
and the modeling of transaction costs, and Appendix~\ref{app:additional-results} reports additional results and findings not included in the main text.
