\chapter{Data and Implementation}\label{chap:data-and-implementation}

In this chapter, we describe the dataset used for the empirical evaluation of the portfolio optimization strategies and the implementation of the backtesting framework. 
In the data section we detail the construction of the investable universe. We follow \cite{RegularizedMultiCurrencyExpectedShortfallPortfolios} to analyze asset and excess currency returns and volatillities. We further expand the analysis to different investable universe sizes, and look at the correlation dependence of asset and currency returns. In the implementation section we describe the modular structure of the backtesting framework,
the workflow of the backtest, numerical considerations in the implementation, and the metrics used to evaluate potrfolio performance.

\section{Data} 

The empirical evaluation of all portfolio optimization strategies in this thesis relies on
a dataset of international equities, foreign exchange spot rates, and forward
exchange rates sourced from Refinitiv Datastream. The the one-month U.S.\ Treasury bill rate used in the return and
performance metric calculations is obtained from
Kenneth R.\ French's online data library.\footnote{\url{https://mba.tuck.dartmouth.edu/pages/faculty/ken.french/data_library.html}}
The datasets span from June 1990 to June 2023, covering several different market regimes, including
the dot-com crash, the 2008 Global Financial Crisis, the European sovereign debt crisis, and the
COVID-19 pandemic.

We construct the investable universe from 14 global equity markets: Australia,
Canada, Denmark, Norway, Sweden, Hong Kong, Japan, New Zealand, Singapore, South
Africa, Switzerland, the Eurozone, the United Kingdom, and the United States. The universe is
rebuilt at the beginning of each year from the largest stocks by market capitalization at the time (a rolling-window
approach that mitigates survivorship bias). At the start of each year we rank stocks within
each market and restrict the following year's investments to that set. To study how the
universe size affects the optimization approaches considered, we form three cross-sections, comprising the
top 10, top 50, and top 100 stocks per market. Because the constituents turn over each
year, each universe spans more distinct stocks over the full sample than are held on any
single date, 407 for the top 10, 1705 for the top 50, and 3189 for the top 100, respectively.

The US dollar serves as the base currency throughout our analysis. For each stock we compute fully hedged
returns, and for each currency the excess currency return, following the model-free return
representation in \Cref{chap:optimization-framework}.

\Cref{tab:summary-statistics} reports annualized average returns and volatilities for the
fully hedged equity returns and excess currency returns, by economy. For each economy, the
asset figures are the cross-sectional average of the daily returns of its individual
stocks. 

We can see that as the investable universe becomes larger, the average returns tend to fall. In the top 10 universe every economy has a positive average return, ranging from
$2.76\%$ (the United Kingdom) to $12.59\%$ (the United States). In the top 50 universe, the range
drops downward, from $-1.99\%$ (Singapore) to $7.99\%$ (the United States), and in the
top 100 universe the range goes from $-4.84\%$ (Singapore) to $6.86\%$ (the United States), with many
economies now having negative average returns. The
trend reflects the firm size, the largest stocks post the strongest returns, and the smaller
firms added pull the average down.
Volatilities exhibit the opposite pattern, rising with universe size, as smaller, more
volatile stocks join the investable universe. Switzerland is the least volatile market in every case, 
its volatility rises from $29.04\%$ to $33.65\%$, and the upper end rises 
from $43.30\%$ (Norway) in the top 10 to $62.12\%$ (Singapore) in the top 100.

Excess currency returns range between $-2.07\%$ for the Japanese yen and $2.48\%$ for the New
Zealand dollar, and excess currency volatilities range from $0.56\%$ for the Hong Kong
dollar, whose peg to the US dollar keeps it stable, up to $15.18\%$ for the
South African rand.

To quantify the dependence between asset and currency returns, we compute Pearson
correlations between economy-level fully hedged equity returns and excess currency returns.
For each economy we form an equally weighted daily portfolio of the fully hedged returns of
its constituent stocks, resetting the constituents at each annual universe reconstitution. 
Correlating each economy's series
with each currency's excess return over the full sample gives a $14 \times 13$ correlation matrix. The
US dollar is dropped from the currency axis, since its excess return is zero by
construction. We repeat this for the three universe sizes.
The resulting heatmaps are shown in \Cref{fig:asset-currency-corr-top-10}, \Cref{fig:asset-currency-corr-top-50}, and \Cref{fig:asset-currency-corr-top-100}.

Across all three universes the correlations range from $-0.2$ to
$0.4$, and remain mostly positive. The pattern is intuitive and confirms well known empirical findings. The strongest positive correlations belong to commodity and risk-sensitive currencies such as the AUD, CAD, NZD and ZAR, whose equity markets and
currencies rise together in good times and fall together in bad \citep{CHEN2003133, common_risk_factors_currency_markets, carry_trades_and_global_foreign_exchange_volatility}. The JPY and CHF are the only negatively correlated currencies with most other economies (ranging from $-0.2$ to $0.03$), in line with their role as safe havens that strengthen when equities fall \cite{safe_haven_currencies}. The EUR, DKK, and HKD are minimally correlated, reflecting the HKD's peg to the US dollar, and the DKK's peg to the EUR. This split between risky and safe-haven currencies is well
documented \citep{global_currency_hedging, safe_haven_currencies}.

This is the dependence structure the overlay optimization approach discards. It sets asset weights first, using only equity information, and adds the currency hedge afterwards, whereas the joint model can act on it
directly. The structure barely changes across universe sizes, and this matters for the
results that follow. A shift
in the relative performance of the joint and overlay approaches in \Cref{chap:backtest-analysis} should therefore
be attributed to estimation error rather than a change in the underlying dependence structure.

\section{Backtesting Framework} \label{sec:backtesting-framework}

In \Cref{chap:backtest-analysis} we evaluate a range of optimization strategies under different objectives (Mean-Variance,  Expected Shortfall, Mean Absolute Deviation), their treatment of
currency exposure (overlay or joint), and the size of the investable
universe. To keep these comparisons fair, the
backtesting framework separates between the
orchestration of the backtest and the optimization logic it evaluates.\footnote{The full codebase with the backtest implementation is publicly available on GitHub: \url{https://github.com/anejrozman/Multi-Currency-Portfolio-Optimization-with-General-Risk-Measures}.}  
The main backtesting loop that ingests data, rolls the estimation window forward, selects
hyperparameters, and records performance is fixed, while the optimizer that
produces portfolio weights is a swappable component. Any optimizer that conforms
to a common interface is therefore evaluated under the same conditions.
The framework is composed of three main components, a data handler, the backtester, and a
family of optimizers under a common abstract base class, whose
interaction is summarized in \Cref{fig:backtest}.

Data ingestion and alignment are implemetned in the \texttt{DataHandler}.
It reads the raw inputs, the local unhedged asset returns $R^u$, the currency returns
$R^c$, the forward premia $f_t$, and the universe mask that identifies which
assets constitute the investable universe at each date, and aligns them into a
\texttt{data\_bundle} which is passed to the backtester. The optimizers operate only on the
bundled series, and the construction of returns from
these inputs follows \Cref{sec:returns}.

All implemented optimizers inherit from an abstract base class, \texttt{BaseOptimizer}, that
defines the three-method interface through which the backtester runs them. The
\texttt{fit} method receives a training data bundle and estimates and stores the
quantities the optimizer requires for its objective (e.g. covariance matrix for the mean-variance optimizer), so that estimation is
performed entirely within each optimizer.
The \texttt{optimize} method solves the specific optimization problem. The \texttt{score} method takes a set of weights and a
validation bundle and returns the validation performance used for hyperparameter
selection. Concrete subclasses implement this interface for each strategy, an
equal-weight optimizer that serves as the naive benchmark, the mean-variance and
Expected Shortfall optimizers, and a general convex optimizer TODO: Update this when done implementing generalization.
The estimators and solver internals invoked inside the methods \texttt{fit} and
\texttt{optimize} are described in \Cref{sec:numerical-implementation}.

The backtest itself is implemented in the \texttt{Backtester.run} method, which
advances through the data sample on a set rebalancing frequency (daily, weekly, monthly, quarterly, or annual). The
empirical analysis in \Cref{chap:backtest-analysis} and Appendix \ref{app:additional-results} uses monthly and quarterly rebalancing on the daily data. At each
rebalancing date, the optimizer is calibrated and deployed using a fixed rolling lookback window, partitioned into  an estimation window and a validation window. Hyperparameters are selected by a grid search over
this partition. Each configuration is fitted on the estimation window
via \texttt{fit}, used to produce weights via \texttt{optimize}, and evaluated on
the validation window via \texttt{score}, and the configuration with the
best validation score is retrained on both the estimation and validation windows and finally used to 
produce the portfolio weights for the next out-of-sample period. 
These weights are held until the next rebalancing date, the window then rolls forward and the procedure
repeats. Because we keep the estimation, validation, and total lookback window lengths
fixed throughout the backtest (10 years split into 9 for estimation and 1 for validation) and across universe sizes, every strategy is evaluated on the same out-of-sample dataset.

Each invocation of the backtester for a given optimizer produces a self-contained
run directory. A new \texttt{run\_<n>} folder is created under
\texttt{run/<optimizer\_name>/} and collects the full record of that run. The
out-of-sample return series \texttt{oos\_returns.csv}, the performance
metrics \texttt{metrics.json}, a cumulative-return plot
\texttt{cum\_returns\_plot.png}, and a configuration file \texttt{config.json}
recording the run specification. Storing the configuration alongside its outputs
makes each result reproducible and attributable to the settings
that generated it.

\section{Performance Evaluation Metrics}
\label{sec:performance-evaluation-metrics}

All portfolio optimization strategies are evaluated on a common set of performance metrics computed from
their out-of-sample return series. Because each optimizer minimizes a different
objective, the strategies cannot be ranked solely by their in-sample objective values
and are instead judged on the same realized metrics, each capturing a different view of the performance. All metrics are computed on USD out-of-sample returns net of the
transaction costs (Derivation detailed in Appendix \ref{app:transaction_costs}). 
Throughout this section, $R_{\mathcal P,t}$ denotes
the realized daily out-of-sample portfolio return, $\bar R_{\mathcal P}$ its
sample mean, $\sigma$ its sample standard deviation, and $R_f$ the risk-free
rate. Daily figures are annualized using the standard $252$ trading days per year assumption. The reported metrics are
the expected shortfall at the $80\%$ and $95\%$ confidence levels, annualized volatility,
maximum drawdown, the Sharpe ratio, the Sortino ratio, the expected shortfall
ratio, and asset and currency turnover.

Expected shortfall at confidence level $\beta$ measures the average loss in the
worst $(1-\beta)$ tail of the return distribution. It is preferred to value at
risk because it is coherent \cite{CoherentMeasuresOfRisk} and, rather than
reporting only the loss threshold, averages the losses beyond it, so it reflects
how severe the tail outcomes are and not merely how often they occur.\footnote{Formal
definitions of value at risk and expected shortfall at confidence level $\beta$ are
given in Appendix \ref{app:es_auxiliary_formulation}.} For the expected shortfall
strategies, it is the quantity the optimizer minimizes,
which makes it the most direct out-of-sample test of whether the expected shortfall
strategy delivers on its objective. Because every strategy, including the
mean--variance and naive benchmarks, is evaluated on the same measure, it also
reveals whether explicitly targeting expected shortfall minimization produces lower
realized tail risk than controlling variance alone. We evaluate it at
$\beta = 0.80$ (the same confidence level used in the optimizer) and $\beta = 0.95$,
which captures a more extreme region of the loss distribution. Reporting both levels
separates a strategy's realized tail risk at the quantile it was optimized for from
its tail risk at a deeper quantile it was not, showing whether (how much) the downside
protection generalizes beyond the optimization level. 

Annualized volatility is the standard deviation of portfolio returns. It is the classical measure of risk in the portfolio optimization literature, but does have some limitations. It is symmetric, meaning it penalizes
favorable and adverse deviations equally, which is the limitation that
motivates the downside and tail-risk measures reported alongside it. For the
mean--variance strategies it plays the role that expected shortfall plays for the
expected shortfall strategies. It is the objective the mean--variance optimizers minimize, and so it is the most direct out-of-sample test of whether they deliver on their objective.
Because a portfolio can
achieve low volatility while still suffering severe cumulative losses over time, it is informative
only when interpretedtogether with the maximum drawdown and other tail-risk measures.

Maximum drawdown is the largest decline of the cumulative return
path over the out-of-sample period. Unlike expected shortfall and volatility,
which summarize the one-period return distribution, drawdown is a property of the
realized wealth path, capturing the cumulative and sequential experience of losses
that an investor goes through.\ Such path-dependent losses matter
in practice.\ For example, deep drawdowns can breach margin requirements. 
Importantly, none of the optimizers considered in the thesis targets drawdown
directly, so it serves as an out-of-sample robustness check on a metric the
strategies were not built to control. Letting $V_t = \prod_{s \le t}(1 + R_{\mathcal P,s})$ denote
the cumulative wealth of portfolio $\mathcal{P}$, with $V_0 = 1$, and $P_t = \max_{s \le t} V_s$ its running
maximum, the maximum drawdown is defined as
\begin{equation*}
\mathrm{MD} = \max_{t} \frac{P_t - V_t}{P_t}.
\end{equation*}

The Sharpe ratio is the annualized ratio of the average excess portfolio return relative to its total
volatility. It is the standard measure of return per unit of total risk. The Sharpe ratio is given by
\begin{equation*}
\mathrm{SR} = \frac{\bar{R}_{\mathcal P} - R_f}{\sigma}\sqrt{252}.
\end{equation*}

The Sortino ratio replaces volatility in the Sharpe ratio with downside volatility, so that a
strategy is not penalized for upside dispersion. By measuring volatility only
over adverse outcomes it addresses the main downside of the Sharpe
ratio. Read against the Sharpe ratio, it indicates whether a strategy's risk-adjusted performance stems from downside protection or from
lower overall variability. It is the annualized ratio of average excess return to
the standard deviation of the negative returns $\sigma_{\text{down}}$, given by
\begin{equation*}
\mathrm{SOR} = \frac{\bar{R}_{\mathcal P} - R_f}{\sigma_{\text{down}}}\sqrt{252}.
\end{equation*}

The expected shortfall ratio is the tail-risk analogue of the Sharpe ratio,
reporting average return per unit of downside risk \citep{RegularizedMultiCurrencyExpectedShortfallPortfolios}.
Where the Sharpe ratio scales return by total volatility and the Sortino ratio by
downside deviation, the expected shortfall ratio scales it by the tail loss
itself.
A strategy scores well only if it earns return efficiently relative to
its realized tail risk, so the ratio rewards the trade-off the expected shortfall optimizers
are solving. Reported in annualized units alongside the Sharpe and Sortino ratios,
the three can be read together to trace how the ranking of strategies shifts as the
risk moves from total, to downside, to tail risk. It is computed from the
annualized average return and the annualized expected shortfall
\begin{equation*}
\mathrm{ESR} = \frac{\bar{R}_{\mathcal P}}{\mathrm{ES}_{\beta}}.
\end{equation*}

We use the expected shortfall ratio at the $\beta = 0.80$ confidencelevel.

Finally, turnover proxies the trading activity, and the implementation
costs, of each strategy. Although the reported returns are already net of
proportional transaction costs, turnover remains informative because it
measures how much the portfolio must trade to maintain its positions, its capacity to scale, and its exposure to frictions such as slippage and market impact that our model does not capture. A strategy whose performance depends on heavy rebalancing can be hard to implement, so low and stable turnover is a desirable property. Turnover is reported separately for the asset and currency legs,
since the two are traded through different instruments with different cost levels.
Asset turnover is the average rebalancing volume over the out-of-sample period
\begin{equation*}
\mathrm{Turnover}^{a} = \frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sum_{i=1}^{N}
\bigl| x_{i,t} - x_{i,t}^{-} \bigr|,
\end{equation*}
where $x_{i,t}^{-}$ is the asset weight just before rebalancing at $t$ and
$x_{i,t}$ the weight set at the rebalance, and $T$ is the number of out-of-sample
rebalancing dates. Currency turnover is the average absolute notional of the
forward positions
\begin{equation*}
\mathrm{Turnover}^{c} = \frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sum_{c=1}^{M}
\bigl| \upphi_{c,t} \bigr|.
\end{equation*}
