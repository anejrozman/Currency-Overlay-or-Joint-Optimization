\chapter{Data and Implementation}\label{chap:data-and-implementation}

In this chapter, we describe the dataset used for the empirical evaluation of the portfolio optimization strategies and the implementation of the backtesting framework. 
In the data section we detail the construction of the investable universe. We follow \cite{RegularizedMultiCurrencyExpectedShortfallPortfolios} to analyze asset and currency returns and volatillities and expand the analysis to different investable universe sizes, and look at the correlation dependence of asset and currency returns. In the implementation section we describe the modular structure of the backtesting framework, the workflow of the backtest, and the metrics used to evaluate potrfolio performance.

\section{Data} 

The empirical evaluation of all portfolio optimization strategies in this thesis relies on
a dataset of international equities, foreign exchange spot rates, and forward
exchange rates sourced from Refinitiv Datastream. The the one-month U.S.\ Treasury bill rate used in the return and
performance metric calculations is obtained from
Kenneth R.\ French's online data library.\footnote{\url{https://mba.tuck.dartmouth.edu/pages/faculty/ken.french/data_library.html}}
The datasets span from June 1990 to June 2023, covering several different market regimes, including
the dot-com crash, the 2008 Global Financial Crisis, the European sovereign debt crisis, and the
COVID-19 pandemic.

We construct the investable universe from 14 global equity markets: Australia,
Canada, Denmark, Norway, Sweden, Hong Kong, Japan, New Zealand, Singapore, South
Africa, Switzerland, the Eurozone, the United Kingdom, and the United States. The universe is
rebuilt at the beginning of each year from the largest stocks by market capitalization at the time (a rolling-window
approach that mitigates survivorship bias). At the start of each year we rank stocks within
each market and restrict the following year's investments to that set. To study how the
universe size affects the optimization approaches, we form three cross-sections, comprising the
top 10, top 50, and top 100 stocks per market. Because the constituents turn over each
year, each universe spans more distinct stocks over the full sample than are held on any
single date, 407 for the top 10, 1705 for the top 50, and 3189 for the top 100, respectively.

The US dollar is the base currency throughout our analysis. For each stock we compute fully hedged
returns, and for each currency the excess currency return, following the model-free return
representation in \Cref{chap:optimization-framework}.

\Cref{tab:summary-statistics} reports annualized average returns and volatilities for these
fully hedged equity returns and excess currency returns, by economy. For each economy, the
asset figures are the cross-sectional average of the daily returns of its individual
stocks.

We can see that as the investable universe becomes larger, the average returns tend to fall. In the top 10 universe every economy has a positive average return, ranging from
$2.76\%$ (the United Kingdom) to $12.59\%$ (the United States). In the top 50 universe, the range
drops downward, from $-1.99\%$ (Singapore) to $7.99\%$ (the United States), and in the
top 100 universe the range goes from $-4.84\%$ (Singapore) to $6.86\%$ (the United States), with many
economies now having negative average returns. The
trend reflects the firm size, the largest stocks post the strongest returns, and the smaller
firms added pull the average down.
Volatilities exhibit the opposite pattern, rising with universe size, as smaller, more
volatile stocks join the investable universe. Switzerland is the least volatile market in every case, 
its volatility rises from $29.04\%$ to $33.65\%$, and the upper end rises 
from $43.30\%$ (Norway) in the top 10 to $62.12\%$ (Singapore) in the top 100.

Excess currency returns range between $-2.07\%$ for the Japanese yen and $2.48\%$ for the New
Zealand dollar, and excess currency volatilities range from $0.56\%$ for the Hong Kong
dollar, whose peg to the US dollar keeps it stable, up to $15.18\%$ for the
South African rand.

To quantify the dependence between asset and currency returns, we compute Pearson
correlations between economy-level fully hedged equity returns and excess currency returns.
For each economy we form an equally weighted daily portfolio of the fully hedged returns of
its constituent stocks, resetting the constituents at each annual universe reconstitution. 
Correlating each economy's series
with each currency's excess return over the full sample gives a $14 \times 13$ correlation matrix. The
US dollar is dropped from the currency axis, since its excess return is zero by
construction. We repeat this for the three universe sizes.
The resulting heatmaps are shown in \Cref{fig:asset-currency-corr}.

Across all three universes the correlations range from $-0.2$ to
$0.4$, and are mostly positive. The pattern is intuitive and confirms well known empirical findings. The strongest positive correlations belong to commodity and risk-sensitive currencies such as the AUD, CAD, NZD and ZAR, whose equity markets and
currencies rise together in good times and fall together in bad \citep{CHEN2003133, common_risk_factors_currency_markets, carry_trades_and_global_foreign_exchange_volatility}. The JPY and CHF are the only negatively correlated currencies with most other economies (ranging from $-0.2$ to $-0.05$), in line with their role as safe havens that strengthen when equities fall \cite{safe_haven_currencies}. The EUR, DKK, and HKD are minimally correlated, reflecting the HKD's peg to the US dollar, the DKK's peg to the EUR. This split between risky and safe-haven currencies is well
documented \citep{global_currency_hedging, safe_haven_currencies}.

This is the dependence structure the overlay optimization approach discards. It sets asset weights first, using only equity information, and adds the currency hedge afterwards, whereas the joint model can act on it
directly. The structure barely changes across universe sizes. This matters for the
results that follow, a shift
in the relative performance of the joint and overlay approaches in \Cref{chap:empirical-analysis-and-baseline-results} should therefore
be attributed to estimation error rather than a change in the underlying dependence structure.

\section{Backtesting Framework} \label{sec:backtesting-framework}

In \Cref{chap:empirical-analysis-and-baseline-results} we evaluate a range of optimization strategies under different objectives (mean-variance or Expected Shortfall), their treatment of
currency exposure (overlay or joint), and the size of the investable
universe. To keep these comparisons fair, the
backtesting framework separates between the
orchestration of the backtest and the optimization logic it evaluates.\footnote{The full codebase with the backtest implementation is publicly available on GitHub: \url{https://github.com/anejrozman/Multi-Currency-Portfolio-Optimization-with-General-Risk-Measures}.}  
The main backtesting loop that ingests data, rolls the estimation window forward, selects
hyperparameters, and records performance is fixed, while the optimizer that
produces portfolio weights is a swappable component. Any optimizer that conforms
to a common interface is therefore evaluated under identical conditions.
The framework is composed of three main components, a data handler, the backtester, and a
family of optimizers under a common abstract base class, whose
interaction is summarized in \Cref{fig:backtest}.

Data ingestion and alignment are implemetned in the \texttt{DataHandler}.
It reads the raw inputs, the local unhedged asset returns $R^u$, the currency returns
$R^c$, the forward premia $f_t$, and the universe mask that identifies which
assets constitute the investable universe at each date, and aligns them into a
\texttt{data\_bundle} which is passed to the backtester. The optimizers operate only on the
bundled series, and the construction of returns from
these inputs follows \Cref{sec:returns}.

All implemented optimizers inherit from an abstract base class, \texttt{BaseOptimizer}, that
defines the three-method interface through which the backtester drives them. The
\texttt{fit} method receives a training data bundle and estimates and stores the
quantities the optimizer requires for its objective (e.g. covariance matrix for the mean-variance optimizer), so that estimation is
performed entirely within each optimizer.
The \texttt{optimize} method solves the specific optimization problem. The \texttt{score} method takes a set of weights and a
validation bundle and returns the validation performance used for hyperparameter
selection. Concrete subclasses implement this interface for each strategy, an
equal-weight optimizer that serves as the naive benchmark, the mean-variance and
Expected Shortfall optimizers, and a general convex optimizer TODO: Update this when done implementing generalization.
The estimators and solver internals invoked inside \texttt{fit} and
\texttt{optimize} are described in \Cref{sec:numerical-implementation}.

The backtest itself is implemented in the \texttt{Backtester.run} method, which
advances through the data sample on a set rebalancing frequency (daily, weekly, monthly, quarterly, or annual). The
empirical analysis in \Cref{chap:empirical-analysis-and-baseline-results} and Appendix \ref{app:additional-results} uses monthly and quarterly rebalancing on the daily data. At each
rebalancing date, the optimizer is calibrated and deployed using a fixed rolling lookback window, partitioned into  an estimation window and a validation window. Hyperparameters are selected by a grid search over
this partition. Each configuration is fitted on the estimation window
via \texttt{fit}, used to produce weights via \texttt{optimize}, and evaluated on
the validation window via \texttt{score}, and the configuration with the
best validation score is retained on both the estimation and validation windows and finally used to 
produce the portfolio weights for the next out-of-sample period. 
These weights are held until the next rebalancing date, and the
portfolio's realized return over the holding period is recorded as a single
out-of-sample observation. The window then rolls forward and the procedure
repeats. Because we keep the estimation, validation, and total lookback window lengths
fixed throughout the backtest (10 years split into 9 for estimation and 1 for validation) and across universe sizes, every strategy is evaluated on the same out-of-sample dataset.

Each invocation of the backtester for a given optimizer produces a self-contained
run directory. A new \texttt{run\_<n>} folder is created under
\texttt{run/<optimizer\_name>/} and collects the full record of that run. The
out-of-sample return series \texttt{oos\_returns.csv}, the performance
metrics \texttt{metrics.json}, a cumulative-return plot
\texttt{cum\_returns\_plot.png}, and a configuration file \texttt{config.json}
recording the run specification. Storing the configuration alongside its outputs
makes each result reproducible and attributable to the settings
that generated it.

\section{Numerical Implementation} \label{sec:numerical-implementation}

The optimization problems derived in \Cref{chap:optimization-framework} are
not assembled in their explicit matrix form in code. Each problem is instead
expressed directly in the high-level modeling syntax of \texttt{CVXPY} and
canonicalized automatically into the standard form passed to the solver; the
matrix derivations in the appendices serve to make the structure of each program
transparent rather than to describe the implementation literally. In particular,
the $\ell_1$ penalty is written with CVXPY's \texttt{norm1} atom, so the
positive--negative variable split used in the derivation is internal to the
canonicalization and never appears explicitly in the code. The resulting problem
class depends only on the regularization in force: the Expected Shortfall
optimizer is a linear program when it is left unregularized, since both the
Rockafellar--Uryasev objective and its constraints are linear, whereas the
$\ell_2$ ridge penalty introduces a quadratic term that turns every regularized
Expected Shortfall problem into a quadratic program. The mean--variance problems
are quadratic programs by construction, through the variance term in their
objective.

All programs are solved with Gurobi, a state-of-the-art solver for linear and
quadratic programs, accessed through CVXPY. The solver is configured to use the
barrier (interior-point) method with tight convergence and accuracy tolerances,
so that the large number of problems solved across rebalancing dates, universes,
and hyperparameter configurations return precise and reproducible optima. The
exact configuration is:

\begin{lstlisting}[language=Python]
problem.solve(
    solver=cp.GUROBI,
    verbose=False,
    BarConvTol=1e-8,
    Method=2,
    ScaleFlag=0,
    AggFill=0,
    PreDual=1,
    Presolve=1,
    Threads=3,
    FeasibilityTol=1e-9,
    OptimalityTol=1e-9,
)
\end{lstlisting}

The barrier method (\texttt{Method=2}) is paired with a barrier convergence
tolerance of $10^{-8}$ and feasibility and optimality tolerances of $10^{-9}$;
the remaining flags pin down the presolve, scaling, and threading behavior so that
solver results remain reproducible across the many runs.

What each optimizer estimates before solving depends on its objective. The
mean--variance optimizers require the first two moments of returns over the
estimation window: the mean vector is estimated with Bayes--Stein shrinkage
\citep{jorion1986}, which pulls the noisy sample means toward a common grand mean,
and the return covariance is estimated with the Ledoit--Wolf shrinkage estimator
\citep{ledoitwolf2004}, which shrinks the sample covariance toward a structured
target and is therefore far better conditioned than the raw sample estimate. The
shrunk mean enters the explicit return-maximization term of the mean--variance
objective, and the shrunk covariance enters the variance term. The Expected
Shortfall optimizers, by contrast, require no moment estimation: the
sample-average approximation of \Cref{app:es-derivation} uses the empirical return
observations in the estimation window directly as the scenario set over which tail
risk is evaluated, so the historical returns themselves are the only input.

A specific instability arises in the joint mean--variance optimizer. Because its
objective rewards expected return while penalizing variance, and the currency
covariance can remain close to singular even after shrinkage, the optimizer is
prone to taking unboundedly large net currency positions: it loads onto currency
directions that combine a favorable estimated mean with a near-zero estimated
variance, where the risk term fails to restrain it. Two elements of the
formulation together render the problem well-posed. First, the
net-currency-exposure box constraint introduced in \Cref{sec:regularization} bounds
each net currency position component-wise, so the feasible set is compact and no
exposure can diverge. Second, the $\ell_2$ ridge penalty conditions the currency
covariance, removing the near-singular directions the optimizer would otherwise
exploit and ensuring the quadratic objective is strictly convex. With a strictly
convex objective over a compact feasible region the optimum is unique and finite,
and the runaway positions no longer occur.

Hyperparameter selection follows the walk-forward grid search described in
\Cref{sec:backtest}. The tuned hyperparameters are the four elastic-net
penalties---the $\ell_1$ and $\ell_2$ coefficients applied separately to the asset
and currency blocks, $\lambda_1^a$, $\lambda_2^a$, $\lambda_1^c$, and
$\lambda_2^c$---and the grid is the full Cartesian product of their individual
ranges. Each candidate configuration is scored on its own terms: the weights it
produces are evaluated by computing, on the validation window, the same objective
the optimizer minimizes, so that each strategy is tuned against the criterion it
is designed to optimize rather than against a common external metric. The
structural and modeling parameters that define the problem itself---the
risk-aversion coefficient, the leverage budget, and the net-currency-exposure box
bounds---are fixed a priori rather than tuned. The penalty grids are reported in
\Cref{tab:hypergrid}; the full numerical specification, including the per-universe
selected values, is collected in \Cref{app:hyperparams}.

\section{Performance Evaluation Metrics}
\label{sec:performance-evaluation-metrics}

All strategies are evaluated on a common set of performance metrics computed from
their out-of-sample return series. Evaluating performance out of sample is
essential here: in sample, the joint optimizer can only weakly improve on the
overlay, since it optimizes over the full asset-and-currency space rather than
the overlay's sequential decomposition, so a meaningful comparison must use data
not seen during estimation. Because each optimizer minimizes a different
objective, the strategies cannot be ranked by their in-sample objective values
and are instead judged on the same realized metrics, each capturing a distinct
facet of risk and reward. All metrics are computed on USD returns net of the
transaction costs detailed in \Cref{app:transaction-costs}, so that reported
performance reflects implementation cost. Throughout, $R_{\mathcal P,t}$ denotes
the realized daily out-of-sample portfolio return, $\bar R_{\mathcal P}$ its
sample mean, $\sigma$ its sample standard deviation, and $R_f$ the risk-free
rate; daily figures are annualized using $252$ trading days per year (means
scaled by $252$, standard deviations by $\sqrt{252}$). The reported metrics are
the expected shortfall at the $80\%$ confidence level, annualized volatility,
maximum drawdown, the Sharpe ratio, the Sortino ratio, the expected shortfall
ratio, and asset and currency turnover.

Expected shortfall at confidence level $\beta$ measures the average loss in the
worst $(1-\beta)$ tail of the return distribution, and is the realized
counterpart of the very objective the ES strategies minimize, so it reports tail
risk on the same footing as the optimization criterion. We first compute the
value at risk $\mathrm{VaR}_\beta$ as the $(1-\beta)$-quantile of the
out-of-sample returns; the expected shortfall is then the negative of the average
of all returns at or below this threshold, reported as a positive loss magnitude.
We evaluate it at $\beta = 0.80$, the same confidence level used in the optimizer,
and report the daily figure.\footnote{Formal definitions of value at risk and
expected shortfall at confidence level $\beta$ are given in Appendix
\ref{app:es_auxiliary_formulation}.}

Annualized volatility is the standard deviation of daily returns scaled by
$\sqrt{252}$. It is the classical dispersion measure of returns and the quantity
the mean--variance strategies control directly, making it the natural
common-scale risk figure across objectives.

Maximum drawdown is the largest peak-to-trough decline of the cumulative wealth
curve over the out-of-sample period, capturing the worst cumulative loss an
investor would have sustained---a path-dependent risk that the distributional
measures do not reflect. Letting $V_t = \prod_{s \le t}(1 + R_{\mathcal P,s})$
denote the cumulative wealth, with $V_0 = 1$, and $P_t = \max_{s \le t} V_s$ its
running peak, the maximum drawdown is
\begin{equation*}
\mathrm{MD} = \max_{t} \frac{P_t - V_t}{P_t}.
\end{equation*}

The Sharpe ratio is the annualized ratio of average excess return to total
volatility, the standard measure of risk-adjusted return used to rank strategies
on a common scale,
\begin{equation*}
\mathrm{SR} = \frac{\bar{R}_{\mathcal P} - R_f}{\sigma}\sqrt{252}.
\end{equation*}

The Sortino ratio replaces total volatility with downside volatility, so that
strategies are not penalized for upside dispersion---appropriate for
downside-risk-focused portfolios. It is the annualized ratio of average excess
return to the standard deviation of the negative returns $\sigma_{\text{down}}$,
\begin{equation*}
\mathrm{SOR} = \frac{\bar{R}_{\mathcal P} - R_f}{\sigma_{\text{down}}}\sqrt{252}.
\end{equation*}

The expected shortfall ratio is the tail-risk analogue of the Sharpe ratio,
reporting average return per unit of downside risk \citep{lucescu_ulrych_2026}.
It is computed from the annualized average return and the annualized expected
shortfall, which places it on the same comparable footing as the Sharpe and
Sortino ratios,
\begin{equation*}
\mathrm{ESR} = \frac{\bar{R}^{\,\text{ann}}_{\mathcal P}}{\mathrm{ES}^{\,\text{ann}}_{\beta}}.
\end{equation*}

Finally, turnover proxies the trading activity---and hence the implementation
cost---of each strategy, and indicates whether performance is sustained by
excessive rebalancing. It is reported separately for the asset and currency legs,
since the two are traded through different instruments. Asset turnover is the
average rebalancing volume over the out-of-sample period
\citep{demiguel2009generalized, kondor2015contour},
\begin{equation*}
\mathrm{Turnover}^{a} = \frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sum_{i=1}^{N}
\bigl| x_{i,t} - x_{i,t}^{-} \bigr|,
\end{equation*}
where $x_{i,t}^{-}$ is the asset weight just before rebalancing at $t$ and
$x_{i,t}$ the weight set at the rebalance, and $T$ is the number of out-of-sample
rebalancing dates. Currency turnover is the average absolute notional of the
forward positions \citep{burkhardt_ulrych_2023, polak_ulrych_2024},
\begin{equation*}
\mathrm{Turnover}^{c} = \frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sum_{c=1}^{M}
\bigl| \upphi_{c,t} \bigr|,
\end{equation*}
which, unlike the asset leg, is measured on the level of the forward positions
because the one-period forward contracts are entered afresh at each rebalancing
date.
