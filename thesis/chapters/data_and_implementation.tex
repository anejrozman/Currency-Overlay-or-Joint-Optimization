\chapter{Data and Implementation}\label{chap:data-and-implementation}

In this chapter, we describe the dataset used for the empirical evaluation of the portfolio optimization strategies and the implementation of the backtesting framework. 
In the data section we detail the construction of the investable universe. We follow \cite{RegularizedMultiCurrencyExpectedShortfallPortfolios} to analyze asset and currency returns and volatillities and expand the analysis to different investable universe sizes, and look at the correlation dependence of asset and currency returns. In the implementation section we describe the modular structure of the backtesting framework, the workflow of the backtest, and the metrics used to evaluate potrfolio performance.

\section{Data} 

The empirical evaluation of all portfolio optimization strategies in this thesis relies on
a comprehensive dataset of international equities, foreign exchange spot rates, and forward
exchange rates sourced from Refinitiv Datastream. The dataset spans from June 1990 to June 2023,
covering several different market regimes, including the dot-com crash, the 2008 Global Financial
Crisis, the European sovereign debt crisis, and the COVID-19 pandemic.

We construct the investable universe from 14 global equity markets: Australia,
Canada, Denmark, Norway, Sweden, Hong Kong, Japan, New Zealand, Singapore, South
Africa, Switzerland, the Eurozone, the United Kingdom, and the United States. The universe is
rebuilt at the beginning of each year from the largest stocks by market capitalization at the time (a rolling-window
approach that mitigates survivorship bias). At the start of each year we rank stocks within
each market and restrict the following year's investments to that set. To study how the
universe size affects the optimization approaches, we form three cross-sections, comprising the
top 10, top 50, and top 100 stocks per market. Because the constituents turn over each
year, each universe spans more distinct stocks over the full sample than are held on any
single date, 407 for the top 10, 1705 for the top 50, and 3189 for the top 100, respectively.

The US dollar is the base currency throughout our analysis. For each stock we compute fully hedged
returns, and for each currency the excess currency return, following the model-free return
representation in \Cref{chap:optimization-framework}.

\Cref{tab:summary-statistics} reports annualized average returns and volatilities for these
fully hedged equity returns and excess currency returns, by economy. For each economy, the
asset figures are the cross-sectional average of the daily returns of its individual
stocks.

We can see that as the investable universe becomes larger, the average returns tend to fall. In the top 10 universe every economy has a positive average return, ranging from
$2.76\%$ (the United Kingdom) to $12.59\%$ (the United States). In the top 50 universe, the range
drops downward, from $-1.99\%$ (Singapore) to $7.99\%$ (the United States), and in the
top 100 universe the range goes from $-4.84\%$ (Singapore) to $6.86\%$ (the United States), with many
economies now having negative average returns. The
trend reflects the firm size, the largest stocks post the strongest returns, and the smaller
firms added pull the average down.
Volatilities exhibit the opposite pattern, rising with universe size, as smaller, more
volatile stocks join the investable universe. Switzerland is the least volatile market in every case, 
its volatility rises from $29.04\%$ to $33.65\%$, and the upper end rises 
from $43.30\%$ (Norway) in the top 10 to $62.12\%$ (Singapore) in the top 100.

Excess currency returns range between $-2.07\%$ for the Japanese yen and $2.48\%$ for the New
Zealand dollar, and excess currency volatilities range from $0.56\%$ for the Hong Kong
dollar, whose peg to the US dollar keeps it stable, up to $15.18\%$ for the
South African rand.

To quantify the dependence between asset and currency returns, we compute Pearson
correlations between economy-level fully hedged equity returns and excess currency returns.
For each economy we form an equally weighted daily portfolio of the fully hedged returns of
its constituent stocks, resetting the constituents at each annual universe reconstitution. 
Correlating each economy's series
with each currency's excess return over the full sample gives a $14 \times 13$ correlation matrix. The
US dollar is dropped from the currency axis, since its excess return is zero by
construction. We repeat this for the three universe sizes.
The resulting heatmaps are shown in \Cref{fig:asset-currency-corr}.

Across all three universes the correlations range from $-0.2$ to
$0.4$, and are mostly positive. The pattern is intuitive and confirms well known empirical findings. The strongest positive correlations belong to commodity and risk-sensitive currencies such as the AUD, CAD, NZD and ZAR, whose equity markets and
currencies rise together in good times and fall together in bad \citep{CHEN2003133, common_risk_factors_currency_markets, carry_trades_and_global_foreign_exchange_volatility}. The JPY and CHF are the only negatively correlated currencies with most other economies (ranging from $-0.2$ to $-0.05$), in line with their role as safe havens that strengthen when equities fall \cite{safe_haven_currencies}. The EUR, DKK, and HKD are minimally correlated, reflecting the HKD's peg to the US dollar, the DKK's peg to the EUR. This split between risky and safe-haven currencies is well
documented \citep{global_currency_hedging, safe_haven_currencies}.

This is the dependence structure the overlay optimization approach discards. It sets asset weights first, using only equity information, and adds the currency hedge afterwards, whereas the joint model can act on it
directly. The structure barely changes across universe sizes. This matters for the
results that follow, a shift
in the relative performance of the joint and overlay approaches in \Cref{chap:empirical-analysis-and-baseline-results} should therefore
be attributed to estimation error rather than a change in the underlying dependence structure.

\section{Implementation} \label{sec:implementation}

This chapter describes the implementation of the walk-forward backtesting framework used throughout the thesis. The code is organised around a small number of clearly separated components: a data handler that prepares the investment universe and return series, a backtester that orchestrates the rolling evaluation, and a family of optimizers that implement the portfolio construction problem.\footnote{The full codebase for this project is publicly available on GitHub: \url{https://github.com/anejrozman/Multi-Currency-Portfolio-Optimization-with-General-Risk-Measures}.} This modular structure keeps the experimental setup reproducible while allowing different objective functions and portfolio architectures to share the same evaluation pipeline.

The figure highlights the main design principle of the implementation: the backtester is intentionally kept generic, while the portfolio logic lives in interchangeable optimizer classes. This makes it possible to compare mean-variance and expected shortfall formulations, asset-only and joint asset--currency strategies, and regularized and unregularized variants under the same walk-forward protocol.

\subsection{Backtesting Workflow and Optimizer Structure}

The framework is built around a strict separation of concerns. The \texttt{DataHandler} prepares the inputs for the backtest by loading precomputed return series, mapping each asset to its reporting currency, and building a daily universe mask from the investment universe file. The backtester receives these objects as a single \texttt{data\_bundle} and does not need to know how they were generated.

The \texttt{Backtester} is responsible for the evaluation protocol. It determines rebalancing dates, constructs estimation and validation windows, performs hyperparameter selection when a grid is supplied, and records the resulting out-of-sample returns. All portfolio construction decisions are delegated to subclasses of \texttt{BaseOptimizer}, which all implement \texttt{fit(train\_bundle)}, \texttt{optimize()}, and \texttt{score(weights, val\_bundle)}.

The backtest follows a standard walk-forward procedure. At each rebalance date, the framework forms a rolling estimation window and a separate validation window, evaluates candidate hyperparameters, refits the best configuration on the full estimation sample, and then carries the portfolio into the next holding period. Rebalancing can be daily, weekly, monthly, quarterly, semi-annual, or annual. Before optimization, the code filters the investable universe to currently eligible assets and checks that each selected asset has a complete return history, which keeps missing-data handling outside the optimization problem. Window lengths are stored centrally in \texttt{backtest\_config.py}; the default setup uses a ten-year estimation window and a one-year validation window.

\subsection{Optimization, Costs, and Outputs}

Two optimizer families are implemented: expected shortfall and mean-variance. Expected shortfall is formulated as a convex program with auxiliary variables, optional \(\ell_1\) and \(\ell_2\) regularization, and an \(\ell_1\)-norm leverage cap. Mean-variance uses Bayes--Stein shrinkage for expected returns and a lightly regularized covariance matrix. Both families are available in asset-only and joint asset--currency variants. The asset-only strategies use either hedged returns (\emph{assets-full}) or unhedged returns (\emph{assets-zero}), while the joint strategies solve for asset weights and currency hedge weights together. The equal-weight benchmark follows the same interface but returns a uniform allocation.

All substantive optimizers are solved with CVXPY. When available, Gurobi is used as the default solver with tight tolerances; otherwise, the code falls back to another compatible solver. Transaction costs are applied at each rebalance using separate assumptions for equities and FX, namely 10 basis points and 1 basis point. Costs are computed with a log-linear turnover approximation, and the backtester tracks drifted weights between rebalances so that turnover reflects actual portfolio evolution. The framework outputs daily out-of-sample returns and summary statistics including annualized volatility, maximum drawdown, Sharpe ratio, Sortino ratio, and an expected shortfall ratio.

\section{Numerical Implementation} \label{sec:numerical-implementation}

\section{Performance Evaluation Metrics} \label{sec:performance-evaluation-metrics}

All backtested portfolios are evaluated on the same set of performance metrics, computed from the out-of-sample return series. These include the expeted shortfall at confidence level 80\%, annualized volatility, maximum drawdown, Sharpe ratio, Sortino ratio, expected shortfall ratio and the average turnover for both the asset and currency legs. 

To calculate the expected shortfall at confidence level \(\alpha\), we first compute the value-at-risk at that level, which is the \((1-\alpha)\)-quantile of the return distribution. Then, the expected shortfall is the average of all returns that are less than or equal to this VaR threshold.\footnote{The formal definitions of value at risk and expected shortfall at confidence level \(\alpha\) are given in \Cref{app:es_auxiliary_formulation}.}

The annualized volatility is computed as the standard deviation of daily returns multiplied by the square root of the number of trading days in a year (assumed to be 252).

The maximum drawdown is the largest decline in the cumulative return series.

The Sharpe ratio is the ratio of the average excess return to the standard deviation of returns, annualized by multiplying by the square root of 252. It is the standard measure of risk-adjusted return, and is given by 
\begin{equation*}
\text{SR} = \frac{\bar{R} - R_f}{\sigma_R} \sqrt{252}.
\end{equation*}

The Sortino ratio is a variation of the Sharpe ratio that only considers downside volatility. It is calculated as the average excess return divided by the standard deviation of negative returns, annualized by multiplying by the square root of 252
\begin{equation*}
\text{SOR} = \frac{\bar{R} - R_f}{\sigma_{\text{down}}} \sqrt{252}.
\end{equation*}

The expected shortfall ratio is the ratio of the average return to the expected shortfall at confidence level 80\%, and is given by
\begin{equation*}
\text{ESR} = \frac{\bar{R}}{\text{ES}_{0.8}}.
\end{equation*} 

Finally, the average turnover is computed as the average absolute change in weights between rebalances, separately for the asset and currency legs. This metric captures the trading activity and associated transaction costs of the portfolio strategies.
