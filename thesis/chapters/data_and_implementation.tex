\chapter{Data and Implementation}\label{chap:data-and-implementation}

In this chapter, we describe the data and the backtest implementation with which we evaluate the optimization programs
of \Cref{chap:optimization-framework} in \Cref{chap:backtest-analysis,chap:joint-vs-sequential}. In \Cref{sec:data}
we describe the data sources and the construction of three investable universes of different sizes from the largest
stocks of 14 global economies, and analyze the fully hedged asset returns, the excess currency returns, and the
correlations between them. In \Cref{sec:backtest-implementation} we describe the implementation of the backtest. We
first present in \Cref{sec:backtesting-framework} the modular structure of the backtesting framework. In
\Cref{sec:backtest-configuration} we present the backtested strategies and their benchmarks, and specify the
rebalancing frequency, the lookback window, the selection of the hyperparameters, and the modeling of transaction
costs. Finally, in \Cref{sec:performance-evaluation-metrics} we define the performance metrics on which the strategies
are evaluated in \Cref{chap:backtest-analysis}.

\section{Data}\label{sec:data}

The empirical evaluation of all portfolio optimization strategies in this thesis relies on
a dataset of international equities, foreign exchange spot rates, and forward
exchange rates sourced from Refinitiv Datastream. The one-month U.S.\ Treasury bill rate used in the return and
performance metric calculations is obtained from
Kenneth R.\ French's online data library.\footnote{\url{https://mba.tuck.dartmouth.edu/pages/faculty/ken.french/data_library.html}}
The datasets span from June 1990 to June 2023, covering several different market regimes, including
the dot-com crash, the global financial crisis, the European sovereign debt crisis, and the
COVID-19 pandemic.

We construct the investable universe from 14 economies: Australia,
Canada, Denmark, Norway, Sweden, Hong Kong, Japan, New Zealand, Singapore, South
Africa, Switzerland, the Eurozone, the United Kingdom, and the United States. The universe is
rebuilt at the beginning of each year from the largest stocks by market capitalization at the time (a rolling-window
approach that mitigates survivorship bias). At the start of each year we rank stocks within
each economy and restrict the following year's investments to that set. To study how the
universe size affects the optimization approaches considered, we form three cross-sections, comprising the
top 10, top 50, and top 100 stocks per economy. We refer to these as the small, medium, and large universes. Because the constituents change each
year, each universe spans more distinct stocks over the full sample than are held on any
single date, $407$ for the top 10, $1{,}705$ for the top 50, and $3{,}189$ for the top 100, respectively.

The US dollar serves as the base currency throughout our analysis. For each stock we compute fully hedged
returns, and for each currency the excess currency return, following the model-free return
representation in \Cref{chap:optimization-framework}.

\Cref{tab:summary-statistics} reports annualized average returns and volatilities for the
fully hedged equity returns and excess currency returns, by economy. For each economy, the
asset figures are the cross-sectional average of the daily returns of its individual
stocks. 

We can see that as the investable universe becomes larger, the average returns tend to fall. In the small universe every economy has a positive average return, ranging from
$2.76\%$ (the United Kingdom) to $12.59\%$ (the United States). In the medium universe, the range
drops from $-1.99\%$ (Singapore) to $7.99\%$ (the United States), and in the
large universe the range goes from $-4.84\%$ (Singapore) to $6.86\%$ (the United States), with many
economies now having negative average returns. The
trend reflects the firm size, the largest stocks post the strongest returns, and the smaller
firms added pull the average down.
Volatilities exhibit the opposite pattern, rising with universe size, as smaller, more
volatile stocks are added to the investable universe. Switzerland exhibits the lowest volatility in every case.
Its volatility rises from $29.04\%$ to $33.65\%$, and the upper end rises 
from $43.30\%$ (Norway) in the small universe to $62.12\%$ (Singapore) in the large universe.

Excess currency returns range between $-2.07\%$ for the Japanese yen and $2.48\%$ for the New
Zealand dollar, and excess currency volatilities range from $0.56\%$ for the Hong Kong
dollar, whose peg to the US dollar keeps it stable, up to $15.18\%$ for the
South African rand.

To quantify the dependence between asset and currency returns, we compute Pearson
correlations between economy-level fully hedged equity returns and excess currency returns.
For each economy we form an equally weighted daily portfolio of the fully hedged returns of
its constituent stocks, resetting the constituents at each annual universe reconstitution. 
Correlating each economy's series
with each currency's excess return over the full sample gives a $14 \times 13$ correlation matrix. The
US dollar is dropped from the currency axis, since its excess return is zero by
construction. We repeat this for the three universe sizes.
The resulting heatmaps are shown in \Cref{fig:asset-currency-corr-top-10,fig:asset-currency-corr-top-50,fig:asset-currency-corr-top-100}.

Across all three universes the correlations range from $-0.2$ to
$0.4$ and remain mostly positive. The results are intuitive and agree with known empirical findings. The strongest positive correlations belong to commodity and risk-sensitive currencies such as the AUD, CAD, NZD, and ZAR, whose equity markets and
currencies rise together in good times and fall together in bad \citep{CHEN2003133, common_risk_factors_currency_markets, MenkhoffSarnoSchmelingSchrimpf2012}. The JPY and CHF are the only negatively correlated currencies with most other economies (ranging from $-0.2$ to $0.03$), in line with their role as safe havens that strengthen when equities fall \cite{safe_haven_currencies}. The EUR, DKK, and HKD are minimally correlated, reflecting the HKD's peg to the US dollar and the DKK's peg to the EUR. This split between risky and safe-haven currencies is well
documented \citep{global_currency_hedging, safe_haven_currencies}.

This is the dependence structure the overlay program discards when it optimizes the asset weights. It sets the asset weights first, using only equity information, and adds the currency hedge afterwards, whereas the joint program can act on it
directly. The structure barely changes across universe sizes, and this matters for the
results that follow. A shift in the relative performance of the joint and overlay programs across universe sizes in
\Cref{chap:backtest-analysis,chap:joint-vs-sequential} is therefore not due to a change in the underlying
dependence structure, and we analyze its causes in \Cref{chap:joint-vs-sequential}.

\section{Backtest Implementation} \label{sec:backtest-implementation}

\subsection{Backtesting Framework} \label{sec:backtesting-framework}

In \Cref{chap:backtest-analysis} we evaluate a range of optimization strategies under different risk measures (variance, semi-variance, mean absolute deviation, Expected Shortfall, and second-order
Expected Shortfall), their treatment of
currency exposure (overlay or joint), and the size of the investable
universe. To keep these comparisons fair, the
backtesting framework separates between the
orchestration of the backtest and the optimization logic it evaluates.\footnote{The full codebase with the backtest implementation is publicly available on GitHub: \url{https://github.com/anejrozman/Multi-Currency-Portfolio-Optimization-with-General-Risk-Measures}.}  
The main backtesting loop that ingests data, rolls the estimation window forward, selects
hyperparameters, and records performance is fixed, while the optimizer that
produces portfolio weights is a swappable component. Any optimizer that conforms
to a common interface is therefore evaluated under the same conditions.
The framework is composed of three main components, a data handler, the backtester, and a
family of optimizers under a common abstract base class, whose
interaction is summarized in \Cref{fig:backtest}.

Data ingestion and alignment are implemented in the \texttt{DataHandler}.
It loads the input data, the local unhedged asset returns $R^u$, the currency returns
$R^c$, the forward premia $f$, and the universe mask that identifies which
assets constitute the investable universe at each date. It then aligns them into a
\texttt{data\_bundle}, which is passed to the backtester. The optimizers operate only on the
bundled series, and the construction of returns from
these inputs follows \Cref{sec:returns}.

All implemented optimizers inherit from an abstract base class, \texttt{BaseOptimizer}, that
defines the three-method interface through which the backtester runs them. The
\texttt{fit} method receives a training data bundle and estimates and stores the
quantities the optimizer requires for its objective (e.g., the covariance matrix for the variance optimizers), so that estimation is
performed entirely within each optimizer.
The \texttt{optimize} method solves the specific optimization problem. The \texttt{score} method takes a set of weights and a
validation bundle and returns the validation performance used for hyperparameter
selection. 
More details on the estimators and solver internals invoked inside the methods \texttt{fit} and
\texttt{optimize} can be found in \Cref{chap:optimization-framework} and Appendix~\ref{sec:numerical-implementation}, respectively.

The backtest itself is implemented in the \texttt{Backtester.run} method, which
advances through the data sample on a set rebalancing frequency (daily, weekly, monthly, quarterly, or annual). At each
rebalancing date, the optimizer is calibrated and deployed using a fixed rolling lookback window, partitioned into  an estimation window and a validation window. Hyperparameters are selected by a grid search over
this partition. Each configuration is fitted on the estimation window
via \texttt{fit}, used to produce weights via \texttt{optimize}, and evaluated on
the validation window via \texttt{score}, and the configuration with the
best validation score is retrained on both the estimation and validation windows and finally used to 
produce the portfolio weights for the next out-of-sample period. 
These weights are held until the next rebalancing date. The window then rolls forward and the procedure
repeats.

Each invocation of the backtester for a given optimizer produces a self-contained
run directory. A new \texttt{run\_<n>} folder is created in
\texttt{results/runs/<optimizer\_name>/} and collects the full record of that run: the
out-of-sample return series \texttt{oos\_returns.csv}, the performance
metrics \texttt{metrics.json}, a cumulative-return plot
\texttt{cumulative\_returns\_plot.png}, the hyperparameters selected at each rebalancing date
\texttt{selected\_params.csv}, the asset and currency weights \texttt{weights\_assets.csv} and
\texttt{weights\_currencies.csv}, and a configuration file \texttt{config.json}
recording the run specification. Storing the configuration alongside its outputs
makes each result reproducible and attributable to the settings
that generated it.



\subsection{Backtest Configuration}
\label{sec:backtest-configuration}

We now specify the configuration under which the considered strategies in the thesis
are backtested. We first list the backtested strategies, then describe the chosen backtest specification, and finally the execution
of the currency positions and the transaction costs.
 

We backtest the joint and the sequential currency overlay optimizers under the five risk measures
considered in the thesis, variance, semi-variance, mean absolute deviation, Expected Shortfall, and second-order Expected
Shortfall, each in an unregularized, a regularized, and a mean-risk formulation, as introduced in
\Cref{chap:optimization-framework}. As benchmarks we use the equally weighted portfolio and two regularized
asset-only optimizers, Full and Zero, under the same five risk measures. EW and Full are evaluated on fully hedged
returns, and the joint and overlay optimizers on fully hedged returns plus the excess currency returns of their currency
positions. Zero optimizes the same objective as Full but is evaluated on unhedged returns, so it keeps the full currency
exposure of its asset positions. The following portfolio strategies are considered:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{EW}: The fully hedged equally weighted portfolio.
  \item \textbf{Full}: The fully hedged regularized asset portfolio with both $L^1$ and $L^2$ penalties.
  \item \textbf{Zero}: The unhedged regularized asset portfolio with both $L^1$ and $L^2$ penalties.
  \item \textbf{Overlay}: The unregularized sequential currency overlay portfolio.
  \item \textbf{Regularized Overlay}: The overlay portfolio with both $L^1$ and $L^2$ penalties on the asset and currency
  weights.
  \item \textbf{Mean-risk Overlay}: The unregularized overlay portfolio with an added mean term.
  \item \textbf{Joint}: The unregularized joint optimization portfolio.
  \item \textbf{Regularized Joint}: The joint optimization portfolio with both $L^1$ and $L^2$ penalties on the asset and
  currency weights.
  \item \textbf{Mean-risk Joint}: The unregularized joint optimization portfolio with an added mean term.
\end{itemize}


Portfolios are rebalanced on the last
trading day of each quarter. The first portfolio is formed
on 30 June 2000, and the last on 30 December 2022, which gives $91$
rebalancing dates. The lookback window covers the ten years
before each rebalancing date, approximately $2{,}500$ daily observations. All strategies invest in the constituents of the current year's universe that have a
complete return history over the lookback window.

The penalty weights of the regularized optimizers are selected at every rebalancing date by a grid search, for which the
lookback window is partitioned into a nine-year estimation window and a one-year validation window. Each configuration is
fitted on the estimation window, and the resulting weights are scored by the risk measure they realize on the validation
window. The
configuration with the lowest validation risk is then re-fitted on both the estimation and validation windows, so that the final weights
also use the most recent year of data. More details on the grid search and the hyperparameter range analysis are given in
\Cref{app:hyperparameter-sensitivity}.

The mean-risk optimizers also require expected asset and currency returns, which are obtained at each
rebalancing date from the lookback window. Since individual stock means are hard to estimate accurately, we combine them by economy, as derived in \Cref{chap:optimization-framework}. That is, the means of the equally weighted 
economy returns are shrunk toward zero with a
James--Stein estimator, and every asset inherits the shrunk mean of its economy. The expected excess currency returns are
not estimated from returns but set to the negative forward premium at the end of the lookback window. 
Due to computational complexity considerations, the risk aversion coefficient $\gamma$ is not selected on the
validation window. Instead, we run each mean-risk optimizer over a grid of $\gamma$ values on the first lookback window between July 1990 and June 2000 and fix one value for each risk
measure. More details on the risk aversion parameter selection can be found in \Cref{app:risk-aversion-calibration}.


The currency positions of the hedged strategies are implemented with three-month forward contracts. Since each contract
expires at the next rebalancing date, the full forward position is traded at every rebalancing date rather than only its
change, which is why the currency turnover \eqref{eq:currency-turnover} in \Cref{sec:performance-evaluation-metrics} equals the notional of the
forward positions. All returns are reported net of transaction costs. We assume constant half-spreads of $10$ basis points for equities and $1$ basis
point for currencies, which gives transaction costs of
$\tau^{asset} \approx 0.002$ and $\tau^{curr} \approx 0.0002$ per unit traded.  We derive the 
transaction costs in detail in Appendix~\ref{app:transaction_costs}.

\subsection{Performance Evaluation Metrics}
\label{sec:performance-evaluation-metrics}

In \Cref{chap:backtest-analysis} we evaluate the performance of all considered portfolio optimization strategies on a common set of performance metrics computed from
their out-of-sample return series, each capturing a different view of the portfolio's performance. How well the
joint and overlay programs reduce the risk measure they minimize is analyzed separately in
\Cref{chap:joint-vs-sequential}. Throughout this section, let $T$ be the number of trading days in the out-of-sample period, and let $R_{\mathcal P,t}$ denote
the realized daily out-of-sample portfolio return, $\bar R_{\mathcal P}$ its
sample mean, $\sigma_{\mathcal P}$ its annualized volatility, $\sigma_{\mathcal{P}, \text{down}}$ its annualized downside volatility, $R_{f, t}$ the
daily risk-free
rate, and $\bar R_f$ the
average daily risk-free rate. Daily figures are annualized using the standard $252$ trading days per year assumption. The metrics we report are
the annualized excess return, annualized volatility, maximum drawdown, the Sharpe ratio, the Sortino ratio, and average asset
and currency turnover.

The annualized excess return is the compound annual growth rate of the portfolio in
excess of that of the risk-free rate
\begin{equation*}
  \mathrm{ER_{\mathcal P}}
  = \left( \prod_{t=1}^{T} \left(1 + R_{\mathcal P,t}\right) \right)^{252/T}
  - \left( \prod_{t=1}^{T} \left(1 + R_{f,t}\right) \right)^{252/T}.
\end{equation*}
It is the annual return an investor holding the portfolio over the full sample would
have earned on top of the risk-free rate. 
Since the risk-minimizing optimizers do not target returns, inspecting the excess return shows
how much return they give up in exchange for lower risk.

Annualized volatility is the standard deviation of portfolio returns
\begin{equation*}
  \sigma_{\mathcal P} = \sqrt{252} \, \sqrt{\frac{1}{T-1} \sum_{t=1}^{T}
  \left(R_{\mathcal P,t} - \bar R_{\mathcal P}\right)^2}.
\end{equation*}
It is the most common measure of risk used to evaluate portfolio performance and is also used in the
calculation of the Sharpe ratio to measure risk-adjusted performance.
Because a portfolio can achieve low volatility while still suffering severe cumulative
losses over time, it is most informative when interpreted together with the maximum
drawdown.

Maximum drawdown is the largest decline of the cumulative return
path of the portfolio. Unlike volatility,
which summarizes a one-period return distribution, drawdown is a property of the
realized wealth path, capturing the cumulative and sequential experience of losses
that an investor goes through.\ Such path-dependent losses matter
in practice.\ For example, deep drawdowns can breach margin requirements. 
Importantly, none of the optimizers considered in the thesis targets drawdown
directly, so it serves as an out-of-sample robustness check. Let $V_{\mathcal{P},t} = \prod_{s \le t}(1 + R_{\mathcal P,s})$ denote
the cumulative wealth of the portfolio, with $V_{\mathcal{P},0} = 1$, and $P_{\mathcal{P},t} = \max_{s \le t} V_{\mathcal{P},s}$ its running
maximum. The maximum drawdown is defined as
\begin{equation*}
\mathrm{MD_{\mathcal P}} = \max_{t} \frac{P_{\mathcal P,t} - V_{\mathcal P,t}}{P_{\mathcal P,t}}.
\end{equation*}

The Sharpe ratio is the annualized ratio of the average excess portfolio return
relative to its total volatility. It is the standard measure of return per unit of
total risk. The Sharpe ratio is given by
\begin{equation*}
\mathrm{SR}_{\mathcal P} = 252\,
\frac{\bar{R}_{\mathcal P} - \bar R_f}{\sigma_{\mathcal P}}.
\end{equation*}
Since the risk-minimizing optimizers run at a much lower volatility than the equally weighted
portfolio, their returns alone cannot be compared fairly. The Sharpe ratio corrects
for this, and a strategy with a higher Sharpe ratio earns a higher return
at any level of risk an investor chooses. Like volatility, however, it penalizes
favorable and adverse deviations equally.

The Sortino ratio replaces volatility in the Sharpe ratio with downside volatility,
so that a strategy is not penalized for upside dispersion. Read against the Sharpe
ratio, it indicates whether a strategy's risk-adjusted performance stems from downside
protection or from lower overall variability. The Sortino ratio is given by
\begin{equation*}
\mathrm{SOR}_{\mathcal P} = 252\,\frac{\bar R_{\mathcal P} - \bar R_f}{\sigma_{\mathcal P, \text{down}}},
\end{equation*}
where the annualized downside volatility
\begin{equation*}
\sigma_{\mathcal P,\text{down}} = \sqrt{252}\,\sqrt{\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}
\min\left(R_{\mathcal P,t},\, 0\right)^2}
\end{equation*}
only considers negative returns. 

Finally, turnover proxies the trading activity and the implementation
costs of each strategy. Although the reported returns are already net of
transaction costs, turnover remains informative because it
measures how much the portfolio must trade to maintain its positions, its capacity to scale, and its exposure to frictions such as slippage and market impact that our analysis does not capture. A strategy whose performance depends on heavy rebalancing can be hard to implement, so low and stable turnover is a desirable property. Turnover is reported separately for the asset and currency legs,
since the two are traded through different instruments with different cost levels.
Asset turnover is the average rebalancing volume over the out-of-sample period
\begin{equation*}
\mathrm{Turnover}^{a} = \frac{1}{D}\sum_{d=1}^{D}\sum_{i=1}^{N}
\bigl| x_{i,d} - x_{i,d}^{-} \bigr|,
\end{equation*}
where $D$ is the number of out-of-sample rebalancing dates, $x_{i,d}^{-}$ the weight of
asset $i$ just before the rebalance at date $d$, and $x_{i,d}$ the weight set at that
rebalance. Currency turnover is the average absolute notional of the
forward positions
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{Turnover}^{c} = \frac{1}{D}\sum_{d=1}^{D}\sum_{c=1}^{M}
\bigl| \upphi_{c,d} \bigr|. \label{eq:currency-turnover} 
\end{equation}
