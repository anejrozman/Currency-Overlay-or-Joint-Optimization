This chapter asks whether joint optimization of assets and currencies is worth the additional complexity 
rather than just doingsequential currency overlay. First we establish that the joint program's
in-sample advantage is guaranteed by construction, since the overlay's solution
is feasible for it, we show this across the five risk measures. We further show that the advantage largely fails to survive out of
sample: it is significant only in the smallest universe, and the share of it
that is realized falls as the in-sample advantage grows. We
identify the reason for that inversion, namely that the in-sample advantage is
inflated by estimation error in proportion to how little of the sample the risk
measure uses. The central evidence is a sweep of Expected Shortfall over
fourteen confidence levels, which varies the number of observations entering the
objective by a factor of sixteen while holding the risk measure, the universe
and the data fixed; it is corroborated by a comparison across measures that
separates those using the whole sample from those using only its tail. Neither
regularization nor the addition of a mean term alters the conclusion. What
follows is that the in-sample gap is a measure of how much room the joint
program has to fit noise rather than of its economic value, so that joint
optimization is justified in small universes and least of all for the tail
measures whose in-sample case appears strongest.

\section{Does Joint Optimization improve Returns?}\label{sec:joint-returns}

The backtests of \Cref{chap:empirical-analysis-and-baseline-results} gave no consistent return advantage
to joint optimization. To futher inspect this, we test the difference of the two annualized
Sharpe ratios, 
\begin{equation*}
\Delta \coloneqq \mathrm{SR}_J - \mathrm{SR}_O,
\qquad
H_0 : \Delta = 0 ,
\end{equation*} 
on the realized daily excess returns of the two backtests, net of transaction costs. Two features of the
setting dictate the choice of test. The two strategies are evaluated on the same dates, over the same
universe and from the same price data, and differ only in how the weights are chosen, so their return
series are strongly dependent, with correlations between $0.90$ and $0.99$ across the comparisons reported
below; this rules out any test that treats the samples as independent. And daily equity returns are
heavy-tailed and serially dependent, which violates the i.i.d.\ normality assumed by the closed-form test
of \cite{JobsonKorkie1981} and its correction in \cite{Memmel2003}. We therefore use the studentized
bootstrap of \cite{LedoitWolf2008}, which is robust to both. The standard error of $\hat\Delta$ follows
from the delta method applied to the four moments $(\mu_J, \mu_O, \gamma_J, \gamma_O)$ that determine the
two Sharpe ratios, with a Bartlett HAC estimate of their long-run covariance; its distribution is obtained
by a circular block bootstrap in which the same index sequence is applied to both return series, so that
the dependence between the strategies is preserved under resampling. We use blocks of $b = 5$ trading days
and $B = 4999$ replications on $T = 5935$ daily observations. The test is run on every pair of joint and
sequential production runs, five risk measures, three universe sizes and both program families, giving
$27$ comparisons once the three cells whose second-order Expected Shortfall backtests are unavailable are
dropped. The derivation and the full set of diagnostics are given in Appendix~\ref{app:sharpe_test}.

The results are reported in \Cref{tab:sharpe-test}. Not one of the $27$ comparisons is significant, at the
$5\%$ level or at the $10\%$: the smallest $p$-value is $0.183$ and the median $0.645$, the point estimates
are as often negative as positive, $15$ of $27$ favouring the joint program, with a median difference of
$+0.007$ annualized Sharpe units, and there is no systematic variation across risk measures, universe sizes
or families. Repeating every test with block lengths from $b = 1$ to $b = 63$ trading days leaves all of
them insignificant.

This is not evidence that the two approaches deliver the same risk-adjusted return; it is a statement about
what the Sharpe ratio can resolve on a sample of this length. The intervals have a median half-width of
$0.073$, so they exclude improvements above roughly $0.13$ Sharpe units, while the volatility reduction the
joint program actually achieves at the top-$10$ universe, between $2.5\%$ and $5.7\%$ in relative terms,
implies a Sharpe improvement of about $0.003$, two orders of magnitude below what these intervals can
exclude. An effect of the size that is present could not have been detected however the test were
constructed. That the same bootstrap applied to the same return series does find differences in volatility
and in Expected Shortfall, with intervals clear of zero at the top-$10$ and top-$50$ universes, confirms
that the procedure detects effects in these data whenever the estimand is one the sample can pin down. The
remainder of this chapter therefore evaluates joint optimization on the quantity it actually targets, the
risk measure itself.


\section{Advantage of Joint Optimization for Risk Minimization}\label{sec:joint-in-sample-case}

In-sample, joint optimization provides a benefit over sequential optimization by construction when it comes to minimizing 
the target risk measure. We write 
the decision vector as $\bm{\theta} = (\bm{x}, \bm{\uppsi})$ and let $\Theta$ denote the 
feasible set defined by the budget and leverage constraints of \Cref{chap:optimization-framework}. 
Let $\hat f$ be the empirical risk on the estimation window and $f^{oos}$ the realized risk on 
the holding quarter. The two programs are
\begin{equation*}
\hat{\bm{\theta}}_J = \argmin_{\bm{\theta} \in \Theta} \hat f(\bm{\theta})
\quad \text{and} \quad
\hat{\bm{x}}_O = \argmin_{\bm{x}} \hat f_A(\bm{x}),
\quad
\hat{\bm{\uppsi}}_O = \argmin_{\bm{\uppsi}} \hat f(\hat{\bm{x}}_O, \bm{\uppsi}),
\end{equation*}
where $\hat f_A$ is the asset-only risk, which ignores the currency block, and $\hat{\bm{\theta}}_O = (\hat{\bm{x}}_O, \hat{\bm{\uppsi}}_O)$. 
The subscript $J$ denotes the joint program and $O$ the sequential overlay. 
The sequential program is a restricted minimizer. It chooses $\bm{x}$ against a different objective, 
then chooses $\bm{\uppsi}$ with $\hat{\bm{x}}_O$ held fixed. 

Its output satisfies the same constraints, so $\hat{\bm{\theta}}_O \in \Theta$, and its second step is a partial minimization of the same $\hat f$. Hence
\begin{equation*}
\hat f(\hat{\bm{\theta}}_J)
= \min_{\bm{x}, \bm{\uppsi}} \hat f(\bm{x}, \bm{\uppsi})
\;\leq\; \min_{\bm{\uppsi}} \hat f(\hat{\bm{x}}_O, \bm{\uppsi})
= \hat f(\hat{\bm{\theta}}_O).
\end{equation*}
We define the in-sample gap between the two optimizers as 
\begin{equation}
g^{is} \coloneqq \hat f(\hat{\bm{\theta}}_O) - \hat f(\hat{\bm{\theta}}_J) \;\geq\; 0 .
\label{eq:gap-in-sample}
\end{equation}
The in-sample gap is non-negative and measures the advantage of joint optimization over sequential 
optimization in the estimation window. Naturally, the question we are interested in is whether this advantage is 
realized out of sample. 

We define the out-of-sample error of a fitted $\hat{\bm{\theta}}$ as
\begin{equation*}
\epsilon(\hat{\bm{\theta}}) \coloneqq f^{oos}(\hat{\bm{\theta}}) - \hat f(\hat{\bm{\theta}}),
\end{equation*}
the amount by which realized risk in the holding quarter exceeds the risk the optimizer achieved in sample. 
Then the out-of-sample gap is written as
\begin{align}
g^{oos} &\coloneqq f^{oos}(\hat{\bm{\theta}}_O) - f^{oos}(\hat{\bm{\theta}}_J) \nonumber \\
        &= \underbrace{\big[\hat f(\hat{\bm{\theta}}_O) - \hat f(\hat{\bm{\theta}}_J)\big]}_{g^{is} \,\geq\, 0}
\;-\; \underbrace{\big[\epsilon(\hat{\bm{\theta}}_J) - \epsilon(\hat{\bm{\theta}}_O)\big]}_{\Delta\epsilon}.
\label{eq:gap-decomposition}
\end{align}

The sign of $\Delta\epsilon$ is not guaranteed. The literature on estimation error in
portfolio optimization suggests it should typically be positive, since the joint program selects over a
larger space and absorbs more noise in doing so (Michaud, 1989; Kan and Zhou, 2007), though a restriction
can also reduce estimation error rather than merely constrain the optimizer (Jagannathan and Ma, 2003).
The magnitude is measure-dependent. Expected shortfall estimates are determined by the small fraction of
tail scenarios that bind at the optimum, so the optimism absorbed by the optimizer is larger and more
volatile than under variance at comparable sample sizes (Lim et al., 2011; Gotoh et al., 2013), and we
expect the $\Delta\epsilon$ estimates to be correspondingly noisier for that measure.

We perform the following gap analysis to investigate the out-of-sample performance of the joint and sequential programs with respect to the risk measure they are minimizing, therefore we initially consider the unregularized formulation from \Cref{chap:optimization-framework}. At each of the quarterly rebalancing dates the joint and the sequential overlay program of a given risk measure are solved on the same ten-year estimation window, and under the same budget and leverage constraints from \Cref{chap:empirical-analysis-and-baseline-results}. The resulting weights are then held fixed and scored twice, once on the estimation window itself and once on the following holding quarter, which gives $\hat f$ and $f^{oos}$ for each of the two programs. Both gaps are reported relative to the risk of the sequential program, $g^{is} / \hat f(\hat{\bm{\theta}}_O)$ and $g^{oos} / f^{oos}(\hat{\bm{\theta}}_O)$ respectively, so they are to be interpreted as the risk reduction achieved by the joint program over the sequential program, expressed as a percentage of the latter's risk. For each risk measure and universe size we report the mean of the risk reduction across the rebalancing dates together with a circular block bootstrap confidence interval at 95\%, using $10{,}000$ resamples and a block length chosen by the Politis--White rule \cite{PolitisWhite2004, PattonPolitisWhite2009}.

We see in \Cref{fig:gap-measures} that the in-sample advantage of joint optimization is positive for all risk measures across all universe sizes, which is expected, and that it is largest for the two considered tail measures. The out-of-sample gap is smaller than the in-sample gap for almost  all risk measures and universe sizes, the only exception being for the variance and semi-variance measures at the smallest universe size. Out of sample the picture depends on the size of the investment universe. At the top-$10$ universe the advantage is significant for four of the five risk measures, the exception being second-order Expected Shortfall; at top-$50$ only volatility and semi-deviation survive, and at top-$100$ none of the five do. The in-sample series separates the two groups of risk measures in a second way. For the deviation measures the in-sample advantage falls sharply as the universe grows, from $2.45\%$ to $0.63\%$ for volatility, from $2.67\%$ to $1.26\%$ for mean absolute deviation and from $3.08\%$ to $1.37\%$ for semi-deviation, whereas for the two tail measures it barely moves, from $3.95\%$ to $3.49\%$ for Expected Shortfall and from $7.73\%$ to $5.81\%$ for second-order Expected Shortfall. Read together with the out-of-sample series this produces an inversion. Ordered by their in-sample advantage at top-$10$, the measures run second-order Expected Shortfall, Expected Shortfall, semi-deviation, mean absolute deviation and volatility, while the share of that advantage which is actually realized runs broadly the other way, at $23\%$, $70\%$, $119\%$, $81\%$ and $133\%$ respectively (Spearman $\rho = -0.90$). The risk measures whose in-sample case is strongest are those that keep the least of it. Five risk measures is a small sample on which to rest an ordering, and we return to it in \Cref{sec:mechanism-level} with an experiment that varies the same quantity within a single risk measure.

\Cref{fig:gap-measures} shows both gaps, but the quantity that \eqref{eq:gap-decomposition} turns on is their difference, and that difference cannot be read off the figure with any precision. The two series move together across rebalancing dates, sharing a universe, an estimation window and a draw of returns, so differencing their confidence intervals discards the pairing and overstates the uncertainty. For Expected Shortfall at the top-$100$ universe the in-sample gap is $[2.81, 4.15]$ and the out-of-sample gap $[-1.54, 0.32]$, which differenced in that way would leave even the sign of $\Delta\epsilon$ open; bootstrapping the paired difference at each rebalancing date instead gives $[3.28, 5.03]$ percentage points. \Cref{fig:delta-epsilon} reports $\Delta\epsilon$ obtained this way, with the deviation and the tail measures on separate panels because the latter run an order of magnitude higher.

Two features stand out. The ordering across risk measures follows how much of the estimation window each objective uses: second-order Expected Shortfall forfeits between $5.8$ and $8.5$ percentage points and Expected Shortfall between $1.2$ and $4.2$, while the three deviation measures never leave a band of $\pm 1.5$ percentage points. Second, $\Delta\epsilon$ grows with the universe size for Expected Shortfall, from $1.17$ to $2.36$ to $4.16$ percentage points, with its confidence interval tightening as it does, while the deviation measures stay flat. The loss therefore has two distinct sources, the number of assets whose weights must be estimated and the share of the sample the risk measure is willing to look at, and only the second separates the tail measures from the deviation measures. We do not read the two negative points as evidence that joint optimization does better out of sample than in: volatility and semi-deviation at top-$10$ both have intervals that cross zero, and no cell in the figure is significantly negative. We also note that $\Delta\epsilon$ is negative on between a quarter and two fifths of the individual rebalancing dates even in the cells whose mean is clearly positive, so what the figure reports is a tendency rather than a rule.

\section{Risk-Return Formulation}\label{sec:no-repair-mean}


\section{When Joint Optimization Is Justified}\label{sec:when-justified}

We conclude that even though joint optimization does not provide a statistical benefit in terms of returns, it has an advantage over 
sequential optimization by construction when it comes to minimizing the targeted risk measure. When the risk measures are
deviation measures, the in-sample benefit from joint optimization falls with an increasing universe size, while it stays stable for the 
two considered tail measures. Out-of-sample, the advantage falls with universe size for all five of the considered risk measures. 
Transforming the tail measures into deviation measures by de-meaning them results in a drop in the in-sample advantage as the 
universe size increases. This leads us to conclude that the implicit risk-return tradeoff is one of the drivers of the in-sample
advantage of joint optimization, and that it disappears out of sample due to estimation error. When we investigate the 
tail behavior of the expected shortfall optimizers, the gap between the in-sample and out-of-sample advantage of 
joint optimization increases with the tail depth, which further supports that the 
in-sample advantage is driven by estimation error. Neither regularization nor the addition of a mean term to 
the objective function changes this. In the larger investment universes, there is no detectable 
benefit from joint optimization out of sample. This leads us to conclude that joint optimization does provide a 
benefit when it comes to risk reduction, but only when the investment universe is small, or rather, when the 
number of assets is smaller relative to the number of currencies.
