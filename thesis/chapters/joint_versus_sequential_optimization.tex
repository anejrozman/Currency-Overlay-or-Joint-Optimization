\chapter{Joint versus Sequential Optimization}\label{chap:joint-vs-sequential}

In this chapter, we compare and analyze the performance of the joint and sequential overlay programs in terms of risk minimization. We first
show in \Cref{sec:joint-in-sample-case} that the joint program has an advantage over the sequential program by construction in terms of 
minimizing the target risk measure, since the overlay's solution is feasible for the joint program. We define the in-sample and out-of-sample gaps between the 
two programs and investigate the realized advantage of joint optimization across the five risk measures for investable universes of different sizes. The results, 
 as in \Cref{chap:empirical-analysis-and-baseline-results}, separate the risk measures into two groups, deviation and tail measures. In \Cref{sec:mechanism-level} we further investigate the 
tail measures, perform the same gap analysis across different confidence levels and show that the in-sample advantage of joint optimization grows with the tail depth, 
while the out-of-sample advantage does not. We then transform the tail measures into deviation measures and show that the in-sample advantage of joint optimization begins to fall
with the universe size. In \Cref{sec:mechanism-measures} we focus on the deviation measures. We argue why a larger percentage of 
the in-sample advantage of joint optimization is realized out of sample than for the tail measures. Finally, in \Cref{sec:reg-and-rr-formulation} 
we show that regularization and the addition of a mean term to the objective function weaken the advantage of joint over sequential optimization out of sample.


\section{Advantage of Joint Optimization for Risk Minimization}\label{sec:joint-in-sample-case}

In-sample, joint optimization provides a benefit over sequential optimization by construction when it comes to minimizing 
the target risk measure. We write 
the weight vector as $\bm{\theta} = (\bm{x}, \bm{\psi})$ and let $\Theta = \mathcal{X} \times \R^M$ denote the 
feasible set defined by the budget and leverage constraints as in \Cref{chap:optimization-framework}.
For a rebalancing period $t \in \{1, \dots, T\}$, let $\rho_t^{\text{IS}}$ be the in-sample risk over the 
estimation window and $\rho_t^{\text{OOS}}$ the out-of-sample realized risk over the next holding period. Let $\hat{\bm{\theta}}_t$ denote
the weight vector an 
optimizer returns on the estimation window, and $\rho_t^{\text{OOS}}(\hat{\bm{\theta}}_t)$ and $\rho_t^{\text{IS}}(\hat{\bm{\theta}}_t)$ are the risk these weight vectors realize. The two optimization programs are written as
\begin{equation*}
\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{J,t} \in \argmin_{\bm{\theta} \in \Theta} \rho_t^{\text{IS}}(\bm{\theta}),
\end{equation*}
and
\begin{equation*}
\hat{\bm{x}}_{O,t} \in \argmin_{\bm{x} \in \mathcal{X}} \rho_{A,t}^{\text{IS}}(\bm{x}),
\quad
\hat{\bm{\psi}}_{O,t} \in \argmin_{\bm{\psi} \in \R^M} \rho_t^{\text{IS}}(\hat{\bm{x}}_{O,t}, \bm{\psi}),
\end{equation*}
where $\rho_{A,t}^{\text{IS}}(\bm{x}) \coloneqq \rho_t^{\text{IS}}(\bm{x}, \bm{0})$ is the in-sample risk of the fully hedged asset portfolio, and $\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{i,t} = (\hat{\bm{x}}_{i,t}, \hat{\bm{\psi}}_{i,t})$, $i\in\{J, O\}$. 
The subscript $J$ denotes the joint optimizer and $O$ the sequential overlay optimizer.
The overlay optimizer is a restricted minimizer. It first minimizes over $\bm{x}$ against a different objective, 
then over $\bm{\psi}$ with $\hat{\bm{x}}_{O,t}$ held fixed. 

The overlay's output satisfies the joint program's constraints, so $\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{O,t} \in \Theta$, and its second step is a partial minimization of the same $\rho_t^{\text{IS}}$. Hence
\begin{equation*}
\rho_t^{\text{IS}}(\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{J,t})
= \min_{\bm{\theta} \in \Theta} \rho_t^{\text{IS}}(\bm{\theta})
\;\leq\; \min_{\bm{\psi} \in \R^M} \rho_t^{\text{IS}}(\hat{\bm{x}}_{O,t}, \bm{\psi})
= \rho_t^{\text{IS}}(\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{O,t}).
\end{equation*}
We define the in-sample gap between the two optimizers as 
\begin{equation*}
g_t^{\text{IS}} \coloneqq \rho_t^{\text{IS}}(\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{O,t}) - \rho_t^{\text{IS}}(\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{J,t}) \;\geq\; 0 .
\label{eq:gap-in-sample}
\end{equation*}
The in-sample gap is non-negative and measures the advantage of joint optimization over sequential 
optimization in the estimation window. Naturally, the question we are interested in is whether this advantage is 
realized out of sample. 

We define the out-of-sample error of a fitted $\hat{\bm{\theta}}_t$ as
\begin{equation*}
\epsilon_t(\hat{\bm{\theta}}_t) \coloneqq \rho_t^{\text{OOS}}(\hat{\bm{\theta}}_t) - \rho_t^{\text{IS}}(\hat{\bm{\theta}}_t),
\end{equation*}
the amount by which realized risk in the holding quarter exceeds the risk the optimizer achieved in 
sample.\footnote{This quantity is the portfolio analogue of the optimism of the training error, see for example Section 7.4 in \cite{HastieTibshiraniFriedman2009}.} 
Then the out-of-sample gap is defined as
\begin{align*}
g_t^{\text{OOS}} &\coloneqq \rho_t^{\text{OOS}}(\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{O,t}) - \rho_t^{\text{OOS}}(\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{J,t}) \nonumber \\
        &= \underbrace{\big[\rho_t^{\text{IS}}(\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{O,t}) - \rho_t^{\text{IS}}(\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{J,t})\big]}_{g_t^{\text{IS}} \,\geq\, 0}
\;-\; \underbrace{\big[\epsilon_t(\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{J,t}) - \epsilon_t(\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{O,t})\big]}_{\Delta\epsilon_t}.
\label{eq:gap-decomposition}
\end{align*}

The difference between the out-of-sample errors $\Delta\epsilon_t$ can be either positive or negative. For the variance, the in-sample risk of an optimized portfolio is known to understate its true risk, and the more
weights are fitted relative to the sample size, the larger this understatement becomes \cite{ElKaroui2010}. Since the joint program fits all of its weights to the same in-sample objective, we expect it to
adapt more closely to the noise in the estimation window than the sequential overlay, and hence $\Delta\epsilon_t$ to be typically positive. The restriction imposed
by the sequential overlay could also reduce its estimation error rather than just constrain the optimizer. If $\Delta\epsilon_t$ exceeds $g_t^{\text{IS}}$, the overlay could in some cases realize lower risk than the joint program out of sample. We expect $\Delta\epsilon_t$ to be measure-dependent. Tail measures, in our case Expected Shortfall and second-order Expected Shortfall, are estimated from
a smaller fraction of the sample, whereas among the deviation measures, variance and mean absolute deviation use every observation and semi-variance
only those below the mean. For a given sample size, we therefore expect  $\Delta\epsilon_t$ to be larger and more volatile for the tail measures. 

We perform the following gap analysis to investigate the in and out of sample performance of the joint and sequential overlay programs with respect to the risk measure they are minimizing. The in-sample gap is guaranteed to be non-negative only when each program minimizes the risk measure it is scored on, so we initially consider the unregularized formulation from \Cref{chap:optimization-framework}. At each of the $T = 91$ quarterly rebalancing dates the joint and the sequential overlay optimizers, for a given risk measure, are solved on the same ten-year estimation window, and under the same budget and leverage constraints. The resulting weights are then held fixed and scored twice, once on the estimation window itself and once on the following holding quarter, which gives $\rho_t^{\text{IS}}$ and $\rho_t^{\text{OOS}}$ for each of the two programs. For variance and semi-variance we score their square roots, volatility and semi-deviation. The square root does not change the minimizers, and makes all five measures positively homogeneous, so that the gaps are comparable across measures. Both gaps are reported relative to the risk of the overlay optimizer,
\begin{equation*}
\tilde g_t^{\text{IS}} \coloneqq \frac{g_t^{\text{IS}}}{\rho_t^{\text{IS}}(\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{O,t})}
\qquad \text{and} \qquad
\tilde g_t^{\text{OOS}} \coloneqq \frac{g_t^{\text{OOS}}}{\rho_t^{\text{OOS}}(\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{O,t})},
\label{eq:gap-relative}
\end{equation*}
so they are to be interpreted as the risk reduction achieved by the joint optimizer over the overlay, expressed as a percentage of the latter's risk. For each risk measure and universe size we report the mean of the risk reduction across the rebalancing dates together with a 95\% bootstrap confidence interval based on $10{,}000$ resamples.

We see in \Cref{fig:gap-measures} that the in-sample advantage of joint optimization is positive for all risk measures across all universe sizes, and that it is largest for the two considered tail measures. The out-of-sample gap is smaller than the in-sample gap for almost  all risk measures and universe sizes, the only exception being the volatility and semi-deviation measures at the smallest universe size. Out-of-sample we see a negative dependence on the size of the investable universe. At the top-$10$ universe the realized out-of-sample advantage of joint optimization is in the range between $1.78\%$ and $3.67\%$, with second-order Expected Shortfall having the smallest benefit and Semi-Deviation having the largest. At top-$50$, the advantage drops to zero for second-order Expected Shortfall, and remains around $1\%$ for the other measures, and at top-$100$ the advantage for Expected Shortfall drops below zero while the remaining measures stay below $1\%$. The in-sample advantage falls as the universe grows, from $2.45\%$ to $0.63\%$ for volatility, from $2.67\%$ to $1.26\%$ for mean absolute deviation and from $3.08\%$ to $1.37\%$ for semi-deviation, whereas for the two tail measures it remains larger and more stable, from $3.95\%$ to $3.49\%$ for Expected Shortfall and from $7.73\%$ to $5.81\%$ for second-order Expected Shortfall. This is expected, since the tail measures are estimated on a smaller fraction of the sample. The second thing is the size of the unrealized

\section{Tail Measures}\label{sec:mechanism-level}

To investigate the mechanism behind the in-sample advantage of joint optimization for the two tail measures, Expected Shortfall and second-order Expected Shortfall, we perform the same gap analysis as in \Cref{sec:joint-in-sample-case} across different confidence levels. We report the results in \Cref{fig:gap-vs-level-es,fig:gap-vs-level-soes}.

  We see in \Cref{fig:gap-vs-level-es} that the in-sample advantage of joint optimization for minimizing Expected Shortfall 
  grows with the confidence level and the growth is more pronounced in the smaller universe. 
  The out-of-sample advantage is much smaller and does not rise with the confidence level at any universe size. 
  In the small top-10 universe, the in-sample advantage rises from $3.11\%$ at confidence level $60\%$ to $10.59\%$ at level $97.5\%$. 
  
  
  advantage grows from $3.11\%$ at level $60\%$ to $10.59\%$ at level $97.5\%$, 
  while the realized advantage stays in the $1.30-2.92\%$ range. In the larger universes the in-sample advantage grows at a 
  lower rate, going from $3.23\%$ to $7.39\%$ at top-50 and from $3.86\%$ to $5.78\%$ at top-100, while the 
  realized advantage never exceeds $1.32\%$ and $0.09\%$ respectively.  

  Expected Shortfall at confidence level $\alpha$ is an average over the worst $(1-\alpha)$ fraction of the estimation window, so at level $97.5\%$ it has sixteen times fewer observations than at $60\%$. The joint program can use its additional freedom in $\bm{x}$ to push the specific worst scenarios of the window down, which the overlay, having fixed $\bm{x}$ against the fully hedged objective, cannot. These scenarios do not recur in the holding quarter, so the extra in-sample reduction is fitted to noise and $\Delta\epsilon_t$ grows at the same rate as $g_t^{\text{IS}}$. This is in line with the known instability of Expected Shortfall optimization, where the estimation error depends on the number of weights relative to the number of tail observations rather than the full sample size \cite{CilibertiKondorMezard2007}.

The ordering across universe sizes follows from what the joint program gains over the overlay. The overlay already chooses all $N$ asset weights freely, and the joint program adds only the coupling between them and the $M$ currency positions. As the universe grows, the currency positions make up a smaller share of the decision variables, and the overlay's first step alone has enough freedom to fit the tail scenarios of the window, so the joint program's in-sample advantage grows more slowly with the level. Out of sample, both programs are fitted to few tail observations with many weights, and at top-100 the joint program's structural advantage is no longer distinguishable from zero at any level.

In \Cref{fig:gap-vs-level-soes} we see that second-order Expected Shortfall behaves similarly to Expected Shortfall in a more extreme form, and that it stops growing at higher confidence levels. In the top-$10$ universe the
in-sample advantage rises from $4.00\%$ at level $60\%$ to $16.28\%$, and is flat
from level $92.5\%$ on. At top-$50$ it rises from $3.44\%$ to $8.52\%$ and is flat from level $85\%$ on, and
at top-$100$ from $3.83\%$ to $5.81\%$ and flat from level $75\%$ on.
As we increase the
confidence level, the second-order Expected Shortfall of a fixed portfolio tends to its worst-case
loss, and once the optimized loss distribution is tight enough for that limit to be reached the program
becomes a minimax optimization problem, so the level ceases to enter it. A larger universe gives the optimizer more
diversification and hence a tighter tail, which is why the saturation point falls as the universe grows.

\newpage

Out of sample in the top-$10$ universe it is significant at the five
shallowest levels and decays across them, from $3.41\%$ at level $60\%$ to $2.70\%$ at $77.5\%$; from
level $80\%$ on the interval covers zero and the point estimates scatter between $-0.74\%$ and $1.78\%$
with no trend. In the top-$50$ and top-$100$ universes the realized advantage is indistinguishable from
zero at every level, and its point estimate is negative at most of them, between $-0.93\%$ and $0.58\%$ at
top-$50$ and between $-0.73\%$ and $0.07\%$ at top-$100$. The two series therefore move in opposite
directions as the tail is made thinner: at top-$10$ the unrealized part of the advantage grows from
$0.59$ to $15.51$ percentage points, while the half-width of the realized interval widens from $1.36$ to
$5.65$ percentage points over the same range.

 
As discussed in \Cref{chap:empirical-analysis-and-baseline-results,chap:optimization-framework}, Expected Shortfall and second-order Expected Shortfall are coherent (strictly expectation bounded) risk measures rather than deviation 
measures. Unlike volatility, mean absolute deviation and semi-deviation, they are not invariant to translation, so
a portfolio return is scored favorably for a high mean even when its dispersion is unchanged. The objectives
therefore carry an implicit risk-return tradeoff that the three deviation measures do not. De-meaning the returns in a coherent (strictly expectation bounded) risk measure functional $\mathcal{R}$
\begin{equation*}
\mathcal{D}(X) = \mathcal{R}\big(X - \mathbb{E}[X]\big),
\end{equation*}
transforms it into a deviation measure. See \Cref{thm:demeaning} in
Appendix \ref{app:demeaning}. We perform the same gap analysis as in \Cref{sec:joint-in-sample-case} on the de-meaned Expected Shortfall and second-order Expected Shortfall optimization programs, and report the results in \Cref{fig:gap-demeaned}.

We see in \Cref{fig:gap-demeaned} that de-meaning the optimizers steepens the decline of the in-sample advantage without removing it. For Expected Shortfall
the in-sample gap falls from $3.66\%$ to $2.15\%$ across the three universes, against $3.95\%$ to
$3.49\%$ for the original tail measure, and for second-order Expected Shortfall from $7.54\%$ to $4.09\%$ against
$7.73\%$ to $5.81\%$. The drop in the gap across universes is $41.3\%$  as opposed to $11.6\%$ for Expected Shortfall and
$45.7\%$ as opposed to $24.8\%$ for second-order Expected Shortfall. This indicates that the implicit
risk-return tradeoff is one of the driving factors for the tail measures in-sample advantage as the
universe grows. With it removed, they begin to decay in the way the deviation measures do. They do not
become deviation measures in their behaviour, however. At top-$100$ the de-meaned Expected Shortfall gap
is still $2.15\%$ against, for example, $0.63\%$ for volatility, so the mean term accounts for part of the persistence
and the tail depth in \Cref{fig:gap-vs-level-es,fig:gap-vs-level-soes} seems to account for the rest.

The two families considered so far leave the gap analysis of \Cref{sec:joint-in-sample-case} intact
because their objective is the risk measure itself. Regularized programs instead minimize the risk plus a
penalty, and mean-bearing ones the risk minus a multiple of the estimated mean, with the penalty weights
and the risk aversion selected on the validation window and routinely differing between the joint and the
overlay arm. The joint program therefore no longer minimizes the quantity we score it on, and the
in-sample gap $g_t^{\text{IS}}$ is no longer guaranteed to be non-negative. Empirically it is violated on
$30.0\%$ of the regularized date-cells and $22.2\%$ of the mean-bearing ones, against $0.6\%$ at solver
tolerance for the unregularized programs. We therefore drop the in-sample side entirely and compare the
two programs on realized risk alone, evaluating each measure's own functional on the full out-of-sample
return series of the production backtests. This is a narrower question than the one
\Cref{sec:joint-in-sample-case} asks, but it is the one that remains available, and it is also the
quantity an investor actually receives.

We see in \Cref{fig:realized-risk-reduction} that both modifications weaken the advantage rather than
leave it unchanged. Unregularized, the joint program realizes significantly less risk than the overlay in
all five measures at top-$10$ and at top-$50$, and in three of the five at top-$100$. Regularized, that
falls to three of five at top-$10$ and one of five at top-$50$; with a mean term, to three of four at
top-$10$ and one of four at top-$50$, and none at top-$100$. The reduction that survives is also smaller
where it survives at all, for example $0.84\%$ against $3.08\%$ for volatility at top-$10$. Both
modifications do what they are intended to do, which is to constrain how far the optimizer may follow the
estimation window, and both consequently remove part of the advantage joint optimization had over the
overlay. That advantage was in large part the freedom to fit the currency block to the same window.

The mean term does more than weaken it. For semi-deviation the realized risk of the joint program is
significantly \emph{higher} than the overlay's at top-$50$, $-1.62\%$ with interval
$[-3.10, -0.21]$, and at top-$100$, $-2.67\%$ with interval $[-4.44, -0.98]$. Volatility and mean
absolute deviation turn negative over the same range without the intervals clearing zero. The three
deviation measures are translation invariant, so a mean term added to their objective is not part of the
risk being measured but a separate estimated quantity competing with it, and the joint program has
$14$ more coefficients with which to fit it. This is the same mechanism \Cref{fig:gap-demeaned} isolates
from the other direction: there, removing the mean from a tail objective made its in-sample advantage
decay like a deviation measure's, and here, adding one to a deviation objective is enough to turn the
realized advantage negative.


\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion-joint-vs-sequential}

Even though joint optimization does not provide a statistical benefit in terms of returns, it has an advantage over 
sequential optimization by construction when it comes to minimizing the targeted risk measure. When the risk measures are
deviation measures, the in-sample benefit from joint optimization falls with an increasing universe size, while it stays stable for the 
two considered tail measures. Out-of-sample, the advantage falls with universe size for all five of the considered risk measures. 
Transforming the tail measures into deviation measures by de-meaning them results in a drop in the in-sample advantage as the 
universe size increases. This leads us to conclude that the implicit risk-return tradeoff is one of the drivers of the in-sample
advantage of joint optimization, and that it disappears out of sample due to estimation error. When we investigate the 
tail behavior of the expected shortfall optimizers, the gap between the in-sample and out-of-sample advantage of 
joint optimization increases with the tail depth, which further supports that the 
in-sample advantage is driven by estimation error. Regularizing the programs or adding a mean term to the
objective removes the nesting argument and with it the in-sample side of the comparison, and measured on
realized risk alone both weaken the advantage further rather than restore it. With a mean term the joint
program realizes significantly more semi-deviation than the overlay in the two larger universes. In the
largest investable universe, the realized advantage is absent for most measures and for every measure
once a mean term is added. This leads us to conclude that joint optimization does provide a 
benefit when it comes to risk reduction, but only when the investable universe is small, or rather, when the 
number of assets is smaller relative to the number of currencies.
