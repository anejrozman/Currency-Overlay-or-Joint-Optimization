\chapter{Joint versus Sequential Optimization}\label{chap:joint-vs-sequential}

In this chapter, we compare the performance of joint and sequential overlay optimization approaches for international equity portfolios. We begin by establishing that the joint program's in-sample advantage
holds by construction, since the overlay's solution is feasible for it, defining the in-sample and realized gaps
between the two optimizer programs, with the decomposition that links them, and measuring both across the five risk
measures and three investment universes. The
results separate the risk measures into the same two groups introduced in
Chapter~\ref{chap:optimization-framework}, which we then analyze separately. In Section~\ref{sec:mechanism-level} we focus on
the tail measures, perform the gap analysis across confidence levels to vary the amount of data used to estimate the risk, and transform the objectives to remove the implicit risk-return
tradeoff they have. In
Section~\ref{sec:mechanism-measures} we focus on the deviation measures, which use the whole estimation window and
carry no mean term. Finally, in Section~\ref{sec:reg-and-rr-formulation} we repeat the analysis for the regularized versions of the
optimizers and the
formulations with an added mean term.

\section{Advantage of Joint Optimization for Risk Minimization}\label{sec:joint-in-sample-case}

In-sample, joint optimization provides a benefit over sequential optimization by construction when it comes to minimizing 
the target risk measure. We write 
the weight vector as $\bm{\theta} = (\bm{x}, \bm{\uppsi})$ and let $\Theta$ denote the 
feasible set defined by the budget and leverage constraints of \Cref{chap:optimization-framework}. 
For a rebalancing period $t \in \{1, \dots, T\}$, let $f_t^{\text{IS}}$ be the in-sample risk on the 
estimation window and $f_t^{\text{OOS}}$ the realized risk on the next holding period. Let $\hat{\bm{\theta}}_t$ denote
the weight vector an 
optimizer returns on the estimation window, and $f_t^{\text{OOS}}(\hat{\bm{\theta}}_t)$ the risk these weight vectors realize on the next holding period. The two optimization programs are therefore written as
\begin{equation*}
\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{J,t} = \argmin_{\bm{\theta} \in \Theta} f_t^{\text{IS}}(\bm{\theta})
\quad \text{and} \quad
\hat{\bm{x}}_{O,t} = \argmin_{\bm{x}} f_{A,t}^{\text{IS}}(\bm{x}),
\quad
\hat{\bm{\uppsi}}_{O,t} = \argmin_{\bm{\uppsi}} f_t^{\text{IS}}(\hat{\bm{x}}_{O,t}, \bm{\uppsi}),
\end{equation*}
where $f_{A,t}^{\text{IS}}$ is the in-sample asset-only risk, which ignores the risk terms related to the currency overlay, and $\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{i,t} = (\hat{\bm{x}}_{i,t}, \hat{\bm{\uppsi}}_{i,t})$, $i\in\{J, O\}$. 
The subscript $J$ denotes the joint and $O$ the sequential overlay optimizers. 
The overlay program is a restricted minimizer. It first minimizes over $\bm{x}$ against a different objective, 
then over $\bm{\uppsi}$ with $\hat{\bm{x}}_{O,t}$ held fixed. 

Its output satisfies the same constraints, so $\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{O,t} \in \Theta$, and its second step is a partial minimization of the same $f_t^{\text{IS}}$. Hence
\begin{equation*}
f_t^{\text{IS}}(\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{J,t})
= \min_{\bm{x}, \bm{\uppsi}} f_t^{\text{IS}}(\bm{x}, \bm{\uppsi})
\;\leq\; \min_{\bm{\uppsi}} f_t^{\text{IS}}(\hat{\bm{x}}_{O,t}, \bm{\uppsi})
= f_t^{\text{IS}}(\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{O,t}).
\end{equation*}
We define the in-sample gap between the two optimizers as 
\begin{equation}
g_t^{\text{IS}} \coloneqq f_t^{\text{IS}}(\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{O,t}) - f_t^{\text{IS}}(\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{J,t}) \;\geq\; 0 .
\label{eq:gap-in-sample}
\end{equation}
The in-sample gap is non-negative and measures the advantage of joint optimization over sequential 
optimization in the estimation window. Naturally, the question we are interested in is whether this advantage is 
realized out of sample. 

We define the out-of-sample error of a fitted $\hat{\bm{\theta}}_t$ as
\begin{equation*}
\epsilon_t(\hat{\bm{\theta}}_t) \coloneqq f_t^{\text{OOS}}(\hat{\bm{\theta}}_t) - f_t^{\text{IS}}(\hat{\bm{\theta}}_t),
\end{equation*}
the amount by which realized risk in the holding quarter exceeds the risk the optimizer achieved in 
sample.\footnote{This quantity is known in the statistical learning literature as the optimism of the 
fitted weights \cite[\S7.4]{HastieTibshiraniFriedman2009}, here evaluated on a single holding quarter 
rather than in expectation over estimation windows.} 
Then the out-of-sample gap is written as
\begin{align}
g_t^{\text{OOS}} &\coloneqq f_t^{\text{OOS}}(\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{O,t}) - f_t^{\text{OOS}}(\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{J,t}) \nonumber \\
        &= \underbrace{\big[f_t^{\text{IS}}(\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{O,t}) - f_t^{\text{IS}}(\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{J,t})\big]}_{g_t^{\text{IS}} \,\geq\, 0}
\;-\; \underbrace{\big[\epsilon_t(\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{J,t}) - \epsilon_t(\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{O,t})\big]}_{\Delta\epsilon_t}.
\label{eq:gap-decomposition}
\end{align}

The out-of-sample error $\Delta\epsilon$ can be either positive or negative. The literature on estimation error in
portfolio optimization suggests it should typically be positive, since the joint program selects over a
larger space and absorbs more noise in doing so (Michaud, 1989; Kan and Zhou, 2007), though a restriction
can also reduce estimation error rather than merely constrain the optimizer (Jagannathan and Ma, 2003).
The magnitude is measure-dependent. Tail measure estimates (in our case Expected Shortfall and Second-Order Expected Shortfall) are determined by a smaller fraction of
tail scenarios, than, for example, variance, so the out-of-sample error absorbed by the optimizer should be larger and more volatile at comparable sample sizes, and we
expect the $\Delta\epsilon$ estimates to be noisier for such measures.

We perform the following gap analysis to investigate the out-of-sample performance of the joint and sequential overlay programs with respect to the risk measure they are minimizing, therefore we initially consider the unregularized formulation from \Cref{chap:optimization-framework}. At each of the $T = 91$ quarterly rebalancing dates the joint and the sequential overlay optimizers, for a given risk measure, are solved on the same ten-year estimation window, and under the same budget and leverage constraints from \Cref{chap:empirical-analysis-and-baseline-results}. The resulting weights are then held fixed and scored twice, once on the estimation window itself and once on the following holding quarter, which gives $f_t^{\text{IS}}$ and $f_t^{\text{OOS}}$ for each of the two programs. Both gaps are reported relative to the risk of the overlay optimizer,
\begin{equation}
\tilde g_t^{\text{IS}} \coloneqq \frac{g_t^{\text{IS}}}{f_t^{\text{IS}}(\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{O,t})}
\qquad \text{and} \qquad
\tilde g_t^{\text{OOS}} \coloneqq \frac{g_t^{\text{OOS}}}{f_t^{\text{OOS}}(\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{O,t})},
\label{eq:gap-relative}
\end{equation}
so they are to be interpreted as the risk reduction achieved by the joint optimizer over the overlay, expressed as a percentage of the latter's risk. For each risk measure and universe size we report the mean of the risk reduction across the rebalancing dates together with a circular block bootstrap confidence interval at the level 95\%, using $10{,}000$ resamples and a block length chosen by the Politis--White rule \cite{PolitisWhite2004, PattonPolitisWhite2009}.

We see in \Cref{fig:gap-measures} that the in-sample advantage of joint optimization is positive for all risk measures across all universe sizes, and that it is largest for the two considered tail measures. The out-of-sample gap is smaller than the in-sample gap for almost  all risk measures and universe sizes, the only exception being for the variance and semi-variance measures at the smallest universe size. Out-of-sample we see a negative dependence on the size of the investable universe. At the top-$10$ universe the realized out-of-sample advantage of joint optimization is significant for four of the five risk measures considered, between $2.17\%$ and $3.67\%$, with second-order Expected Shortfall being the least significant at $1.78\%$. At top-$50$, the advantage drops to zero for second-order Expected Shortfall, and remains around $1\%$ for the other measures, and at top-$100$ the advantage for Expected Shortfall drops below zero while the remaining measures stay below $1\%$. The in-sample series separates the two groups of risk measures the same way as in \Cref{chap:empirical-analysis-and-baseline-results}, that is, whether they are deviation or tail measures. The in-sample advantage falls as the universe grows, from $2.45\%$ to $0.63\%$ for volatility, from $2.67\%$ to $1.26\%$ for mean absolute deviation and from $3.08\%$ to $1.37\%$ for semi-deviation, whereas for the two tail measures it remains larger and more stable, from $3.95\%$ to $3.49\%$ for Expected Shortfall and from $7.73\%$ to $5.81\%$ for second-order Expected Shortfall. For the deviation measures the two series fall together, from $2.45\%$ to $0.63\%$ in sample against $3.25\%$ to $0.42\%$ out of sample for volatility and similarly for the other two, so the advantage these measures show in sample is not inflated in the way the tail measures are. It simply shrinks as the investable universe grows.

The first thing we notice in \Cref{fig:delta-epsilon} is that the bootstrapped confidence intervals for
the two tail measures are much wider than those for the three deviation measures, averaging $4.37$
against $1.41$ percentage points.
This is expected, since the tail measures are estimated on a smaller fraction of the sample and
their out-of-sample error is therefore more volatile. The second thing is the size of the unrealized
advantage itself, which is between $1.17$ and $8.50$ percentage points for the tail measures while the
three deviation measures are between $-0.81$ and $1.16$ percentage points. Read together with the in-sample
series of \Cref{fig:gap-measures}, which for the tail measures stays between $3.49\%$ and $8.14\%$ at
every universe size, this indicates that the tail measures hold on to a large in-sample
advantage that is not realized out-of-sample, whereas for the deviation measures the advantage that is possible in-sample is realized but drops with universe size. The two groups therefore behave in different ways, and we investigate them
separately in \Cref{sec:mechanism-level} and \Cref{sec:mechanism-measures}.

  We see in \Cref{fig:gap-vs-level-es} that the in-sample advantage of joint optimization for minimizing Expected Shortfall grows with the confidence level and the growth is more pronounced in the smaller universe. The out-of-sample advantage is much smaller and does not rise with the confidence level at any universe size. In the small top-10 universe, the in-sample advantage grows from $3.11\%$ at level $60\%$ to $10.59\%$ at level $97.5\%$, while the realized advantage stays in the $1.30-2.92\%$ range. In the larger universes the in-sample advantage grows at a lower rate, going from $3.23\%$ to $7.39\%$ at top-50 and from $3.86\%$ to $5.78\%$ at top-100, while the realized advantage never exceeds $1.32\%$ and $0.09\%$ respectively. The in-sample advantage of joint optimization is therefore inflated by estimation error, which grows with the confidence level and the tail depth. The out-of-sample advantage is small and decays with universe size, irrespective of the confidence level.

As discussed in \Cref{chap:empirical-analysis-and-baseline-results,chap:optimization-framework}, Expected Shortfall and its second-order variant are coherent risk measures rather than deviation 
measures. Unlike volatility, mean absolute deviation and semi-deviation, they are not invariant to translation, so
a portfolio return is scored favorably for a high mean even when its dispersion is unchanged. The objectives
therefore carry an implicit risk-return tradeoff that the three deviation measures do not. De-meaning the returns before applying the risk measure
\begin{equation*}
\mathcal{D}(X) = \mathcal{R}\big(X - \mathbb{E}[X]\big),
\end{equation*}
transforms them into deviation measures. See \Cref{thm:demeaning} in
Appendix \ref{app:demeaning}. We perform the same gap analysis as in \Cref{sec:joint-in-sample-case} on the de-meaned Expected Shortfall and second-order Expected Shortfall optimization programs, and report the results in \Cref{fig:gap-demeaned}.

We see in \Cref{fig:gap-demeaned} that de-meaning the optimizers steepens the decline of the in-sample advantage without removing it. For Expected Shortfall
the in-sample gap falls from $3.66\%$ to $2.15\%$ across the three universes, against $3.95\%$ to
$3.49\%$ for the original tail measure, and for second-order Expected Shortfall from $7.54\%$ to $4.09\%$ against
$7.73\%$ to $5.81\%$. The drop in the gap across universes is $41.3\%$  as opposed to $11.6\%$ for Expected Shortfall and
$45.7\%$ as opposed to $24.8\%$ for second-order Expected Shortfall. This indicates that the implicit
risk-return tradeoff is one of the driving factors for the tail measures in-sample advantage as the
universe grows. With it removed, they begin to decay in the way the deviation measures do. They do not
become deviation measures in their behaviour, however. At top-$100$ the de-meaned Expected Shortfall gap
is still $2.15\%$ against, for example, $0.63\%$ for volatility, so the mean term accounts for part of the persistence
and the tail depth in \Cref{fig:gap-vs-level-es,fig:gap-vs-level-soes} seems to account for the rest.

For the three considered deviation measures the advantage of joint optimization is smaller in-sample, 
and most of it survives out of sample. If we compare the two series from \Cref{fig:gap-measures} measure by
measure, the realized gap $\tilde g^{\text{OOS}}$ as a fraction of the in-sample gap $\tilde g^{\text{IS}}$
is $133\%$, $70\%$ and $67\%$ for volatility across the top-$10$, top-$50$ and top-$100$ universes,
$81\%$, $35\%$ and $59\%$ for mean absolute deviation, and $119\%$, $71\%$ and $42\%$ for semi-deviation.
At the smallest universe volatility and semi-deviation realize more than the entire in-sample gap, so the
mean of $\Delta\epsilon_t$ is negative.
The tail measures realize less. The same ratio is $70\%$, $36\%$ and $-19\%$ for Expected Shortfall
and $23\%$, $-4\%$ and $0\%$ for second-order Expected Shortfall. 

Volatility,
mean absolute deviation and semi-deviation are smooth functionals of the return distribution to which every
observation in the ten-year window contributes, so the objective, and with it the gap between the two
programs, is estimated with higher accuracy and carries over to the next quarter. Expected Shortfall and its
second-order variant are determined by a small fraction of the
observations at the confidence levels, so the same gap is a far noisier
quantity. \Cref{fig:gap-vs-level-es,fig:gap-vs-level-soes} make this explicit, as the tail is made thinner the in-sample gap
grows while the realized gap stays flat or decays. Additinaly, the absence of an implicit risk-return tradeoff.
The deviation measures are translation invariant, so the joint program cannot manufacture an in-sample
advantage by tilting the currency leg towards a high realized mean, whereas the tail measures can, and
\Cref{fig:gap-demeaned} shows that removing the mean term accounts for part of their persistence across
universes. Taken together, the deviation measures are left with a present but small advantage that shrinks both in-sample and out-of-sample
as the asset universe grows.

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion-joint-vs-sequential}

Even though joint optimization does not provide a statistical benefit in terms of returns, it has an advantage over 
sequential optimization by construction when it comes to minimizing the targeted risk measure. When the risk measures are
deviation measures, the in-sample benefit from joint optimization falls with an increasing universe size, while it stays stable for the 
two considered tail measures. Out-of-sample, the advantage falls with universe size for all five of the considered risk measures. 
Transforming the tail measures into deviation measures by de-meaning them results in a drop in the in-sample advantage as the 
universe size increases. This leads us to conclude that the implicit risk-return tradeoff is one of the drivers of the in-sample
advantage of joint optimization, and that it disappears out of sample due to estimation error. When we investigate the 
tail behavior of the expected shortfall optimizers, the gap between the in-sample and out-of-sample advantage of 
joint optimization increases with the tail depth, which further supports that the 
in-sample advantage is driven by estimation error. Neither regularization nor the addition of a mean term to 
the objective function changes this. In the larger investable universes, we observe no  
benefit from joint optimization out of sample. This leads us to conclude that joint optimization does provide a 
benefit when it comes to risk reduction, but only when the investable universe is small, or rather, when the 
number of assets is smaller relative to the number of currencies.
