\chapter{Joint versus Sequential Optimization}\label{chap:joint-vs-sequential}

\Cref{chap:backtest-analysis} shows that the joint and overlay programs achieve similar returns, and that the most
consistent difference between them is the lower realized volatility of the joint optimizers. In this chapter, we
compare how well the two programs minimize their target risk measure, in sample and out of sample. In
\Cref{sec:joint-in-sample-case} we show why the joint program has an in-sample advantage over the overlay program by
construction, define the in-sample and out-of-sample gaps between the two programs, and estimate them for the five risk
measures and three universe sizes. In \Cref{sec:mechanism-level} we study the tail measures in more detail. We repeat
the gap analysis across confidence levels in \Cref{sec:mechanism-level-analysis} and for the de-meaned tail measures in
\Cref{sec:mechanism-level-demeaning}. In \Cref{sec:mechanism-measures} we analyze the deviation measures and compare
them with the tail measures in terms of how much of the in-sample advantage is realized out of sample.

\section{Advantage of Joint Optimization for Risk Minimization}\label{sec:joint-in-sample-case}

In sample, joint optimization provides a benefit over sequential optimization by construction when it comes to minimizing 
the target risk measure. We write 
the weight vector as $\bm{\theta} = (\bm{x}, \bm{\psi})$ and let $\Theta = \mathcal{X} \times \R^M$ denote the 
feasible set defined by the budget and leverage constraints as in \Cref{chap:optimization-framework}.
For a rebalancing period $t \in \{1, \dots, T\}$, let $\rho_t^{\text{IS}}$ be the in-sample risk over the 
lookback window and $\rho_t^{\text{OOS}}$ the out-of-sample realized risk over the next holding period. Let $\hat{\bm{\theta}}_t$ denote
the weight vector an 
optimizer returns on the lookback window, so that $\rho_t^{\text{IS}}(\hat{\bm{\theta}}_t)$ and $\rho_t^{\text{OOS}}(\hat{\bm{\theta}}_t)$ are the in-sample and out-of-sample risks it realizes. The two optimization programs are written as
\begin{equation*}
\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{J,t} \in \argmin_{\bm{\theta} \in \Theta} \rho_t^{\text{IS}}(\bm{\theta}),
\end{equation*}
and
\begin{equation*}
\hat{\bm{x}}_{O,t} \in \argmin_{\bm{x} \in \mathcal{X}} \rho_{A,t}^{\text{IS}}(\bm{x}),
\quad
\hat{\bm{\psi}}_{O,t} \in \argmin_{\bm{\psi} \in \R^M} \rho_t^{\text{IS}}(\hat{\bm{x}}_{O,t}, \bm{\psi}),
\end{equation*}
where $\rho_{A,t}^{\text{IS}}(\bm{x}) \coloneqq \rho_t^{\text{IS}}(\bm{x}, \bm{0})$ is the in-sample risk of the fully hedged asset portfolio, and $\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{i,t} = (\hat{\bm{x}}_{i,t}, \hat{\bm{\psi}}_{i,t})$, $i\in\{J, O\}$. 
The subscript $J$ denotes the joint optimizer and $O$ the sequential overlay optimizer.
The overlay optimizer is a restricted minimizer. It first minimizes over $\bm{x}$ against a different objective, 
then over $\bm{\psi}$ with $\hat{\bm{x}}_{O,t}$ held fixed. 

The overlay optimizer's output satisfies the joint program's constraints, so $\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{O,t} \in \Theta$, and its second step is a partial minimization of the same $\rho_t^{\text{IS}}$. Hence
\begin{equation*}
\rho_t^{\text{IS}}(\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{J,t})
= \min_{\bm{\theta} \in \Theta} \rho_t^{\text{IS}}(\bm{\theta})
\;\leq\; \min_{\bm{\psi} \in \R^M} \rho_t^{\text{IS}}(\hat{\bm{x}}_{O,t}, \bm{\psi})
= \rho_t^{\text{IS}}(\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{O,t}).
\end{equation*}
This inequality holds for exact minimizers. In practice, both programs are solved numerically, so it holds only up to
the convergence tolerance of the solver, and we assume that the solver converges to a solution within a sufficiently
small numerical tolerance. The solver configuration is given in \Cref{sec:numerical-implementation}.
We define the in-sample gap between the two optimizers as 
\begin{equation*}
g_t^{\text{IS}} \coloneqq \rho_t^{\text{IS}}(\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{O,t}) - \rho_t^{\text{IS}}(\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{J,t}) \;\geq\; 0 .
\end{equation*}
The in-sample gap is non-negative and measures the advantage of joint optimization over sequential 
optimization in the lookback window. Naturally, the question we are interested in is whether this advantage is 
realized out of sample. 

We define the out-of-sample error of a fitted $\hat{\bm{\theta}}_t$ as
\begin{equation*}
\epsilon_t(\hat{\bm{\theta}}_t) \coloneqq \rho_t^{\text{OOS}}(\hat{\bm{\theta}}_t) - \rho_t^{\text{IS}}(\hat{\bm{\theta}}_t),
\end{equation*}
the amount by which realized risk in the holding quarter exceeds the risk the optimizer achieved in 
sample.\footnote{This quantity is the portfolio analogue of the optimism of the training error; see, for example, Section 7.4 in Hastie et al.~\cite{HastieTibshiraniFriedman2009}.} 
Then the out-of-sample gap is defined as
\begin{align*}
g_t^{\text{OOS}} &\coloneqq \rho_t^{\text{OOS}}(\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{O,t}) - \rho_t^{\text{OOS}}(\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{J,t}) \nonumber \\
        &= \underbrace{\big[\rho_t^{\text{IS}}(\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{O,t}) - \rho_t^{\text{IS}}(\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{J,t})\big]}_{g_t^{\text{IS}} \,\geq\, 0}
\;-\; \underbrace{\big[\epsilon_t(\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{J,t}) - \epsilon_t(\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{O,t})\big]}_{\Delta\epsilon_t}.
\end{align*}

The difference between the out-of-sample errors $\Delta\epsilon_t$ can be either positive or negative. For the variance, the in-sample risk of an optimized portfolio is known to understate its true risk, and the more
weights are fitted relative to the sample size, the larger this understatement becomes \cite{ElKaroui2010}. Since the joint program fits all of its weights to the same in-sample objective, we expect it to
adapt more closely to the noise in the lookback window than the overlay program, and hence $\Delta\epsilon_t$ to be typically positive. If $\Delta\epsilon_t$ exceeds $g_t^{\text{IS}}$, the overlay program could in some cases realize lower risk than the joint program out of sample. We also expect $\Delta\epsilon_t$ to be measure-dependent. Tail measures, in our case expected shortfall and second-order expected shortfall, are estimated from
a smaller fraction of the sample, whereas the deviation measures variance and mean absolute deviation use every observation, and semi-variance
 those below the mean. For a given sample size, we therefore expect  $\Delta\epsilon_t$ to be larger and more volatile for the tail measures. 

We perform the following gap analysis to investigate the in-sample and out-of-sample performance of the joint and 
overlay programs with respect to the risk measure they are minimizing. The in-sample gap is guaranteed to 
be non-negative only when each program minimizes the risk measure it is scored on, so we consider the 
unregularized formulation from \Cref{chap:optimization-framework}. At each of the $T = 91$ quarterly rebalancing 
dates, the joint and the overlay optimizers, for a given risk measure, are solved on the same ten-year 
lookback window and under the same budget and leverage constraints. The resulting weights are then held fixed 
and scored twice, once on the lookback window itself and once on the following holding quarter, which 
gives $\rho_t^{\text{IS}}$ and $\rho_t^{\text{OOS}}$ for each of the two programs. Both gaps are reported relative to the risk of the overlay optimizer
\vspace{0.3em}
\begin{equation*}
\tilde g_t^{\text{IS}} \coloneqq \frac{g_t^{\text{IS}}}{\rho_t^{\text{IS}}(\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{O,t})}
\qquad \text{and} \qquad
\tilde g_t^{\text{OOS}} \coloneqq \frac{g_t^{\text{OOS}}}{\rho_t^{\text{OOS}}(\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{O,t})},
\end{equation*}
so they are to be interpreted as the risk reduction achieved by the joint optimizer over the overlay optimizer, expressed as a percentage of the latter's risk. For each risk measure and universe size we report the mean of the relative gaps across the rebalancing dates together with a 95\% block bootstrap confidence interval based on $10{,}000$ resamples.

  \caption{In-sample and out-of-sample relative gaps between the unregularized joint and overlay optimizers, by risk measure and universe size. Each panel shows one risk measure, with the size of the investable universe on the horizontal axis and the risk reduction of the joint optimizer over the overlay optimizer on the vertical axis. Squares mark the mean across the $T = 91$ quarterly rebalancing dates, in blue for the in-sample gap $\tilde g_t^{\text{IS}}$ and in red for the out-of-sample gap $\tilde g_t^{\text{OOS}}$, and shaded areas mark the $95\%$ bootstrap confidence intervals.}
  \label{fig:gap-measures}
\end{figure}

We see in \Cref{fig:gap-measures} that the in-sample gap of joint optimization is positive for 
all risk measures across all universe sizes and that it is largest for the two considered tail measures. The out-of-sample gap is 
smaller than the in-sample gap for almost  all risk measures and universe sizes, the only exception being the variance and 
semi-variance measures in the small universe. Out of sample, we see a negative dependence on the size of the investable 
universe. In the small universe the realized out-of-sample advantage of joint optimization is in the range between $1.78\%$ and $3.67\%$, with second-order expected shortfall having the smallest benefit and semi-variance having the largest. In the medium universe, the 
advantage drops to zero for second-order expected shortfall and remains around $1\%$ for the other measures, and in the large universe the 
advantage for expected shortfall drops slightly below zero while the remaining measures stay below $1\%$.
The realized risk measures over the whole out-of-sample period in \Cref{app:additional-backtest-results} show the same
pattern, with the advantage of the joint optimizers falling as the universe grows. 


The in-sample series separates 
the risk measures into deviation and tail measures, as in \Cref{chap:backtest-analysis}. 
The in-sample advantage falls as the universe grows, from $2.45\%$ to $0.63\%$ for variance, from $2.67\%$ to $1.26\%$ for mean 
absolute deviation, and from $3.08\%$ to $1.37\%$ for semi-variance, whereas for the two tail measures it remains larger and more 
stable, from $3.95\%$ to $3.49\%$ for expected shortfall and from $7.73\%$ to $5.81\%$ for second-order expected shortfall. For the 
deviation measures the in-sample and out-of-sample series fall together, whereas for the tail measures, the out-of-sample series falls to zero, 
and the in-sample series remains large and positive. We visualize the difference between the two series in \Cref{fig:delta-epsilon}.

The first thing we notice in \Cref{fig:delta-epsilon} is that the bootstrapped confidence intervals for
the two tail measures are wider than those for the three deviation measures, averaging $4.37$
against $1.41$ percentage points.
This is expected, since the tail measures are estimated on a smaller fraction of the sample. The second thing is the size of the unrealized
advantage itself, which is between $1.17$ and $8.50$ percentage points for the two tail measures while the
three deviation measures are between $-0.81$ and $1.16$ percentage points. This, together with the in-sample
series of \Cref{fig:gap-measures} (which for the tail measures stays between $3.49\%$ and $8.14\%$ at
every universe size), shows that the tail measures have a larger in-sample
advantage coming from joint optimization that is not realized out of sample, whereas for the deviation measures the advantage that is achieved in sample is largely realized out of sample but drops with universe size. The two groups produce different results, and we analyze the mechanisms behind them
separately in \Cref{sec:mechanism-level,sec:mechanism-measures}.

\section{Tail Measures}\label{sec:mechanism-level}
\subsection{Level Analysis}\label{sec:mechanism-level-analysis}

To investigate the mechanism behind the in-sample advantage of joint optimization for the two tail measures, expected shortfall and second-order expected shortfall, 
we perform the same gap analysis as in \Cref{sec:joint-in-sample-case} across a range of confidence levels. 
We report the results in \Cref{fig:gap-vs-level-es,fig:gap-vs-level-soes}.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=1\textwidth]{../results/in_sample_vs_out_of_sample_es_at_diff_levels/gap_vs_level.pdf}
  \caption{In-sample and out-of-sample relative gaps between the unregularized joint and overlay expected shortfall optimizers, by confidence level and universe size. Each panel shows one universe size, with thirteen confidence levels from $60\%$ to $97.5\%$ on the horizontal axis. Squares and shaded areas denote the same quantities as in \Cref{fig:gap-measures}.}
  \label{fig:gap-vs-level-es}
\end{figure}

  We see in \Cref{fig:gap-vs-level-es} that the in-sample advantage of joint optimization for minimizing expected shortfall 
  grows with the confidence level and the growth is more pronounced in the small universe. 
  The out-of-sample advantage is much smaller and does not rise with the confidence level at any universe size. 
  In the small universe, the in-sample advantage rises from $3.11\%$ at confidence level $60\%$ to $10.59\%$ at level $97.5\%$, while the 
  out-of-sample advantage stays in the $1.30$ to $2.92\%$ range. In the larger universes the in-sample advantage grows at a 
  lower rate, going from $3.23\%$ to $7.39\%$ in the medium and from $3.86\%$ to $5.78\%$ in the large universe, while the 
  realized advantage never exceeds $1.32\%$ and $0.09\%$, respectively. 
  
  

Expected shortfall at confidence level $\beta$ is the average loss over the worst $1-\beta$ fraction of the lookback window, 
which is roughly $1{,}000$ observations at $60\%$ and $65$ at $97.5\%$. Which observations fall in the tail depends largely on the portfolio weights. 
The joint program chooses the asset and currency weights together, so it can select assets whose worst days the currency 
positions can offset. The overlay program fixes the asset weights of the fully hedged portfolio, 
and its currency step can adjust $M$ weights against a largely fixed set of the worst-performing days. As we increase the confidence level, the number of observations in the tail shrinks, so the joint program's currency weights can cover a larger share of the worst-performing days, and the in-sample advantage grows. 
The growth is largest in the small universe, where the currency weights represent a larger
share of all the decision variables. In larger universes, the overlay program's asset step has more weights to diversify, so the joint program's advantage decreases.
Consistent with this, the unregularized overlay optimizers of mean absolute deviation and the two tail measures hold
almost no currency exposure in the medium and large universes (see \Cref{app:portfolio-sparsity}). 
Since the weights are fitted to specific worst-performing days of the lookback window that do not necessarily occur in the next period, the mechanism generalizes poorly out of sample. 


\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=1\textwidth]{../results/in_sample_vs_out_of_sample_soes_at_diff_levels/gap_vs_level.pdf}
  \caption{In-sample and out-of-sample relative gaps between the unregularized joint and overlay second-order expected shortfall optimizers, by confidence level and universe size. Squares and shaded areas denote the same quantities as in \Cref{fig:gap-vs-level-es}.}
  \label{fig:gap-vs-level-soes}
\end{figure}

In \Cref{fig:gap-vs-level-soes} we see the same pattern for second-order expected shortfall. The in-sample advantage of joint optimization grows with the confidence level and the growth is faster in the 
small universe. We also observe that above certain confidence levels, the in-sample advantage becomes constant. 
The out-of-sample advantage is much smaller and does not rise with the confidence level at any universe size. In the small universe, the in-sample advantage rises 
from $4.00\%$ at level $60\%$ to $16.28\%$ at level $92.5\%$ and from there remains constant. The out-of-sample advantage stays in the $-0.45$ to $3.88\%$ range. In the larger 
universes the in-sample advantage grows at a lower rate and becomes constant at lower confidence levels, going from $3.44\%$ to $8.52\%$ in the medium and from $3.83\%$ to $5.81\%$ in the
large universe, while the out-of-sample advantage fluctuates around zero. 

Second-order expected shortfall squares the tail observations, so more weight is placed on the most extreme observations. 
At level $\beta$ it therefore behaves like expected shortfall at a higher confidence level, which is why the in-sample 
advantage is larger at every confidence level and every universe size. As we increase the confidence level, the squared weighting 
concentrates on a smaller and more stable set of the worst-performing days, and above a certain level, raising the confidence level 
further no longer changes which observations dominate the objective, and the in-sample series becomes constant. In larger universes 
this happens at lower confidence levels, since the overlay program's asset step has more weights to fit those days. Out of sample, the same 
reasoning as for expected shortfall applies, and the realized advantage does not rise with the confidence level at any universe size 
and is only present in the small universe.
 
\subsection{De-meaning the Tail Measures}\label{sec:mechanism-level-demeaning}
As discussed in \Cref{sec:coherent-and-deviation-measures}, expected shortfall and second-order expected shortfall are coherent (strictly expectation bounded) risk measures rather than deviation 
measures. Unlike variance, mean absolute deviation, and semi-variance, they are not invariant to translation, so
a portfolio with a higher mean return is scored more favorably than one with a lower mean return, everything else being equal. The objectives
therefore carry an implicit risk-return tradeoff that the three deviation measures do not. We can transform a coherent strictly 
expectation bounded risk measure $\mathcal{R}$ into a deviation measure $\mathcal{D}$ via a simple de-meaning operation. More precisely

\begin{demeaningthmDthree}
Let $\mathcal{R}$ be a coherent strictly expectation bounded risk measure, and let $\mathcal{D}$ be the functional defined by 
\begin{equation*}
\mathcal{D}(X) \coloneqq \mathcal{R}(X - \mathbb{E}[X]).
\end{equation*}
Then $\mathcal{D}$ is a deviation measure.
\end{demeaningthmDthree}


The proof of \Cref{thm:demeaning} and further details can be found in
Appendix \ref{app:demeaning}. We perform the same gap analysis as in \Cref{sec:joint-in-sample-case} on the de-meaned expected shortfall and second-order expected shortfall optimization programs, and report the results in \Cref{fig:gap-demeaned}.
 
\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{../results/in_sample_vs_out_of_sample_demeaned/gap_vs_universe.pdf}
  \caption{In-sample and out-of-sample relative gaps between the de-meaned unregularized joint and overlay expected shortfall and second-order expected shortfall optimizers, by universe size. Squares and shaded areas denote the same quantities as in \Cref{fig:gap-measures}.}
  \label{fig:gap-demeaned}
\end{figure}

We see in \Cref{fig:gap-demeaned} that de-meaning the risk measures steepens the decline of the in-sample relative gaps. For expected shortfall
the in-sample gap falls from $3.66\%$ to $2.15\%$ across the three universes, against $3.95\%$ to
$3.49\%$ for the original tail measure, and for second-order expected shortfall from $7.54\%$ to $4.09\%$ against
$7.73\%$ to $5.81\%$. The drop in the gap across universes is $41.3\%$  as opposed to $11.6\%$ for expected shortfall and
$45.7\%$ as opposed to $24.8\%$ for second-order expected shortfall. This indicates that the implicit
risk-return tradeoff is one of the driving factors for the tail measures' in-sample advantage as the
universe grows. With it removed, they begin to decay in the way the deviation measures do. In the large universe the de-meaned expected shortfall gap
is still $2.15\%$ against, for example, $0.63\%$ for variance, so the mean term accounts for only part of the in-sample advantage.



\section{Deviation Measures}\label{sec:mechanism-measures}

For the three considered deviation measures the advantage of joint optimization is smaller in sample than for the tail measures, and most of it survives out of sample. 
If we compare the two series from \Cref{fig:gap-measures} measure by measure, the realized gap $\tilde g^{\text{OOS}}$ as a fraction of the in-sample gap $\tilde g^{\text{IS}}$ 
is $133\%$, $70\%$, and $67\%$ for variance across the small, medium, and large universes, $81\%$, $35\%$, and $59\%$ for mean absolute deviation, and $119\%$, $71\%$,
and $42\%$ for semi-variance. For the tail measures the same ratio is $70\%$, $36\%$, and $-19\%$ for expected shortfall and $23\%$, $-4\%$, and $0\%$ for second-order expected shortfall.

Variance, semi-variance, and mean absolute deviation are estimated from all
observations in the window (half for semi-variance),
so the objective is estimated more accurately and the out-of-sample gap follows
the in-sample gap more closely. Expected shortfall and its second-order variant are determined 
by a small fraction of the observations at the confidence levels considered, so the same gap is a noisier quantity. \Cref{fig:gap-vs-level-es,fig:gap-vs-level-soes} make this 
explicit. As the tail is made thinner, the in-sample gap grows while the realized gap stays flat or decays. The deviation measures are also translation invariant, so they carry 
no implicit risk-return tradeoff, and the joint program cannot produce an in-sample advantage by tilting the weights toward a high realized mean. The tail measures can, 
and \Cref{fig:gap-demeaned} shows that removing the mean term accounts for part of the in-sample advantage across universes.
So deviation measures are left with a small advantage that shrinks both in sample and out of sample as the investable universe grows.

\section{Summary}\label{sec:conclusion-joint-vs-sequential}

Even though joint optimization does not consistently outperform in terms of returns, it has an advantage over sequential optimization by construction when it comes to minimizing the targeted 
risk measure. In sample, the advantage for the deviation measures falls with universe size, while for the two considered tail measures it stays stable. Out of sample, the advantage falls with 
universe size for all five considered risk measures. 

The tail measures' in-sample advantage is driven by two mechanisms. The first is a tail-fitting mechanism, which lets the joint program align the currency weights with the specific worst-performing
days of the lookback window, and which does not generalize out of sample. The second is an implicit risk-return tradeoff, which lets the joint program produce an in-sample advantage by tilting the 
weights toward a high realized mean. Once the tail measures are de-meaned, their in-sample advantage begins to fall with universe size, in the same way as the 
deviation measures. Neither of the two mechanisms is available to the deviation measures, which is why their in-sample advantage was smaller to begin with and a larger fraction of it survives out of 
sample.

The out-of-sample advantage of joint optimization is largest in the small universe, where the currency weights represent a larger share of the decision variables. In larger universes the overlay program's asset step has more weights to fit the objective on its own, and the joint program's advantage falls to 1\% in the medium universe and to below 1\% in the large universe, where it is no longer distinguishable from zero.  Joint optimization is therefore beneficial for risk reduction when the number of assets is small relative to the number of currencies. These results agree with the findings in \Cref{chap:backtest-analysis}, where the joint program's lower realized volatility was largest in the small universe.
