\chapter{Literature Review}\label{chap:literature-review}

International diversification exposes an investor to currency risk, and early studies ask whether this risk should be
hedged at all. Solnik \cite{Solnik1974} shows in an equilibrium model of the international capital market that, under
specific conditions, it is optimal for investors to fully hedge their currency exposure. Perold and Schulman
\cite{PeroldSchulman1988} argue that US investors should fully hedge their foreign holdings, since hedging substantially
reduces risk while leaving the long-run expected return unchanged. Full hedging, however, treats the currency exposure
only as a risk to be removed and ignores that currency positions can also diversify the risk of the assets. 

The literature on currency overlays therefore studies how to choose the currency positions so that they improve the
risk-return profile of a given asset portfolio. Glen and Jorion \cite{GlenJorion1993} find that adding forward contracts
significantly improves the performance of internationally diversified portfolios that contain bonds, and Campbell et
al.~\cite{global_currency_hedging} show that the US dollar, the euro, and the Swiss franc tend to move against world
equity markets, which makes them attractive to risk-minimizing equity investors despite their low average returns.
More recent studies hedge dynamically. Boudoukh et al.~\cite{BoudoukhRichardsonThaparWang2019} split the currency
positions into a hedge component, which reduces the volatility of the equity portfolio, and an alpha component based on
value, momentum, and carry signals, and Opie and Riddiough \cite{OpieRiddiough2020} exploit a forecastable component of
currency factor returns. All of these strategies, however, take the asset portfolio as given. Van Binsbergen et
al.~\cite{VanBinsbergenBrandtKoijen2008} show that delegating the investment decisions for different asset classes to
separate managers can be costly for the investor, and the same argument applies to the separation of the asset and the
currency decisions in a currency overlay.

Jorion \cite{Jorion1994} shows that a currency overlay adds value to global equity and bond portfolios but improves them
less than an integrated approach that chooses the asset and the currency positions together. Subsequent studies develop
such integrated models further. Suzuki et al.~\cite{SuzukiKonnoMorjiri1997} include forward currency contracts in an
international portfolio optimization model, and Konno and Li \cite{KonnoLi1998, KonnoLi2000} select the individual stocks
and bonds of several countries in a single mean-variance or mean absolute deviation program instead of the usual
two-stage allocation, and hedge the currency risk with forward contracts. Topaloglou et
al.~\cite{TopaloglouVladimirouZenios2002} choose the asset weights and the currency hedge of each market together by
minimizing Expected Shortfall over simulated scenarios, and Mhiri and Prigent \cite{MhiriPrigent2010} consider the
higher moments of the returns in international portfolio optimization. Burkhardt and Ulrych
\cite{SparseAndStableInternationalPortfolioOptimization} point out that the evidence for joint optimization was mostly
established in sample, where the joint program can only improve on the overlay program, whereas out of sample the
advantage can be lost to overfitting. They add regularization to joint and sequential mean-variance optimization and
find that the regularized joint programs achieve on average higher out-of-sample Sharpe ratios than the overlay
programs. Lucescu and Ulrych \cite{RegularizedMultiCurrencyExpectedShortfallPortfolios} apply $L^1$ and $L^2$ penalties
to Expected Shortfall optimization and find that joint optimization performs better in the smaller of their two
investable universes. 

Whatever the risk measure, the optimized portfolio depends on estimates from a finite sample. Merton \cite{Merton1980}
shows that the precision of an estimated expected return depends only on the length of the sample period, so that
expected returns are much harder to estimate than variances. Michaud \cite{Michaud1989} calls mean-variance optimizers
estimation-error maximizers, since they overweight the assets with large estimated expected returns and small estimated
variances, which are the most likely to be estimated with large errors, and DeMiguel et al.~\cite{DeMiguelGarlappiUppal2009}
find that none of the optimized portfolios they consider consistently outperforms the equally weighted portfolio out of
sample. Minimizing risk alone, as Lucescu and Ulrych do in their backtests, avoids the estimation of expected returns, but
 it does not avoid the estimation error in
the risk measure itself. This error grows with the number of assets relative to the number of observations, and Kondor
et al.~\cite{kondor2007noise} show that close to a critical value of this ratio the optimized weights fluctuate so
strongly from sample to sample that the optimization loses its meaning. The joint program uses the dependence between
the individual assets and the currencies, whereas the overlay program takes the currencies into account only through
their dependence on the return of the fixed asset portfolio, which Burkhardt and Ulrych regard as a form of
dimensionality reduction. The size of the investable universe may therefore affect the comparison of the two
approaches.

The estimation error also depends on the risk measure one chooses to optimize. Markowitz
\cite{Markowitz1952} measures the risk of a portfolio by the variance of its return, which penalizes returns above the
mean as much as returns below it, and in his later book \cite{Markowitz1959} he proposes the semi-variance as a downside
alternative. Konno and Yamazaki
\cite{KonnoYamazaki1991} propose the mean absolute deviation, which turns portfolio optimization into a linear program.
Artzner et al.~\cite{CoherentMeasuresOfRisk} define coherent risk measures, a class to which Expected Shortfall belongs
\cite{acerbi2002coherenceexpectedshortfall}, and Rockafellar and Uryasev \cite{RockafellarUryasev2000,
RockafellarUryasev2002} show that Expected Shortfall can be minimized by a linear program. Krokhmal \cite{Krokhmal2007}
extends Expected Shortfall to higher-moment coherent risk measures, which put more weight on the largest losses, and
Rockafellar et al.~\cite{RockafellarUryasevZabarankin2006} introduce deviation measures, which quantify the dispersion of
a return rather than its losses. Since these measures depend on different parts of the return distribution, they are
affected differently by estimation error, and the measures of tail risk, which depend only on the largest losses, are
particularly affected. Kondor et al.~\cite{kondor2007noise} show that portfolios optimized under Expected Shortfall are
more sensitive to estimation error than those optimized under the variance, and Ciliberti et
al.~\cite{ciliberti2007feasibility} show that, without bounds on the weights, the optimization has no finite solution in
some samples, and that for large portfolios a solution ceases to exist once the ratio of the number of assets to the
number of observations exceeds a critical value. Kondor and Varga-Haszonits \cite{KondorVargaHaszonits2010} show that
this instability affects all coherent risk measures. Each of the multi-currency studies above relies on a single risk measure, which leaves
open how much the comparison of the two approaches depends on that choice.

One approach to reducing the estimation error is to shrink the estimates. James and Stein \cite{JamesStein1961} show
that, for three or more independent normal means, shrinking the sample means toward zero lowers their total expected
squared error, and Ledoit and Wolf \cite{LedoitWolf2003, LedoitWolf2004} shrink the sample covariance matrix toward a
structured target. Jagannathan and Ma \cite{JagannathanMa2003} show that a no-short-sale constraint is equivalent to
shrinking the large entries of the sample covariance matrix and reduces the risk of the optimized portfolio. Another
approach is to regularize the weights directly. DeMiguel et al.~\cite{DeMiguelGarlappiNogalesUppal2009} bound the norm
of the weights of minimum-variance portfolios, Brodie et al.~\cite{BrodieDaubechiesDeMolGiannoneLoris2009} add an $L^1$
penalty, which yields sparse and stable portfolios, and Li \cite{Li2015} combines the $L^1$ and the $L^2$ penalty. These
methods are developed for portfolios of assets in a single currency. Burkhardt and Ulrych
\cite{SparseAndStableInternationalPortfolioOptimization} carry them over to the mean-variance optimization of assets and
currencies, and Lucescu and Ulrych \cite{RegularizedMultiCurrencyExpectedShortfallPortfolios} to Expected Shortfall.

In this thesis we extend this literature in four directions. First, we compare joint and sequential optimization under
five risk measures, the variance, the semi-variance, the mean absolute deviation, Expected Shortfall, and second-order
Expected Shortfall, in the same out-of-sample framework with and without regularization. Second, we study how the
comparison depends on the size of the investable universe. Third,
we add a mean term to the programs of all five risk measures, with expected asset returns that are pooled by economy and
shrunk and expected currency returns that are derived from the forward premia. Finally, we distinguish the advantage that
joint optimization has in sample from what is realized out of sample.
