\chapter{Optimization Framework} 
\label{chap:optimization-framework}

In this chapter, we formulate the portfolio optimization programs that we evaluate in
\Cref{chap:backtest-analysis,chap:joint-vs-sequential}. In \Cref{sec:risk-based-optimization} we state the risk-based
portfolio optimization problem for a single currency and introduce the five risk measures that we consider in the thesis. In \Cref{sec:returns} we derive the return of an international

programs of the sequential and the joint optimization approaches. Finally, in
\Cref{sec:mean-risk-portfolio-optimization} we add a mean term to these programs and specify the estimators 
of the expected asset returns and the expected excess currency returns.

\section{Risk-Based Portfolio Optimization}\label{sec:risk-based-optimization}

Portfolio optimization theory studies how an investor should allocate their wealth across a set of different risky assets. In one of the most 
influential works in finance, Markowitz \cite{Markowitz1952} proposes to choose among the assets by trading off the expected
portfolio return against its variance. This requires estimates of the expected asset returns, which are
less precise than estimates of variances and covariances. Merton \cite{Merton1980} shows that the precision
of an estimated expected return depends only on the length of the sample period and not on how often the returns are
observed within it, whereas the precision of an estimated variance increases with the observation frequency. Michaud
\cite{Michaud1989} describes mean-variance optimizers as estimation-error maximizers, since they overweight the assets
with large estimated expected returns and small estimated variances, which are the assets most likely to have large
estimation errors. DeMiguel et al.~\cite{DeMiguelGarlappiUppal2009} find that, out of sample, none of the optimized
portfolios they consider consistently outperforms the equally weighted portfolio, because the gain from optimal
diversification is more than offset by estimation error. Following Lucescu and Ulrych
\cite{RegularizedMultiCurrencyExpectedShortfallPortfolios}, we therefore first consider portfolios that minimize a
measure of risk alone and require no explicit estimates of expected returns. We consider the expected portfolio return later in
this chapter.

We initially formulate the portfolio optimization problem for an investor who holds $N\in\mathbb{N}$ assets at time $t\in\mathbb{N}_0$ denominated in a single currency and extend the setting
to multiple currencies in the next section. Let $\bm{x}_t \in \R^N$ denote the portfolio weights chosen at time $t$ and
held until $t+1$, and $\bm{R}_{t+1} \in \R^N$ the vector of simple asset returns over the period from $t$ to $t+1$. The
portfolio return at time $t+1$ is then $\bm{x}_t^\top \bm{R}_{t+1}$, and the weights satisfy the budget constraint
$\bm{x}_t^\top \bm{1}_N = 1$, where $\bm{1}_N \in \R^N$ denotes the vector of ones. A risk measure $\rho \ : \ \mathcal{L}^2\to\mathbb{R}$ assigns to the
random portfolio return a real number that quantifies its risk. We write 
$\rho$ as a function of the portfolio return $\bm{x}_t^\top \bm{R}_{t+1}$, so that the risk-based portfolio optimization problem is
\begin{equation} \label{eq:risk-based-problem}
    \bm{x}_t^* = \argmin_{\bm{x}_t} \rho\bigl( \bm{x}_t^\top \bm{R}_{t+1} \bigr), \quad \text{s.t. } \bm{x}_t^\top \bm{1}_N = 1.
\end{equation}
The risk measure is evaluated under the distribution of $\bm{R}_{t+1}$ conditional on the information available at time
$t$.

\subsection{Variance}\label{sec:risk-variance}

In the portfolio theory of Markowitz \cite{Markowitz1952}, the risk of a portfolio is measured by the variance of its
return. Let $\mathbb{E}_t[\cdot]$ denote the expectation conditional on the information available at time $t$, and let
$\bm{\mu}_t = \mathbb{E}_t[\bm{R}_{t+1}] \in \R^N$ and
$\bm{\Sigma}_t = \mathbb{E}_t\bigl[ (\bm{R}_{t+1} - \bm{\mu}_t)(\bm{R}_{t+1} - \bm{\mu}_t)^\top \bigr] \in \R^{N \times N}$
denote the expected asset return and the conditional covariance matrix of the asset returns. The variance of the portfolio
return is
\begin{equation*}
    \text{Var}_t\bigl( \bm{x}_t^\top \bm{R}_{t+1} \bigr)
    = \mathbb{E}_t\bigl[ (\bm{x}_t^\top \bm{\mu}_t - \bm{x}_t^\top \bm{R}_{t+1})^2 \bigr]
    = \bm{x}_t^\top \bm{\Sigma}_t \bm{x}_t.
\end{equation*}
The variance thus depends on the distribution of the asset returns only through their covariance matrix.

With the variance as the risk measure, the objective of problem \eqref{eq:risk-based-problem} is the quadratic form
$\bm{x}_t^\top \bm{\Sigma}_t \bm{x}_t$. Since $\bm{\Sigma}_t$ is positive semi-definite, the objective is convex in the
weights, and the problem is a convex quadratic program, which standard solvers handle efficiently
\cite{boyd2004convex}. If $\bm{\Sigma}_t$ is positive definite, the first-order conditions of the Lagrangian further give
the unique solution in closed form
\begin{equation} \label{eq:var-closed-form-solution}
    \bm{x}_t^* = \frac{\bm{\Sigma}_t^{-1} \bm{1}_N}{\bm{1}_N^\top \bm{\Sigma}_t^{-1} \bm{1}_N},
\end{equation}
which is known as the global minimum-variance portfolio. The closed form is a particularity of the variance, since the
problems under the other risk measures we consider have no closed-form solution in general and must be solved
numerically. It no longer applies once we add the leverage constraint and the penalties later in this chapter, although
the problem remains a convex quadratic program. The closed form also shows that the weights depend on the covariance
matrix through its inverse, so that errors in an estimate of $\bm{\Sigma}_t$ carry over directly to the portfolio.

Since the conditional distribution of $\bm{R}_{t+1}$ is unknown, $\bm{\Sigma}_t$ has to be estimated. For the variance
and for the other risk measures later in the chapter, we use $q$ historical realizations $\bm{y}_1, \dots, \bm{y}_q \in \R^N$ of
$\bm{R}_{t+1}$. In the empirical analysis, these are the daily asset returns over a lookback window of the ten years
before time $t$, so that $q \approx 2{,}500$. We denote their sample mean
by $\bar{\bm{y}} = \frac{1}{q} \sum_{k=1}^q \bm{y}_k$.

The natural estimate of $\bm{\Sigma}_t$ is the sample covariance matrix of the realizations
\begin{equation*}
    \bar{\bm{\Sigma}}_t = \frac{1}{q} \sum_{k=1}^q (\bm{y}_k - \bar{\bm{y}})(\bm{y}_k - \bar{\bm{y}})^\top.
\end{equation*}
It has $N(N+1)/2$ distinct
entries, all estimated from the same $q$ observations, and when $N$ is large relative to $q$, its eigenvalues are more
dispersed than those of $\bm{\Sigma}_t$, with the smallest eigenvalues biased downward \cite{LedoitWolf2004}. Through
the inverse in the closed-form solution \eqref{eq:var-closed-form-solution}, the minimum-variance portfolio then concentrates on the combinations of assets whose
risk is most underestimated, and its in-sample variance understates its out-of-sample variance \cite{ElKaroui2010}.
When $N \ge q$, the sample covariance matrix is singular, and the closed form does not exist.

We therefore estimate $\bm{\Sigma}_t$ with the shrinkage estimator of Ledoit and Wolf \cite{LedoitWolf2003}, which
combines the sample covariance matrix with a structured target. The sample covariance matrix imposes no structure but
has a large estimation error when $N$ is large relative to $q$, while a structured target has few parameters and
therefore a small estimation error but is biased whenever its structure does not hold. A weighted average of the two
trades off the estimation error of the sample covariance matrix with the bias of the structured target. Ledoit and Wolf use the constant-correlation
target, which keeps the sample variances of the assets and replaces every pairwise correlation by the average sample
correlation.

To define the estimator, let $y_{k,i}$ denote the $i$-th entry of $\bm{y}_k$, $k \in \{1, \ldots, q\}$, $\bar{y}_i$ the $i$-th entry of
$\bar{\bm{y}}$, and $\mathring{y}_{k,i} = y_{k,i} - \bar{y}_i$ the demeaned return of asset $i$ in realization $k$. We
suppress the dependence on $t$ of the scalar quantities that follow. The sample covariance matrix $\bar{\bm{\Sigma}}_t$
has the entries
\begin{equation*}
    \bar{\sigma}_{ij} = \frac{1}{q} \sum_{k=1}^q \mathring{y}_{k,i}\, \mathring{y}_{k,j},
\end{equation*}
from which we obtain the sample correlations $r_{ij} = \bar{\sigma}_{ij} / \sqrt{\bar{\sigma}_{ii} \bar{\sigma}_{jj}}$
and their average $\bar{r} = \frac{2}{N(N-1)} \sum_{i<j} r_{ij}$. The constant-correlation target $\bm{T}_t$ has the
diagonal entries $\bar{\sigma}_{ii}$ and the off-diagonal entries $\bar{r} \sqrt{\bar{\sigma}_{ii} \bar{\sigma}_{jj}}$,
and the Ledoit--Wolf estimate is the convex combination
\begin{equation*}
    \hat{\bm{\Sigma}}_t = \hat{\delta}\, \bm{T}_t + (1 - \hat{\delta})\, \bar{\bm{\Sigma}}_t
\end{equation*}
with the shrinkage intensity $\hat{\delta} \in [0, 1]$.

Ledoit and Wolf choose the intensity that minimizes the expected squared Frobenius distance between
$\hat{\bm{\Sigma}}_t$ and $\bm{\Sigma}_t$, that is, the expected sum of the squared differences between their entries.
The optimal intensity is asymptotically proportional to $1/q$, and its estimate is
\begin{equation*}
    \hat{\delta} = \max\Bigl\{ 0, \min\Bigl\{ \frac{\hat{\pi} - \hat{\nu}}{q\, \hat{\omega}}, 1 \Bigr\} \Bigr\},
\end{equation*}
where
\begin{align*}
    \hat{\pi} &= \sum_{i,j} \hat{\pi}_{ij},
    \qquad \hat{\pi}_{ij} = \frac{1}{q} \sum_{k=1}^q \bigl( \mathring{y}_{k,i}\, \mathring{y}_{k,j} - \bar{\sigma}_{ij} \bigr)^2, \\
    \hat{\nu} &= \sum_{i} \hat{\pi}_{ii} + \bar{r} \sum_{i \neq j} \sqrt{\bar{\sigma}_{jj} / \bar{\sigma}_{ii}}\;
    \hat{\vartheta}_{ij}, 
    \qquad \hat{\vartheta}_{ij} = \frac{1}{q} \sum_{k=1}^q \bigl( \mathring{y}_{k,i}^2 - \bar{\sigma}_{ii} \bigr)
    \bigl( \mathring{y}_{k,i}\, \mathring{y}_{k,j} - \bar{\sigma}_{ij} \bigr), \\
    \hat{\omega} &= \sum_{i \neq j} \bigl( \bar{r} \sqrt{\bar{\sigma}_{ii} \bar{\sigma}_{jj}} - \bar{\sigma}_{ij} \bigr)^2.
\end{align*}
The quantity $\hat{\pi}$ estimates the sum of the asymptotic variances of the entries of $\sqrt{q}\,\bar{\bm{\Sigma}}_t$,
and $\hat{\nu}$ the sum of the asymptotic covariances between the entries of $\sqrt{q}\,\bm{T}_t$ and those of
$\sqrt{q}\,\bar{\bm{\Sigma}}_t$, which arise because both matrices are computed from the same realizations. The quantity
$\hat{\omega}$ is the squared Frobenius distance between $\bm{T}_t$ and $\bar{\bm{\Sigma}}_t$, which measures how far the
structure of the target is from the data. The intensity therefore increases with the estimation error of the sample
covariance matrix, decreases with the misspecification of the target, and tends to zero as $q$ grows, so that
$\hat{\bm{\Sigma}}_t$ approaches the sample covariance matrix when many observations are available. For the positive
average correlations typical of stock returns, the target is positive definite, and $\hat{\bm{\Sigma}}_t$ is then
positive definite whenever $\hat{\delta} > 0$, even when $N \ge q$.

The variance program replaces $\bm{\Sigma}_t$ by the Ledoit--Wolf estimate
\begin{equation*}
    \bm{x}_t^* = \argmin_{\bm{x}_t} \bm{x}_t^\top \hat{\bm{\Sigma}}_t \bm{x}_t, \quad \text{s.t. } \bm{x}_t^\top \bm{1}_N = 1,
\end{equation*}
and we write it in the standard form of a quadratic program in Appendix~\ref{app:mv_var_qp}.

One of the common criticisms of the variance is that it penalizes returns above the expected portfolio return as much as returns below it, although an
investor is typically concerned with underperformance rather than outperformance \cite{Markowitz1993}. This motivates
the semi-variance, which we consider next. 

\subsection{Semi-Variance}\label{sec:risk-semi-variance}

The semi-variance only considers the deviations of the portfolio return
below a threshold, so that returns above the threshold do not add to the risk of the portfolio. Markowitz et
al.~\cite{Markowitz1993} consider two thresholds, the expected portfolio return and a benchmark return, which may also be
a constant. Measured against a benchmark, the semi-variance captures the risk of underperforming the benchmark, while
measured against the expected return, it captures the dispersion of the portfolio return below its mean. We use the
expected portfolio return as the threshold. With $z^+ = \max\{z, 0\}$, the semi-variance of the portfolio return is
\begin{equation*}
    \text{SVar}_t\bigl( \bm{x}_t^\top \bm{R}_{t+1} \bigr)
    = \mathbb{E}_t\Bigl[ \bigl( (\bm{x}_t^\top \bm{\mu}_t - \bm{x}_t^\top \bm{R}_{t+1})^+ \bigr)^2 \Bigr].
\end{equation*}

As for the variance, the
conditional expectation has to be estimated. With the historical realizations $\bm{y}_1, \dots, \bm{y}_q$ and their
sample mean $\bar{\bm{y}}$ of \Cref{sec:risk-variance}, we replace $\bm{\mu}_t$ by $\bar{\bm{y}}$ and the conditional
expectation by the empirical average over the realizations, which gives the sample semi-variance
\begin{equation}\label{eq:sample-semi-variance}
    \frac{1}{q} \sum_{k=1}^q \bigl( (\bm{x}_t^\top \bar{\bm{y}} - \bm{x}_t^\top \bm{y}_k)^+ \bigr)^2
    = \bm{x}_t^\top \bm{\Sigma}^-(\bm{x}_t)\, \bm{x}_t,
\end{equation}
where
\begin{equation*}
    \bm{\Sigma}^-(\bm{x}_t) = \frac{1}{q} \sum_{k \,:\, \bm{x}_t^\top (\bm{y}_k - \bar{\bm{y}}) < 0}
    (\bm{y}_k - \bar{\bm{y}})(\bm{y}_k - \bar{\bm{y}})^\top
\end{equation*}
is the sample semi-covariance matrix of the realizations in which the portfolio return falls below its sample mean.

The sample semi-covariance matrix has the same form as the sample covariance matrix of \Cref{sec:risk-variance}, but it
only contains the realizations in which the portfolio underperforms its mean, and which realizations these are depends
on the weights. The semi-covariance matrix is therefore endogenous \cite{Estrada2008}, so unlike for the variance,
there is no fixed matrix that could be estimated before the optimization. For the same reason, the problem has no
closed-form solution, and the Ledoit--Wolf estimator of \Cref{sec:risk-variance} cannot be used. Estrada \cite{Estrada2008} proposes a heuristic semi-covariance
matrix that does not depend on the weights, but it only approximates the semi-variance of the portfolio. We therefore
minimize the sample semi-variance directly.

Each summand of the sample semi-variance \eqref{eq:sample-semi-variance} is only piecewise quadratic in the weights. Following Markowitz et al.~\cite{Markowitz1993}, we introduce for each realization $k$ an
auxiliary variable $u_k \ge 0$ subject to $u_k \ge \bm{x}_t^\top \bar{\bm{y}} - \bm{x}_t^\top \bm{y}_k$, and collect
them in $\bm{u} = [u_1, \dots, u_q]^\top$. Since the objective $\frac{1}{q} \sum_{k=1}^q u_k^2$ increases in every
$u_k \ge 0$, each $u_k$ equals $(\bm{x}_t^\top \bar{\bm{y}} - \bm{x}_t^\top \bm{y}_k)^+$ at the optimum, and the
semi-variance program is the quadratic program
\begin{equation*} 
\begin{aligned}
    \bm{x}_t^*, \bm{u}^* = \argmin_{\bm{x}_t,\, \bm{u}} \quad & \frac{1}{q} \sum_{k=1}^q u_k^2, \\
    \text{s.t.} \quad & \bm{x}_t^\top \bm{1}_N = 1, \\
    & \bm{x}_t^\top (\bm{y}_k - \bar{\bm{y}}) + u_k \ge 0, \quad u_k \ge 0, \quad k = 1, \dots, q.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation*}
Each realization adds one auxiliary variable and one linear constraint, so that the size of the program grows with the
number of realizations, whereas the variance program works with a covariance matrix whose size grows quadratically with
the number of assets. We write the program in the standard form of a quadratic program in
Appendix~\ref{app:mv_svar_qp}.

Like the variance, the semi-variance squares the deviations, so that a few large deviations below the mean can dominate
its estimate, and minimizing it requires a quadratic program. The mean absolute deviation, which we consider next,
measures the deviations linearly instead.

\subsection{Mean Absolute Deviation}\label{sec:risk-mad}

Konno and Yamazaki \cite{KonnoYamazaki1991} propose the mean absolute deviation as an alternative to the variance. It
measures the deviations of the portfolio return from its expected return by their absolute values instead of their
squares. Large deviations therefore have less
influence on the measure. The mean absolute deviation of the portfolio return is
\begin{equation*}
    \text{MAD}_t\bigl( \bm{x}_t^\top \bm{R}_{t+1} \bigr)
    = \mathbb{E}_t\bigl[ \lvert \bm{x}_t^\top \bm{\mu}_t - \bm{x}_t^\top \bm{R}_{t+1} \rvert \bigr].
\end{equation*}
The main motivation of Konno and Yamazaki is computational. Since the absolute value is piecewise linear, minimizing the
sample mean absolute deviation can be formulated as a linear program rather than a quadratic program, which makes it more efficient for larger problems, 
and setting up the problem requires no estimate of the covariance matrix.

As for the semi-variance, we replace $\bm{\mu}_t$ by the sample mean $\bar{\bm{y}}$ and the conditional expectation by
the empirical average over the realizations $\bm{y}_1, \dots, \bm{y}_q$ of \Cref{sec:risk-variance}, which gives the
sample mean absolute deviation $\frac{1}{q} \sum_{k=1}^q \lvert \bm{x}_t^\top \bar{\bm{y}} - \bm{x}_t^\top \bm{y}_k
\rvert$. Since the deviations $\bm{x}_t^\top \bar{\bm{y}} - \bm{x}_t^\top \bm{y}_k$ sum to zero over the sample, the sum
of their positive parts equals the sum of their negative parts, and the absolute value of each deviation is the sum of
the two. The sample mean absolute deviation is therefore twice the average shortfall of the portfolio return below
its sample mean \cite{Speranza1993, OgryczakRuszczynski1999}
\begin{equation*}
    \frac{1}{q} \sum_{k=1}^q \lvert \bm{x}_t^\top \bar{\bm{y}} - \bm{x}_t^\top \bm{y}_k \rvert
    = \frac{2}{q} \sum_{k=1}^q (\bm{x}_t^\top \bar{\bm{y}} - \bm{x}_t^\top \bm{y}_k)^+.
\end{equation*}

To write the problem as a linear program, we introduce, as for the semi-variance, an auxiliary variable $u_k \ge 0$ for
each realization $k$ subject to $u_k \ge \bm{x}_t^\top \bar{\bm{y}} - \bm{x}_t^\top \bm{y}_k$.
Since the objective $\frac{2}{q} \sum_{k=1}^q u_k$ increases in every $u_k$, each $u_k$ equals
$(\bm{x}_t^\top \bar{\bm{y}} - \bm{x}_t^\top \bm{y}_k)^+$ at the optimum, and the mean absolute deviation program is the
linear program
\begin{equation*}
\begin{aligned}
    \bm{x}_t^*, \bm{u}^* = \argmin_{\bm{x}_t,\, \bm{u}} \quad & \frac{2}{q} \sum_{k=1}^q u_k, \\
    \text{s.t.} \quad & \bm{x}_t^\top \bm{1}_N = 1, \\
    & \bm{x}_t^\top (\bm{y}_k - \bar{\bm{y}}) + u_k \ge 0, \quad u_k \ge 0, \quad k = 1, \dots, q.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation*}
It has the same constraints as the semi-variance program, but its objective is linear in the auxiliary variables. We write it in the standard form of a linear program in Appendix~\ref{app:mad_representation}.

The linear program is computationally efficient, but its solution is sensitive to estimation error. Kondor et
al.~\cite{kondor2007noise} show that portfolios optimized under the mean absolute deviation are even more sensitive to
estimation error than those optimized under the variance, and that their weights fluctuate more strongly from sample to
sample.

The three risk measures considered so far measure the dispersion of the portfolio return around its mean. Expected
shortfall, which we consider next, instead measures the average size of the largest losses.

\subsection{Expected Shortfall}\label{sec:risk-es}

Expected shortfall measures the risk of a portfolio by the average of its largest losses. Let
$L(\bm{x}_t) = -\bm{x}_t^\top \bm{R}_{t+1}$ denote the loss of the portfolio at time $t+1$ and $\mathbb{P}_t(\cdot)$
the probability conditional on the information available at time $t$. The value-at-risk (VaR) at confidence level
$\beta \in (0, 1)$ is the $\beta$-quantile of the loss distribution
\begin{equation*}
    \text{VaR}_\beta(\bm{x}_t) = \min \{ \alpha \mid \mathbb{P}_t(L(\bm{x}_t) \le \alpha) \ge \beta \}.
\end{equation*}
VaR does not take into account how large the losses beyond it are. Expected shortfall is the expected loss given that
the loss exceeds VaR
\begin{equation*}
    \text{ES}_\beta(\bm{x}_t) = \mathbb{E}_t\bigl[ L(\bm{x}_t) \mid L(\bm{x}_t) \ge \text{VaR}_\beta(\bm{x}_t) \bigr],
\end{equation*}
so that it accounts for both the probability and the size of the losses in the tail. 
In its capital requirements for market risk, the Basel Committee on Banking
Supervision has replaced VaR at the $99\%$ level by expected shortfall at the $97.5\%$ level \cite{BCBS2019}, indicating
that it is a more appropriate measure of risk.

Since $\text{VaR}_\beta(\bm{x}_t)$ itself depends on the portfolio weights, the conditional expectation in the definition is not
in a suitable form for optimization. Rockafellar and Uryasev \cite{RockafellarUryasev2000, RockafellarUryasev2002} show
that expected shortfall is the minimum over $\alpha \in \R$ of the auxiliary function
\begin{equation*}
    F_\beta(\bm{x}_t, \alpha) = \alpha + \frac{1}{1-\beta}\, \mathbb{E}_t\bigl[ (L(\bm{x}_t) - \alpha)^+ \bigr],
\end{equation*}  
and that the minimizing $\alpha$ is $\text{VaR}_\beta(\bm{x}_t)$. Minimizing expected shortfall over the portfolio weights $\bm{x}_t$ is
therefore equivalent to minimizing $F_\beta$ jointly over $\bm{x}_t$ and $\alpha$
\begin{equation*}
    \min_{\bm{x}_t} \text{ES}_\beta(\bm{x}_t) = \min_{\bm{x}_t, \alpha} F_\beta(\bm{x}_t, \alpha),
\end{equation*}
where $F_\beta$ is jointly convex in $(\bm{x}_t, \alpha)$. The derivation of this representation is given in Appendix~\ref{app:es_auxiliary}.

With the historical realizations $\bm{y}_1, \dots, \bm{y}_q$ of \Cref{sec:risk-variance}, we replace the conditional
expectation in $F_\beta$ by the empirical average, which gives the objective
$\alpha + \frac{1}{q(1-\beta)} \sum_{k=1}^q (-\bm{x}_t^\top \bm{y}_k - \alpha)^+$. As for the semi-variance and the
mean absolute deviation, we introduce for each realization $k$ an auxiliary variable $u_k \ge 0$ subject to
$u_k \ge -\bm{x}_t^\top \bm{y}_k - \alpha$. Since the objective increases in every $u_k$, each $u_k$ equals
$(-\bm{x}_t^\top \bm{y}_k - \alpha)^+$ at the optimum, and the expected shortfall program is the linear program
\begin{equation*}
\begin{aligned}
    \bm{x}_t^*, \alpha^*, \bm{u}^* = \argmin_{\bm{x}_t,\, \alpha,\, \bm{u}} \quad
    & \alpha + \frac{1}{q(1-\beta)} \sum_{k=1}^q u_k, \\
    \text{s.t.} \quad & \bm{x}_t^\top \bm{1}_N = 1, \\
    & \bm{x}_t^\top \bm{y}_k + \alpha + u_k \ge 0, \quad u_k \ge 0, \quad k = 1, \dots, q.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation*}
Since expected shortfall measures losses rather than deviations from the mean, the constraints contain the realizations
$\bm{y}_k$ themselves instead of their deviations from the sample mean. We write the program in the standard form of a
linear program in Appendix~\ref{app:lp_es}.

At the optimum, only the realizations whose loss exceeds $\alpha^*$, which are the $(1-\beta) q$ realizations with
the largest losses, contribute to the objective. The estimate of expected shortfall is therefore based on a fraction of the
sample, and Kondor et al.~\cite{kondor2007noise} show that portfolios optimized under expected shortfall are more
sensitive to estimation error than those optimized under the variance and the mean absolute deviation. Goldberg et
al.~\cite{goldberg2013minimizingshortfall} note that estimation error matters more for expected shortfall than for the
variance, since most realizations only serve to determine which losses are the largest. The confidence level thus
trades off the focus on the tail against the number of realizations that determine the estimate. We set $\beta = 0.8$,
at which about $500$ of the $q \approx 2{,}500$ daily realizations contribute to the objective. Testing confidence levels from $60\%$ to $99\%$, Goldberg et al.~\cite{goldberg2013minimizingshortfall} also find
that minimum expected shortfall portfolios outperform minimum-variance portfolios most at relatively low confidence
levels, at which expected shortfall captures the asymmetry of the return distribution rather than its extreme losses.

Furthermore, Ciliberti et
al.~\cite{ciliberti2007feasibility} show that, without bounds on the portfolio weights, the program is unbounded in some samples. 
For large portfolios, it
has no finite solution once $N/q$ exceeds a critical value, which is about $0.5$ for high confidence levels, and the weights fluctuate from sample to sample as $N/q$ approaches this value. In the large
universe of our empirical analysis in \Cref{chap:backtest-analysis,chap:joint-vs-sequential}, which contains up to $1{,}400$ stocks, $N/q$ can exceed this value. The leverage
constraint that we add later in this chapter bounds the positions and thereby ensures that the program has a solution.

Expected shortfall weighs all losses beyond VaR equally, so that the largest losses in the tail count no more
than the losses just beyond the quantile. Second-order expected shortfall, which we consider next, puts more weight on
the largest losses.

\subsection{Second-Order Expected Shortfall}\label{sec:risk-soes}

Second-order expected shortfall belongs to the family of higher-moment coherent risk measures of Krokhmal
\cite{Krokhmal2007}, which measure the risk in the tail of the loss distribution through its higher moments and also contain
expected shortfall. The second-order formulation replaces the expectation of the excess loss in the auxiliary
function $F_\beta$ of \Cref{sec:risk-es} by the $L^2$ norm $\|Z\|_{L^2} = (\mathbb{E}_t[Z^2])^{1/2}$ of the excess loss, which gives the
auxiliary function
\begin{equation*}
    G_\beta(\bm{x}_t, \alpha) = \alpha + \frac{1}{1-\beta}\, \bigl\| (L(\bm{x}_t) - \alpha)^+ \bigr\|_{L^2},
\end{equation*}
and second-order expected shortfall at confidence level $\beta$ is defined as its minimum over $\alpha$
\begin{equation*}
    \text{SOES}_\beta(\bm{x}_t) = \min_{\alpha \in \R} G_\beta(\bm{x}_t, \alpha).
\end{equation*}
Since the $L^2$ norm squares the excess losses, large losses beyond $\alpha$ receive more weight than in expected
shortfall. Krokhmal notes that second-order expected shortfall measures the risk in the tail through the second moment
of the losses, which relates it to the mean-variance framework. In a case study on fifty S\&P~500 stocks, he finds that 
portfolios minimizing the higher-moment measures at the $90\%$ level outperform
those minimizing expected shortfall or the variance out of sample \cite{Krokhmal2007}.

By definition, minimizing second-order expected shortfall is
\begin{equation*}
    \min_{\bm{x}_t} \text{SOES}_\beta(\bm{x}_t) = \min_{\bm{x}_t, \alpha} G_\beta(\bm{x}_t, \alpha),
\end{equation*}
where $G_\beta$ is jointly convex in $(\bm{x}_t, \alpha)$, which we show in Appendix~\ref{app:soes_auxiliary}.

With the historical realizations $\bm{y}_1, \dots, \bm{y}_q$ of \Cref{sec:risk-variance}, we replace the $L^2$ norm in
$G_\beta$ by its empirical estimate
$\bigl( \frac{1}{q} \sum_{k=1}^q \bigl( (-\bm{x}_t^\top \bm{y}_k - \alpha)^+ \bigr)^2 \bigr)^{1/2}$. As for expected
shortfall, we introduce for each realization $k$ an auxiliary variable $u_k \ge 0$ subject to
$u_k \ge -\bm{x}_t^\top \bm{y}_k - \alpha$, so that the empirical norm becomes $\frac{1}{\sqrt{q}} \|\bm{u}\|_2$. Since the objective is still not linear, we introduce
one more auxiliary variable $s$ subject to $\|\bm{u}\|_2 \le s$, which equals $\|\bm{u}\|_2$ at the optimum, and the
second-order expected shortfall program is
\begin{equation*}
\begin{aligned}
    \bm{x}_t^*, \alpha^*, \bm{u}^*, s^* = \argmin_{\bm{x}_t,\, \alpha,\, \bm{u},\, s} \quad
    & \alpha + \frac{1}{(1-\beta)\sqrt{q}}\, s, \\
    \text{s.t.} \quad & \bm{x}_t^\top \bm{1}_N = 1, \\
    & \bm{x}_t^\top \bm{y}_k + \alpha + u_k \ge 0, \quad u_k \ge 0, \quad k = 1, \dots, q, \\
    & \|\bm{u}\|_2 \le s.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation*}
The constraint $\|\bm{u}\|_2 \le s$ requires the vector $[\bm{u}^\top, s]^\top$ to lie in the second-order cone, the set
of vectors whose last entry is at least the Euclidean norm of the remaining entries \cite[Section~2.2.3]{boyd2004convex},
so that the program is a second-order cone program \cite[Section~4.4.2]{boyd2004convex}. We write it in standard form in
Appendix~\ref{app:socp_soes}.

The cone constraint makes the program harder to solve than the linear and quadratic programs of the other risk
measures, and the second-order expected shortfall programs are computationally the most expensive programs in our
backtests (see Appendix~\ref{sec:computational-complexity}). We use the same confidence level $\beta = 0.8$ as for expected
shortfall, so that the two tail measures can be compared, and as for expected shortfall, the leverage constraint that we
add later in this chapter bounds the positions and thereby ensures that the program has a solution.

\subsection{Coherent Risk Measures and Deviation Measures}\label{sec:coherent-and-deviation-measures}
Expected shortfall and second-order expected shortfall measure the size of the largest losses, whereas the variance,
the semi-variance, and the mean absolute deviation measure deviations from the mean.
The five risk measures can be divided into two classes, which differ in how they treat the return
distribution.

Artzner et al.~\cite{CoherentMeasuresOfRisk} call a risk measure $\mathcal{R}$ coherent if it satisfies four
properties. Adding a riskless amount to the return reduces the risk by that amount (translation equivariance),
$\mathcal{R}(X + C) = \mathcal{R}(X) - C$, scaling the position by a positive factor scales the risk by the same
factor (positive homogeneity), the risk of a combined position is at most the sum of the risks of its parts (subadditivity),
so that diversification does not
increase risk, and a return that is higher carries no more risk (monotonicity). Out of the five risk measures we define in this chapter, only expected shortfall \cite{acerbi2002coherenceexpectedshortfall} and
second-order expected shortfall \cite{Krokhmal2007} are coherent. Additionally, they are both strictly expectation bounded, that
is, their risk exceeds the expected loss of every nonconstant return.

Rockafellar et al.~\cite{RockafellarUryasevZabarankin2006} define deviation measures $\mathcal{D}$, which quantify
the uncertainty of a return rather than its losses. They share positive homogeneity and subadditivity with coherent risk
measures, are non-negative and zero only for constant returns, and replace the first property by translation
invariance, $\mathcal{D}(X + C) = \mathcal{D}(X)$. The standard deviation, the semi-deviation, and the mean absolute deviation are deviation measures.
The variance and the semi-variance are the
squares of the standard deviation and the semi-deviation, so they are not positively homogeneous and therefore not
deviation measures themselves. They are, however, translation invariant, and they have the same minimizers as 
the standard deviation and the semi-deviation.

The two translation properties capture the difference between the classes. A deviation measure depends only on the
dispersion of the return around its mean and is unaffected by a shift of the whole distribution, whereas a coherent
risk measure decreases when the distribution shifts upward. This fundamental difference between the two classes of risk measures is reflected 
in our empirical results in \Cref{chap:backtest-analysis,chap:joint-vs-sequential}. The reader can find the technical definitions and results we use for coherent risk measures 
and deviation measures in Appendix~\ref{app:demeaning}. For brevity, we refer in the remainder of the thesis to
the variance, the semi-variance, and the mean absolute deviation as the deviation measures, and to expected shortfall
and second-order expected shortfall as the tail measures.
 
So far, we have treated the asset returns as given and denominated in the investor's domestic currency. An
international investor, however, holds assets denominated in several currencies, whose returns in the domestic currency
also depend on the exchange rates and on the currency positions that the investor takes to hedge them. All five risk
measures are evaluated on the portfolio return in the domestic currency, so before we can formulate the multi-currency
programs, we have to express this return in terms of the asset weights and the currency positions. In the next section
we derive this representation, without assuming any underlying model for the joint distribution of asset and currency returns.

\section{Model-Free Multi-Currency Portfolio Returns}\label{sec:returns}

Burkhardt and Ulrych \cite{SparseAndStableInternationalPortfolioOptimization} express the return of an international
portfolio in the domestic currency in terms of its asset weights and currency positions, and use this representation for
regularized mean-variance optimization, with the currency positions chosen either jointly with the asset weights or after
them. Lucescu and Ulrych \cite{RegularizedMultiCurrencyExpectedShortfallPortfolios} apply the same representation to
expected shortfall portfolios, and we follow their derivation and notation.

\subsection{Unhedged Portfolio Returns}\label{sec:unhedged-returns}

We consider an international investor who allocates their wealth across $N$ assets denominated in up to $M+1$ currencies. Let the investor's domestic currency be indexed by $0$ and let the remaining $M$ foreign currencies be indexed by $\mathcal{C} = \{1,2,\dots,M\}$.

Let $P_{i,t}>0$ be the price of asset $i$ at time $t$ in its local currency $c_i \in \mathcal{C}\cup \{0\}$. Define the corresponding simple local-currency asset return over the period from $t$ to $t+1$ as
\begin{equation*}
R_{i,t+1} = \frac{P_{i,t+1}-P_{i,t}}{P_{i,t}}.
\end{equation*}
Thus $P_{i,t+1}=P_{i,t}(1+R_{i,t+1})$. For a domestic asset, $R_{i,t+1}$ is also the return in the domestic currency, as in the previous section.

For each foreign currency $c\in\mathcal{C}$, let $S_{c,t} > 0$ denote the exchange rate at time $t$, quoted as units of domestic currency per unit of foreign currency $c$. The associated simple currency return over the period from $t$ to $t+1$ is
\begin{equation*}
e_{c,t+1} = \frac{S_{c,t+1}-S_{c,t}}{S_{c,t}}.
\end{equation*}
Equivalently, $S_{c,t+1} = S_{c,t}(1+e_{c,t+1})$. For the domestic currency, we set $S_{0,t}\equiv 1$ and $e_{0,t+1}\equiv 0$.

The domestic-currency value of holding one unit of asset $i$ at time $t$ is $P_{i,t}\,S_{c_i,t}$. Therefore, the unhedged return of asset $i$ in the domestic currency is
\begin{align}
R^{u}_{i,t+1}
&=
\frac{P_{i,t+1}S_{c_i,t+1} - P_{i,t}S_{c_i,t}}{P_{i,t}S_{c_i,t}}
\nonumber\\
&=
\frac{P_{i,t}(1+R_{i,t+1})\,S_{c_i,t}(1+e_{c_i,t+1}) - P_{i,t}S_{c_i,t}}{P_{i,t}S_{c_i,t}}
\nonumber\\
&=
R_{i,t+1} + e_{c_i,t+1} + R_{i,t+1}\,e_{c_i,t+1}.
\label{eq:unhedged-asset-return}
\end{align}

As in the previous section, we denote the portfolio weights at time $t$ by $\bm{x}_t = [x_{1,t}, \dots, x_{N,t}]^\top$, with $x_{i,t}$ the fraction of total portfolio value invested in asset $i$,
and impose the budget constraint $\bm{x}_t^\top \bm{1}_N = 1$.

Assuming no rebalancing between times $t$ and $t+1$, the unhedged portfolio return in the domestic currency is given by
\begin{equation*}
R^{u}_{\mathcal{P},t+1} = \sum_{i=1}^N x_{i,t}\,R^{u}_{i,t+1}.
\end{equation*}

\subsection{Hedged Portfolio Returns}\label{sec:hedged-returns}

To manage currency risk, the investor can take positions in one-period currency forward contracts. For each foreign currency $c\in\mathcal{C}$, let $F_{c,t}$ be the forward rate quoted at time $t$ for settlement at $t+1$, in units of domestic currency per unit of foreign currency $c$. We define the forward premium by
\begin{equation*}
f_{c,t} = \frac{F_{c,t}-S_{c,t}}{S_{c,t}}.
\end{equation*}

The investor hedges by selling foreign currency forward. Let $\upphi_{c,t}$ denote the relative notional of the short forward position in currency $c$ at time $t$, expressed as a fraction of total portfolio value, with the convention that $\upphi_{c,t}>0$ corresponds to hedging. Per unit of notional, the short position pays $f_{c,t}-e_{c,t+1}$ in domestic currency over the period. Adding these forward payoffs to the unhedged portfolio return gives the hedged portfolio return
\begin{equation}
R^{h}_{\mathcal{P},t+1}
=
\underbrace{\sum_{i=1}^N x_{i,t}\,R^{u}_{i,t+1}}_{R^{u}_{\mathcal{P},t+1}}
+
\sum_{c=1}^M \upphi_{c,t}\,(f_{c,t}-e_{c,t+1}).
\label{eq:hedged-portfolio-return}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Matrix Representation and Decomposition}\label{sec:matrix-representation}

To express the returns in a compact form suitable for optimization, we define the currency incidence matrix $\bm{C}\in\{0,1\}^{N\times M}$ by
\begin{equation*}
C_{i,c} =
\begin{cases}
1, & \text{if asset $i$ is denominated in foreign currency $c$},\\
0, & \text{otherwise}.
\end{cases}
\end{equation*}
Each row of $\bm{C}$ has at most one nonzero entry, and the rows corresponding to domestic assets are identically zero. This allows us to define the portfolio's implicit foreign-currency exposure as
\begin{equation*}
\bm{w}_t = \bm{C}^\top \bm{x}_t \in \R^M,
\qquad
w_{c,t} = \sum_{i:\,c_i=c} x_{i,t},
\end{equation*}
so that $w_{c,t}$ is the total portfolio weight invested in assets denominated in currency $c$. The implicit domestic exposure follows residually as $w_{0,t} = 1 - \bm{1}_M^\top \bm{w}_t$, since the asset weights sum to one.

We express returns in vector form $\bm{R}_{t+1} \in \R^N$, $\bm{e}_{t+1} \in \R^M$, and the forward premia as $\bm{f}_t \in \R^M$. The product $\bm{C}\bm{e}_{t+1}\in\R^N$ aligns currency returns to assets, and its $i$-th component is $e_{c_i,t+1}$ for foreign assets and $0$ for domestic ones. Using \eqref{eq:unhedged-asset-return}, the vector of unhedged asset returns in the domestic currency can be written as
\begin{equation*}
\bm{R}^{u}_{t+1}
=
\bm{R}_{t+1}
+
\bm{C}\bm{e}_{t+1}
+
(\bm{R}_{t+1}\had\bm{C}\bm{e}_{t+1}),
\end{equation*}
where $\had$ denotes the Hadamard product. 

With the forward positions collected in $\bm{\upphi}_t\in\R^M$, the hedged portfolio return \eqref{eq:hedged-portfolio-return} can be written as
\begin{equation}
R^{h}_{\mathcal{P},t+1} = \bm{x}_t^\top \bm{R}^{u}_{t+1} + \bm{\upphi}_t^\top(\bm{f}_t-\bm{e}_{t+1}).
\label{eq:hedged-vector}
\end{equation}

Setting $\bm{\upphi}_t=\bm{w}_t=\bm{C}^\top\bm{x}_t$ neutralizes the implicit currency exposure. Substituting this and the unhedged return vector into \eqref{eq:hedged-vector} gives the fully hedged portfolio return
\begin{align*}
R^{fh}_{\mathcal{P},t+1}
&= \bm{x}_t^\top\bm{R}^{u}_{t+1} + \big(\bm{C}^\top\bm{x}_t\big)^\top(\bm{f}_t-\bm{e}_{t+1})\\[2pt]
&= \bm{x}_t^\top\Big[\bm{R}_{t+1} + \bm{C}\bm{e}_{t+1} + \big(\bm{R}_{t+1}\had\bm{C}\bm{e}_{t+1}\big)\Big] + \bm{x}_t^\top\bm{C}(\bm{f}_t-\bm{e}_{t+1})\\[2pt]
&= \bm{x}_t^\top\Big[\bm{R}_{t+1} + \bm{C}\bm{e}_{t+1} + \big(\bm{R}_{t+1}\had\bm{C}\bm{e}_{t+1}\big) + \bm{C}\bm{f}_t - \bm{C}\bm{e}_{t+1}\Big]\\[2pt]
&= \bm{x}_t^\top\Big[\bm{R}_{t+1} + \bm{C}\bm{f}_t + \big(\bm{R}_{t+1}\had\bm{C}\bm{e}_{t+1}\big)\Big]\\[2pt]
&= \bm{x}_t^\top\bm{R}^{fh}_{t+1}.
\end{align*}
The first-order currency terms $\pm\,\bm{x}_t^\top\bm{C}\bm{e}_{t+1}$ cancel, leaving the fully hedged asset return vector
\begin{equation*}
\bm{R}^{fh}_{t+1}
=
\bm{R}_{t+1}
+
\bm{C}\bm{f}_t
+
(\bm{R}_{t+1}\had\bm{C}\bm{e}_{t+1}).
\end{equation*}

More generally, define the net currency exposure $\bm{\psi}_t = \bm{w}_t-\bm{\upphi}_t$. Since a forward position in the domestic currency has no payoff, we can set the domestic position to $\upphi_{0,t} = 1 - \bm{1}_M^\top \bm{\upphi}_t$. The domestic net exposure is then $\psi_{0,t} = w_{0,t} - \upphi_{0,t} = -\bm{1}_M^\top \bm{\psi}_t$, so the net currency exposures sum to zero and form a zero-investment portfolio, the currency overlay. Substituting $\bm{\upphi}_t=\bm{w}_t-\bm{\psi}_t$ into \eqref{eq:hedged-vector} expresses the hedged return in terms of net exposures
\begin{align}
R^{h}_{\mathcal{P},t+1}
&= \bm{x}_t^\top \bm{R}^{u}_{t+1} + (\bm{w}_t-\bm{\psi}_t)^\top(\bm{f}_t-\bm{e}_{t+1})\nonumber \\[2pt]
&= \bm{x}_t^\top \bm{R}^{u}_{t+1} + \bm{w}_t^\top(\bm{f}_t-\bm{e}_{t+1}) + \bm{\psi}_t^\top(\bm{e}_{t+1}-\bm{f}_t) \nonumber\\[2pt]
&= \bm{x}_t^\top \bm{R}^{fh}_{t+1} + \bm{\psi}_t^\top(\bm{e}_{t+1}-\bm{f}_t),
\label{eq:hedged-net}
\end{align}
where $\bm{e}_{t+1} - \bm{f}_t$ is the vector of excess currency returns. We adopt this representation in all programs that follow, as it makes the asset weights $\bm{x}_t$ and the net currency exposures $\bm{\psi}_t$ the decision variables.

\section{Regularized Multi-Currency Portfolio Optimization}\label{sec:regularized-multi-currency}

In practice, international investors often manage currency risk with a currency overlay. The asset portfolio is
constructed first, without regard to its currency exposure, and a portfolio of currency positions is then laid over it,
often by a specialized overlay manager \cite{SparseAndStableInternationalPortfolioOptimization}. Jorion
\cite{Jorion1994} argues that this structure is inherently suboptimal, since it ignores the interactions between the
assets in the underlying portfolio and the exchange rates. In his simulations, a currency overlay adds value to global
equity and bond portfolios, but improves their performance less than an integrated approach to currency management.
Burkhardt and Ulrych \cite{SparseAndStableInternationalPortfolioOptimization} find that regularized joint
mean-variance optimization of assets and currencies also outperforms its currency overlay counterpart out of sample. For expected shortfall, Lucescu and Ulrych
\cite{RegularizedMultiCurrencyExpectedShortfallPortfolios} report that joint optimization performs better in the
smaller of their two investable universes. 

We formulate both approaches for the five risk measures introduced earlier in this chapter, applied to the hedged
portfolio return of the previous section. In sequential optimization, the asset weights $\bm{x}_t$ are chosen first by
minimizing the risk of the fully hedged asset portfolio, and the currency overlay $\bm{\psi}_t$ is then chosen by
minimizing the risk of the hedged portfolio return with the asset weights held fixed. In joint optimization,
$\bm{x}_t$ and $\bm{\psi}_t$ are chosen together in a single program. The joint program can therefore exploit the
dependence between the individual assets and the currencies, whereas the overlay program takes the currencies into
account only through their dependence on the return of the fixed asset portfolio, which Burkhardt and Ulrych
\cite{SparseAndStableInternationalPortfolioOptimization} regard as a form of dimensionality reduction. 
\subsection{Regularization}\label{sec:regularization}

Due to estimation error, the optimal weights of these programs fluctuate from one sample to the next. Kondor
et al.~\cite{kondor2007noise} find that the weights are even more sensitive to estimation error than the risk of the
optimized portfolio, and that close to a critical value of the ratio of the number of assets to the number of
observations, their fluctuations become so strong that the optimization loses its meaning. Bounding the weights, for
example with a long-only constraint, prevents them from diverging, but Ciliberti et
al.~\cite{ciliberti2007feasibility} note that large fluctuations remain and that many weights stick to the bounds.

Regularization reduces these fluctuations at the cost of introducing bias in the optimization. A penalty on the size of the weights pulls the solution
toward the portfolios that the penalty favors.
Empirically it tends to improve out-of-sample performance. DeMiguel et
al.~\cite{DeMiguelGarlappiNogalesUppal2009} find that minimum-variance portfolios with a constraint on the norm of the
weights often have higher out-of-sample Sharpe ratios than portfolios based only on shrinkage estimators. 
Brodie et al.~\cite{BrodieDaubechiesDeMolGiannoneLoris2009} find that mean-variance
portfolios with an $L^1$ penalty consistently outperform the equally weighted portfolio out of sample, and Li
\cite{Li2015} finds that portfolios with constraints that induce sparsity and stability have better
out-of-sample performance and lower turnover than many other strategies. In the multi-currency setting, Burkhardt and
Ulrych \cite{SparseAndStableInternationalPortfolioOptimization} find that their regularized joint mean-variance programs
outperform unregularized benchmarks, and Lucescu and Ulrych \cite{RegularizedMultiCurrencyExpectedShortfallPortfolios}
find that regularization lowers the realized risk and raises the risk-adjusted returns of both the sequential and the
joint optimization approach under expected shortfall.

Following Lucescu and Ulrych \cite{RegularizedMultiCurrencyExpectedShortfallPortfolios}, we regularize the programs with
an $L^1$ penalty, an $L^2$ penalty, and a leverage constraint. The $L^1$ penalty
$\lambda_1 \|\bm{x}_t\|_1 = \lambda_1 \sum_{i=1}^N |x_{i,t}|$, with $\lambda_1 \ge 0$, penalizes the absolute size of the
weights. Brodie et al.~\cite{BrodieDaubechiesDeMolGiannoneLoris2009}
show that the penalty is effective in portfolio selection, where it yields portfolios with few active positions.
Under the budget constraint, the long positions exceed the short positions by one, so that
\begin{equation*}
    \|\bm{x}_t\|_1 = 1 + 2 \sum_{i=1}^N (-x_{i,t})^+.
\end{equation*}
The $L^1$ penalty therefore acts only on the short positions. Once $\lambda_1$ is large enough to remove them, the
penalty becomes constant, and the solution is the long-only minimizer of the risk measure
\cite{BrodieDaubechiesDeMolGiannoneLoris2009}. For expected shortfall, Papp et al.~\cite{PappKondorCaccioli2021} show
that removing short positions tends to remove the most volatile assets from the portfolio.

The $L^2$ penalty $\lambda_2 \|\bm{x}_t\|_2^2 = \lambda_2 \sum_{i=1}^N x_{i,t}^2$, with $\lambda_2 \ge 0$, penalizes large
weights more than small ones and spreads the portfolio more evenly over many assets. Under the budget constraint,
$\|\bm{x}_t\|_2^2$ is smallest for the equally weighted portfolio, so the penalty shrinks the weights toward it. 

The leverage constraint $\|\bm{x}_t\|_1 \le \ell^a$, with $\ell^a \ge 1$, bounds the gross exposure of the asset
portfolio. Setting $\ell^a = 1$ rules out short positions, and the value $\ell^a = 2$ that we
use in the empirical analysis limits the total short position to half of the portfolio value. Ammann et
al.~\cite{AmmannCoqueretSchade2016} find that a leverage constraint improves the implementation of
portfolios, since it lowers their transaction costs, the number of negative weights, and their volatility, and makes them
less sensitive to the choice of their parameters. Together with the budget constraint, the leverage constraint
defines the set of feasible asset weights
\begin{equation*}
    \mathcal{X} = \bigl\{ \bm{x} \in \R^N : \bm{x}^\top \bm{1}_N = 1, \ \|\bm{x}\|_1 \le \ell^a \bigr\}.
\end{equation*}

In the multi-currency programs that follow, the asset weights and the currency exposures have separate penalty weights,
$\lambda_1^a$ and $\lambda_2^a$ for the assets and $\lambda_1^c$ and $\lambda_2^c$ for the currencies, since the two
differ in their constraints and transaction costs. The
currency exposures carry neither a budget constraint nor a leverage constraint, so both penalties shrink them toward
zero, that is, toward the fully hedged portfolio, and the $L^1$ penalty sets the exposures to some currencies exactly to
zero. Lucescu and Ulrych \cite{RegularizedMultiCurrencyExpectedShortfallPortfolios} note that the sparsity that the
$L^1$ penalty promotes may be desirable for the selection of assets but less so for the currencies. In our backtests the penalty weights
are selected at every rebalancing date by the grid search described in \Cref{sec:backtest-configuration}.


\subsection{Sequential Optimization}\label{sec:sequential-optimization}

In sequential optimization, the asset weights and the currency exposures are chosen in two steps. The asset step chooses
the asset weights by minimizing the risk of the fully hedged asset portfolio
\begin{equation}\label{eq:sequential-asset}
    \bm{x}_t^* = \argmin_{\bm{x}_t \in \mathcal{X}} \Bigl\{ \rho\bigl( \bm{x}_t^\top \bm{R}^{fh}_{t+1} \bigr)
    + \lambda_1^a \|\bm{x}_t\|_1 + \lambda_2^a \|\bm{x}_t\|_2^2 \Bigr\}.
\end{equation}
This is the regularized version of the single-currency problem \eqref{eq:risk-based-problem}, with the fully hedged
return vector $\bm{R}^{fh}_{t+1}$ in place of $\bm{R}_{t+1}$.
The currency step then chooses the currency overlay by minimizing the risk of the hedged portfolio return
\eqref{eq:hedged-net}, with the asset weights held fixed
\begin{equation}\label{eq:sequential-currency}
    \bm{\psi}_t^* \mid \bm{x}_t^* = \argmin_{\bm{\psi}_t \in \R^M} \Bigl\{ \rho\bigl( \bm{x}_t^{*\top} \bm{R}^{fh}_{t+1}
    + \bm{\psi}_t^\top (\bm{e}_{t+1} - \bm{f}_t) \bigr) + \lambda_1^c \|\bm{\psi}_t\|_1 + \lambda_2^c \|\bm{\psi}_t\|_2^2
    \Bigr\}.
\end{equation}
We derive the programs of both steps for the five risk measures in
Appendices~\ref{app:separate-optimization}, \ref{app:mv_separate}, and~\ref{app:mad_separate}.

With $\bm{\psi}_t = \bm{0}_M$, the implicit currency
exposure of the asset portfolio is fully hedged, and with $\bm{\psi}_t = \bm{w}_t = \bm{C}^\top \bm{x}_t^*$, the
portfolio holds no forward positions and is unhedged. The two resulting asset-only portfolios serve as the benchmarks
Full and Zero in \Cref{sec:backtest-configuration}.

\subsection{Joint Optimization}\label{sec:joint-optimization}

The joint program chooses the asset weights and the currency exposures together. We stack them into the combined
decision vector $\bm{\theta}_t = [\bm{x}_t^\top, \bm{\psi}_t^\top]^\top \in \R^{N+M}$ and the fully hedged and excess
currency returns into the combined return vector
$\bm{R}^{\text{all}}_{t+1} = [\bm{R}^{fh\top}_{t+1}, (\bm{e}_{t+1} - \bm{f}_t)^\top]^\top \in \R^{N+M}$, so that
$\bm{\theta}_t^\top \bm{R}^{\text{all}}_{t+1}$ is the hedged portfolio return \eqref{eq:hedged-net}. The joint program is then written as
\begin{equation}\label{eq:joint}
\begin{gathered}
    \bm{\theta}_t^* = \argmin_{\bm{\theta}_t} \Bigl\{ \rho\bigl( \bm{\theta}_t^\top \bm{R}^{\text{all}}_{t+1} \bigr)
    + \|\bm{S}_1 \bm{\theta}_t\|_1 + \|\bm{S}_2 \bm{\theta}_t\|_2^2 \Bigr\} \\[4pt]
    \text{s.t. } \bm{\theta}_t^\top \bm{q}_{N,M} = 1, \quad \|\bm{L}^a \bm{\theta}_t\|_1 \le \ell^a,
\end{gathered}
\end{equation}
where the vector $\bm{q}_{N,M} \in \R^{N+M}$ has ones in the $N$ asset positions and zeros in the $M$ currency
positions, and the matrix $\bm{L}^a \in \R^{(N+M) \times (N+M)}$ is diagonal with ones in the asset positions and zeros
in the currency positions. The budget and the leverage constraint thus apply to the asset weights only, and the currency exposures remain unconstrained. The regularization matrix
$\bm{S}_1 \in \R^{(N+M) \times (N+M)}$ is diagonal with $\lambda_1^a$ in the asset positions and $\lambda_1^c$ in the
currency positions, and $\bm{S}_2 \in \R^{(N+M) \times (N+M)}$ is diagonal with $\sqrt{\lambda_2^a}$ and
$\sqrt{\lambda_2^c}$ in the same positions, so that
$\|\bm{S}_1 \bm{\theta}_t\|_1 = \lambda_1^a \|\bm{x}_t\|_1 + \lambda_1^c \|\bm{\psi}_t\|_1$ and
$\|\bm{S}_2 \bm{\theta}_t\|_2^2 = \lambda_2^a \|\bm{x}_t\|_2^2 + \lambda_2^c \|\bm{\psi}_t\|_2^2$. This formulation allows us to treat
the asset and the currency penalties of \Cref{sec:regularization} separately within a single objective. We give the
explicit matrices and derive the joint programs for the five risk measures in
Appendices~\ref{app:joint-optimization}, \ref{app:mv_joint}, and~\ref{app:mad_joint}.

All programs of this section are convex. Since the $L^1$ penalties and the leverage constraint can be written with
linear constraints, the regularized programs are quadratic programs under the variance, the semi-variance, the mean
absolute deviation, and expected shortfall, and a second-order cone program with a convex quadratic objective under
second-order expected shortfall. Without the $L^2$ penalties, the programs under the mean absolute deviation and expected
shortfall are linear programs. Standard solvers handle all of these programs efficiently~\cite{boyd2004convex}.

\section{Mean-Risk Portfolio Optimization} \label{sec:mean-risk-portfolio-optimization}
 
The programs of the previous section minimize a measure of risk without an explicit estimate of the expected returns,
and we argued in \Cref{sec:risk-based-optimization} why such estimates are imprecise. The resulting portfolios,
however, may give up excess returns that a moderately riskier allocation would earn. A mean term in the objective lets
the programs trade off expected return against risk explicitly. With it, the joint program can also weigh the expected
returns of the assets and the currencies against each other, whereas the overlay program considers the expected
currency returns only once the asset weights are fixed.

Mean-risk optimization goes back to Markowitz \cite{Markowitz1952}, and the works on which our programs for the other
four risk measures are based also trade off risk against expected return. Markowitz et al.~\cite{Markowitz1993} compute
the portfolios that are efficient with respect to the expected return and the semi-variance, and Konno and Yamazaki
\cite{KonnoYamazaki1991}, Rockafellar and Uryasev \cite{RockafellarUryasev2000}, and Krokhmal \cite{Krokhmal2007}
minimize the mean absolute deviation, expected shortfall, and the higher-moment coherent risk measures, respectively. 
In the multi-currency setting, Burkhardt and Ulrych
\cite{SparseAndStableInternationalPortfolioOptimization} use a mean-variance objective and estimate the expected returns with 
the Bayes--Stein estimator of Jorion \cite{Jorion1986BayesStein}.
Lucescu and Ulrych \cite{RegularizedMultiCurrencyExpectedShortfallPortfolios} formulate their programs with an objective
that trades off the expected return against expected shortfall but evaluate them out of sample under infinite risk
aversion, which removes the expected return from the objective. We add the mean term back to the programs
of the previous section and estimate the expected returns in a way that makes them more stable and less affected by estimation error.

\subsection{Mean-Risk Programs}\label{sec:mean-risk-programs}

To add a mean term to the sequential and joint programs, let
$\bm{\mu}^{fh}_t = \mathbb{E}_t[\bm{R}^{fh}_{t+1}] \in \R^N$ and
$\bm{\mu}^c_t = \mathbb{E}_t[\bm{e}_{t+1} - \bm{f}_t] \in \R^M$ denote the expected fully hedged asset returns and the
expected excess currency returns, and let
$\bm{\mu}^{\text{all}}_t = [(\bm{\mu}^{fh}_t)^\top, (\bm{\mu}^c_t)^\top]^\top \in \R^{N+M}$, so that
$\bm{\theta}_t^\top \bm{\mu}^{\text{all}}_t$ is the expected hedged portfolio return. Each program subtracts the
expected return from the objective. We scale the expected return by $1/\gamma$, where $\gamma > 0$ is the risk
aversion coefficient. The asset step \eqref{eq:sequential-asset} becomes
\begin{equation}
    \bm{x}_t^* = \argmin_{\bm{x}_t \in \mathcal{X}} \Bigl\{ \rho\bigl( \bm{x}_t^\top \bm{R}^{fh}_{t+1} \bigr)
    - \frac{1}{\gamma}\, \bm{x}_t^\top \bm{\mu}^{fh}_t
    + \lambda_1^a \|\bm{x}_t\|_1 + \lambda_2^a \|\bm{x}_t\|_2^2 \Bigr\},
\end{equation}
and the currency step \eqref{eq:sequential-currency} becomes
\begin{equation}
\begin{gathered}
    \begin{aligned}
    \bm{\psi}_t^* \mid \bm{x}_t^* = \argmin_{\bm{\psi}_t \in \R^M} \Bigl\{ &
    \rho\bigl( \bm{x}_t^{*\top} \bm{R}^{fh}_{t+1} + \bm{\psi}_t^\top (\bm{e}_{t+1} - \bm{f}_t) \bigr)
    - \frac{1}{\gamma}\, \bm{\psi}_t^\top \bm{\mu}^c_t \\
    & + \lambda_1^c \|\bm{\psi}_t\|_1 + \lambda_2^c \|\bm{\psi}_t\|_2^2 \Bigr\}
    \end{aligned} \\[4pt]
    \text{s.t. } \|\bm{\psi}_t\|_1 \le \ell^c,
\end{gathered}
\end{equation}
where the expected return of the fixed asset portfolio is a constant and is removed from the objective. The joint program
\eqref{eq:joint} becomes
\begin{equation}
\begin{gathered}
    \bm{\theta}_t^* = \argmin_{\bm{\theta}_t} \Bigl\{ \rho\bigl( \bm{\theta}_t^\top \bm{R}^{\text{all}}_{t+1} \bigr)
    - \frac{1}{\gamma}\, \bm{\theta}_t^\top \bm{\mu}^{\text{all}}_t
    + \|\bm{S}_1 \bm{\theta}_t\|_1 + \|\bm{S}_2 \bm{\theta}_t\|_2^2 \Bigr\} \\[4pt]
    \text{s.t. } \bm{\theta}_t^\top \bm{q}_{N,M} = 1, \quad \|\bm{L}^a \bm{\theta}_t\|_1 \le \ell^a, \quad
    \|\bm{L}^c \bm{\theta}_t\|_1 \le \ell^c,
\end{gathered}
\end{equation}
where the matrix $\bm{L}^c \in \R^{(N+M) \times (N+M)}$ is diagonal with zeros in the asset positions and ones in the
currency positions. Both the currency step and the joint program thus contain a currency leverage constraint, which
the programs of \Cref{sec:regularized-multi-currency} do not have. We derive the mean-risk programs for the five risk
measures in Appendices~\ref{app:mean-risk}, \ref{app:mv_mean_risk}, and~\ref{app:mad_mean_risk}.

We use the same risk aversion coefficient in the asset step, the currency step, and the joint program. It determines
the weight of the expected return relative to the risk measure and the penalties. As $\gamma \to \infty$, the mean
term vanishes, and the programs reduce to the risk-minimization programs
\eqref{eq:sequential-asset}--\eqref{eq:joint} with the additional currency leverage constraint. As $\gamma \to 0$, the mean
term dominates, and the programs maximize the expected portfolio return subject to the constraints. More details on the 
calibration of the risk aversion coefficient are given in Appendix~\ref{app:risk-aversion-calibration}.

The currency leverage constraint bounds the currency exposures, which otherwise carry neither a budget constraint nor a
leverage constraint. Without it, the mean term can push the currency exposures toward very large positions. The asset
weights are already bounded by the
leverage constraint of \Cref{sec:regularization}, and for the currency exposures we set $\ell^c = 2$ as well.

\subsection{Expected Asset Returns}\label{sec:expected-asset-returns}

The natural estimate of $\bm{\mu}^{fh}_t$ is the vector of sample means of the historical realizations of
$\bm{R}^{fh}_{t+1}$. As discussed at the beginning of this chapter, the precision of an estimated expected
return depends on the length of the sample period \cite{Merton1980}. For example, if the returns of a stock with an annual volatility of $30\%$ are independent and normally distributed, 
the standard error of its sample mean over ten years is $30\% / \sqrt{10} \approx 9.5\%$ per year, and a
$95\%$ confidence interval for its expected return is about $37$ percentage points wide. 

To reduce the noise in the expected returns, we pool the stocks of each economy and estimate one expected return per economy. Each of the $14$ economies
of the empirical analysis has its own currency, so we index the economies by their currencies
$c \in \mathcal{C} \cup \{0\}$, and asset $i$ belongs to the economy of its local currency $c_i$. As in
\Cref{sec:risk-variance}, we suppress the dependence on $t$ of the scalar quantities that follow. Let $y^{fh}_{k,i}$
denote the fully hedged return of asset $i$ in the $k$-th historical realization. The return of economy $c$ in
realization $k$ is the equally weighted return of its $N_c$ assets
\begin{equation*}
    z_{k,c} = \frac{1}{N_c} \sum_{i \,:\, c_i = c} y^{fh}_{k,i},
\end{equation*}
with the sample mean $\bar{z}_c = \frac{1}{q} \sum_{k=1}^q z_{k,c}$. Pooling reduces the number of estimated means
from $N$ to the number of economies $M + 1$. The equally weighted economy
return also diversifies part of the volatility of its assets, so its mean is estimated more precisely than the means of
the individual assets. Every asset then uses the expected return of its economy, so the mean term depends on the
asset weights only through the total weight of each economy. 

The economy means are still imprecise, since the length of the sample period is unchanged, so we further shrink them with the estimator of James and Stein \cite{JamesStein1961}. Let $\bar{\bm{z}} = \left[\bar{z}_0, \bar{z}_1, \ldots, \bar{z}_M\right]^\top \in \R^{M+1}$ be the vector of the
sample means, and let $\bar{s}^2$ denote the average of the sample variances
$s_c^2 = \frac{1}{q-1} \sum_{k=1}^q (z_{k,c} - \bar{z}_c)^2$ of the economy returns. The shrunk economy means are
\begin{equation*}
    \hat{m}_c = (1 - \hat{\eta})\, \bar{z}_c,
    \qquad
    \hat{\eta} = \min\Bigl\{ 1, \frac{(M - 1)\, \bar{s}^2}{q\, \|\bar{\bm{z}}\|_2^2} \Bigr\},
\end{equation*}
and the estimate $\hat{\bm{\mu}}^{fh}_t$, whose $i$-th entry is $\hat{m}_{c_i}$, replaces $\bm{\mu}^{fh}_t$ in the
programs. James and Stein show that, for three or more independent normal means with a common variance, shrinking the
sample means toward zero by such a factor lowers their total expected squared error below that of the sample means,
whatever the true means are. 

\subsection{Expected Currency Returns}\label{sec:expected-currency-returns}

Unlike the expected asset returns, the expected excess currency returns are not estimated from the realizations but
derived from the forward premia. By covered interest parity, the forward rate of currency $c$ is
$F_{c,t} = S_{c,t} (1 + r_{0,t}) / (1 + r_{c,t})$, where $r_{0,t}$ and $r_{c,t}$ denote the domestic and the foreign
interest rate from $t$ to $t+1$, so that the forward premium
\begin{equation*}
    f_{c,t} = \frac{r_{0,t} - r_{c,t}}{1 + r_{c,t}} \approx r_{0,t} - r_{c,t}
\end{equation*}
is approximately the difference between the domestic and the foreign interest rate. A net exposure to currency $c$ 
therefore earns the spot return $e_{c,t+1}$ plus approximately the interest rate differential $r_{c,t} - r_{0,t}$,
which is known as the carry. Since $f_{c,t}$ is known at time $t$, the expected excess return of currency $c$ is
\begin{equation*}
    \mathbb{E}_t[e_{c,t+1} - f_{c,t}] = \mathbb{E}_t[e_{c,t+1}] - f_{c,t}.
\end{equation*}
Under uncovered interest parity, the expected change of the spot rate offsets the interest rate differential,
$\mathbb{E}_t[e_{c,t+1}] = f_{c,t}$, and the expected excess return is zero. We 
instead set the expected excess currency returns to the negative forward premia
\begin{equation*}
    \bm{\mu}^c_t = -\bm{f}_t,
\end{equation*}
which approximate the carry, so that the mean term rewards long exposures to currencies with a higher interest rate
than the domestic currency and short exposures to currencies with a lower one. Since the forward
premia are observed at time $t$, nothing has to be estimated, and the expected currency returns change over time with
the interest rate differentials.
