\chapter{Empirical Analysis: The Joint--Overlay Crossover}\label{chap:empirical-analysis-and-baseline-results}

In this chapter, we present an empirical analysis of the multi-currency portfolio optimization problem using historical data. We apply the model-free return representation derived in chapter \ref{chap:model-free-returns} to compute realized returns for the portfolio strategies derived in section \ref{todo}. We evalutate the performance of the various strategies on two investment universes, a large investment universe consisting of 50 biggest stocks in the 14 largest developed markets and a small investment universe consisting of 10 biggest stocks in the same markets. We compare the performance of the optimized portfolios to a benchmark equally weighted portfolio.

We can see in table \ref{tab:es-universe-sizes} that the performance of the expected shortfall optimization approaches varies across 
different universe sizes. In the small universe, the optimized portfolios generally outperform the equal weight benchmark in terms of 
risk-adjusted returns, as measured by the Sharpe Ratio and Sortino Ratio. However, as we increase the universe size to medium and large, 
the performance of the optimized portfolios relative to the equal weight benchmark deteriorates, with some strategies even underperforming 
the benchmark in terms of risk-adjusted returns. This suggests that the benefits of optimization may diminish as the investment universe expands, 
potentially due to increased estimation error and model uncertainty in larger universes.

List of results interpretations to write up
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Optimizers vs 1/N} All strategies outperform 1/N on all risk metrics and SR, SOR and ESR in all 3 universe sizes. The risk reduction is larger in ES and Vol than in MD, suggesting that the optimized portfolios are better at controlling tail risk and volatility, but may still be sucseptible to large drawdowns.
  
  \item \textbf{Risk metrics with increasing universe size} 
  We can observe that the risk metrics for the 1/N portfolio do not improve by much as the universe size increases. They do increase substantially for the optimized portfolios. They drop by around 50\% from the small to the large universe, while the 1/N portfolio only drops by around 15\%. This suggests that the benefits of optimization are more pronounced in larger universes, where there is more opportunity to diversify and reduce risk.

  \item \textbf{Joint-Reg vs.\ Overlay-Reg} In the small universe, Joint-Reg is ahead on SR, SOR and ESR  at slightly higher. Still waiting for results on the medium and large universes. Hypothesis: with the large universe there is no more benefit to the joint optimization. 

  \item \textbf{Regularized vs.\ unregularized.} Overlay vs.\ Overlay-Reg and Joint vs.\ Joint-Reg. Regularization does not improve risk metrics but does improve ER, SOR and ESR. It typically reduces turnover, but not in every situation. 

  \item \textbf{Plots} TODO: Compare drawdown depth of the optimizers against 1/N around 2002, 2008--09 and 2020, and compare how that gap changes based on universe size.

\end{itemize}

\section{The Effects of Universe Size on Portfolio Performance}

Motivation and hypothesis: When analyzing the results of the backtests, we see that the performance of the joint optimization approaches deteriorates as the universe size increases. The hypothesis for this is that
the joint approach must estimate the asset-currency dependance structure directly, while overlay avoids this with sequentially optimizing the weigths, which is a form of dimensionality reduction and 
should improve the performance in a higher dimensional setting. So the joint optimization should have an edge in a smaller dimensional setting since it 
can capture this dependence structure, but in a lerger dimensional setting, the estimation error would cause it to lose this edge. 


Analysis structure and expectation: To test this hypothesis, we analyze the relationship between the share of currency exposure in the portfolio (same as dimensionality) and the gap in the target risk measure attained between the in-sample and out-of-sample optimization.  

We calculate the mean of $$ g^{in sample, risk measure} = (f^{joint, in sample, risk measure} - f^{overlay, in sample, risk measure}) $$ and $$ g^{out of sample, risk measure} = (f^{joint, out of sample, risk measure} - f^{overlay, out of sample, risk measure}) $$ for each universe size and each risk measure. 

In the gap calculation, both $f^{in sample}$ and $f^{out of sample}$ are the same
risk measure evaluated on the combined portfolio return. The
weights are fit on the estimation window, scored on that window to give
$f^{in sample}$, and scored on the following quarter to give $f^{out of sample}$. In total we have 91 rebalance dates in our backtest (Quarterly from 2000-06-30 to 2022-12-30). We report the mean and we we bootstrap the 95\% confidence interval for the mean of the gap. If $g$ is negative that means the joint optimization attains a lower risk measure score. The unregularized version of the optimizers is used since the relevant comparison is between the joint and overlay optimization (I could change this). 

We define the currency share as $$ s = \frac{K}{K + 14N}$$ where K is the number of tradable currencies and N the number of assets in an economy. 

The bootstrappind to get the confidence interval is done by taking the 91 dates and resampling the series $g_t$ with replacement 10000 times. Then the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of the resampled means are computed and visualized as the confidence interval. We resample blocks of time instead of individual dates (A block of 14 to 16 quarterly rebalances for the in-sample and 1 to 3 for the out of sample). This is done to account for the fact that the $g_t$ series is autocorrelated, and resampling individual dates would not give a good estimate of the confidence interval.

We and additional analysis to check if the currency share $s$ could be attributed to the degradation in performance of the joint optimization. We 
hold the top 50 asse universe and cut the number of currencies that are tradable to $K = 10$ and $K = 6$. We also hold the top 10 assset universe and cut the number of currencies that are tradable to $K = 10$ and $K = 6$ and $K=3$. This gives us an additional five points on the $g$ vs $s$ plot. 

If the hypothesis is correct, we should see that the gap in sample should remain stable across universe sizes, indicating that the joint optimization is able to capture the dependence structure when there is no estimation error, and the out of sample gap should drift towards zero with increasing universe size, indicating that the joint optimization is losing its edge.

Results: The in sample joint program attains a lower risk measure score at almost every rebalancing date (all 91 for es, and mad, and about 93\% of the time for mv). THis is expected by construction (further explained on page 28 for now) since the solution for the overlay optimization is feasible for the joint optimizer and the joint optimizer has more degrees of freedom/ dimensionality to find a better solution. The mv joint optimizer likely underperforms some of the time due to the fact that the joint optimizer uses a different covariance matrix than the overlay optimizer (estimation error). The in-sample advantage of the joint optimization ranges from $0.63\% to 4\%$ and is the upper bound of what joint optimization can achieve before any estimation error is introcuded. 

This upper bound does not behave in line with the hypothesis (only for es). The expectation was that the in-sample gap would remain stable across universe sizes, indicating that the joint optimization is able to capture the dependence structure when there is no estimation error. However, we see that the in-sample gap for mv and mad increases with an increasing currency share, indicating that the joint optimization is not able to meaningfully capture the dependence structure in these cases. In case of optimizing expected shortfall, the in-sample gap remains stable, which is in line with the hypothesis.

THe out-of-sample results are the same for all three risk measures. The out-of-sample gap drifts towards zero with increasing currency share, indicating that the joint optimization is losing its edge. This is in line with the hypothesis and suggests that the benefits of joint optimization diminish as the investment universe expands. In case of expected shortfall, the decrease could be attributed to estimation error, but in the case of mean-variance and mean absolute deviation, the decrease could be attributed to the fact that the joint optimization is just not able to capture the dependence structure in the first place (since the in sample gap moves the same way). 


The additional analysis with the reduced number of currencies is consistent with the main analysis. The out-of-sample gap drifts towards zero with increasing currency share, indicating that the joint optimization is losing its edge. This tells us that the benefits of joint optimization go away as the investment universe expands, and that the currency share (could) is an important factor.

Write the decision vector $\theta=(x,\psi)$, the feasible set is $\Theta$, the empirical risk on the estimation window $\hat f$, and the realized risk on the holding quarter $f^{oos}$. The two programs are

$$\hat\theta_J=\arg\min_{\theta\in\Theta}\hat f(\theta),\qquad \hat x_O=\arg\min_{x}\hat f_A(x),\quad \hat\psi_O=\arg\min_{\psi}\hat f(\hat x_O,\psi)$$

where $\hat f_A$ is the asset-only risk that ignores the currency block. The overlay is a restricted minimiser. It optimises $x$ against a different objective, then it optimises $\psi$ and jas fixed $\hat x_O$.

The in-sample gap is therefore non-positive by construction. The overlay's output satisfies the same constraints, so $\hat\theta_O\in\Theta$, and its second step is a partial minimisation of the same $\hat f$


$$\hat f(\hat\theta_J)=\min_{x,\psi}\hat f(x,\psi)  \le \min_{\psi}\hat f(\hat x_O,\psi)=\hat f(\hat\theta_O)$$

Hence $g^{is}=\hat f(\hat\theta_J)-\hat f(\hat\theta_O)\le 0$.

Now we decompose the out-of-sample gap $g^{oos}$. We define the generalization gap of a fitted $\hat\theta$ as $\epsilon(\hat\theta)=f^{oos}(\hat\theta)-\hat f(\hat\theta)$. Then 

$$g^{oos}=\underbrace{\big[\hat f(\hat\theta_J)-\hat f(\hat\theta_O)\big]}_{g^{is} \le0}+\underbrace{\big[\epsilon(\hat\theta_J)-\epsilon(\hat\theta_O)\big]}_{\Delta\epsilon}$$

The second term is non-negative in expectation. $\hat\theta_J$ is selected by minimising over a strictly larger set than $\hat\theta_O$, so it fits more of the estimation noise and therefore $\mathbb{E}[\epsilon(\hat\theta_J)]\ge\mathbb{E}[\epsilon(\hat\theta_O)]$, i.e. $\mathbb{E}[\Delta\epsilon]\ge 0$. Therefore
 
$$\mathbb{E}\big[g^{oos}\big]=g^{is}+\mathbb{E}[\Delta\epsilon]\ge g^{is}$$

Since $g<0$ means joint is better, $|g^{is}|\ge|g^{oos}|$. The in-sample advantage bounds the realizable advantage from below. The larger parameter set that lets the joint program win in sample is the same freedom that costs it out of sample...

In case of mean variance the above doesnt hold since the covariance matrix is estimated differently for the joint and overlay programs.
