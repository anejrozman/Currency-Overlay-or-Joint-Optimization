\begin{abstract}
Investing internationally exposes a portfolio to exchange rate risk. A common way to manage this risk is a currency
overlay, in which the investor first chooses the asset portfolio and then adds a separate set of currency positions to
hedge or adjust its currency exposure. This thesis studies whether optimizing the asset and currency positions jointly
improves on the sequential currency overlay out of sample, and under which conditions. We compare the two approaches in 
three investable universes that contain up to $140$, $700$, and $1{,}400$ stocks
from 14 global economies, under five risk measures: variance, semi-variance, mean absolute deviation, expected
shortfall, and second-order expected shortfall.
An investor who only minimizes risk earns very similar risk-adjusted returns with both approaches, with or without
regularization. Joint optimization attains lower risk in sample, and out of sample
its advantage is largest in the smallest universe, where currencies make up a larger share of the decision
variables. Whichever approach is used, managing the currencies actively lowers risk compared with full hedging at similar
risk-adjusted returns, and in the larger universes it also raises risk-adjusted returns compared with leaving the
currencies unhedged. An investor who also uses expected returns, by contrast, earns higher Sharpe ratios with joint optimization than with
an overlay for every risk measure in the larger universes because only the joint program can weigh the expected
returns of assets and currencies against each other. For asset managers, these results 
suggest that a separate currency overlay is sufficient when they only minimize
risk in a portfolio with many assets per currency, whereas optimizing the asset and currency positions jointly is beneficial
when the portfolio holds few assets per currency and when expected returns are part of the objective.


\end{abstract}
