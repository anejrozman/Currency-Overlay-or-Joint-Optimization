\chapter{Conclusion}\label{chap:conclusion}

In this thesis, we studied how risk-based portfolio optimization performs for an investor who holds assets denominated
in several currencies and whether optimizing the asset and currency weights jointly improves on the industry-standard
sequential currency overlay. In \Cref{sec:conclusion-findings} we summarize the findings of
\Cref{chap:backtest-analysis,chap:joint-vs-sequential}, and in \Cref{sec:conclusion-limitations} we discuss the
limitations of our analysis and directions for future research.

\section{Findings}\label{sec:conclusion-findings}

Out of sample, the unregularized risk-minimizing optimizers reduce volatility by $34\%$ to $64\%$ relative to the equally weighted
portfolio and their maximum drawdown by up to $30$ percentage points. Without a return
term in the objective, they earn lower excess returns, possibly also because a lower exposure to the common movement of
the equity markets gives them a smaller share of the equity risk premium. On a risk-adjusted basis, they outperform the equally
weighted portfolio, with Sharpe ratios up to $70\%$ higher in the medium universe and up to $105\%$ higher in the large universe, 
but do not consistently outperform in the small universe. The optimizers tilt the portfolio toward low-risk stocks, and a larger
universe offers more of them, while in the small universe they lose more excess return than they reduce volatility.

Regularizing the optimizers further improves their risk-adjusted performance, most in the large universe, where the Sharpe ratios 
increase by $0.17$ on average. 
The penalties limit the extreme positions that the unregularized programs fit to
noise in the lookback window. Compared with the Full optimizer, which keeps the currency exposure fully hedged, the
regularized joint and overlay optimizers realize a lower value of the risk measure they minimize at a similar Sharpe
ratio, so actively trading the currencies reduces risk relative to full hedging without giving up risk-adjusted returns.
The Zero optimizer, which leaves the currency exposure unhedged,
outperforms them only in the small universe. In the medium and large universes, where currency risk makes up a larger
share of the portfolio's total risk, managing it actively is more effective than leaving it unhedged, and the 
regularized joint and overlay optimizers achieve better risk-adjusted performance than the Zero optimizer.


Adding a mean term to the optimizers transforms the problem into a risk-return optimization problem. 
It has a large effect on risk-adjusted performance, increasing the Sharpe ratios by $0.16$ to $0.24$ on average for the deviation
measures and by $0.01$ to $0.10$ for the tail measures. This is not surprising, as deviation measures are
invariant to a shift in the portfolio return, so minimizing them ignores expected returns entirely, whereas Expected
Shortfall and second-order Expected Shortfall already reward a higher mean. 
A possible further source of the gain is currency carry. Since the expected excess currency returns are set to the
interest rate differential, the mean term rewards exposure to higher-yielding currencies, so the currency positions may
earn part of the carry premium that pure risk minimization gives up. The optimizers earn higher risk-adjusted returns than the equally weighted portfolio in the medium and large
universes, and the deviation measures also in the small universe, at about $40\%$ to $75\%$ of its volatility and
maximum drawdown.

Across all backtests in Tables~\ref{tab:baseline}, \ref{tab:regularized-top10} to~\ref{tab:regularized-top100},
and~\ref{tab:mean-risk}, the mean-variance joint optimizer performs best out of sample, and in the small universe the
mean-risk semi-variance optimizers perform similarly. It more than doubles the Sharpe ratio of the equally
weighted portfolio in every universe. Although several of the risk-minimizing optimizers
have smaller drawdowns, the mean-variance joint optimizer still outperforms them on a risk-adjusted basis. The optimizer
benefits from two sources of improvement over its other formulations. Variance is a deviation measure, which gains the most from the mean term, and the
joint program can weigh the expected returns of assets and currencies against each other, unlike the overlay program.

The benefit of optimizing assets and currencies jointly rather than in two steps
depends on whether expected returns are part of the objective. When the optimizers only
minimize a risk measure, the joint and overlay optimizers perform very similarly, with or
without regularization, with joint optimization consistently achieving lower volatility,
 although predominantly in the small universe.
Once a mean term is added to the unregularized programs, the joint optimizers achieve higher Sharpe and Sortino ratios
than the overlay optimizers for every risk measure in the medium and large universes.

Since the lower volatility of the joint optimizers is the most consistent difference between the two approaches, we
compare them in \Cref{chap:joint-vs-sequential} on the risk measure they minimize. In sample, the joint program has an
advantage by construction, since the solution of the overlay program is feasible for it. Out of sample, 
this advantage is largest in the small universe, where the currency weights represent a larger 
share of the decision variables. It shrinks to 1\% in the medium universe and 
disappears in the large universe, as the asset step of the overlay program has more weights to fit the objective on its own.
Since the dependence
between asset and currency returns barely changes across universe sizes, this decline is driven by the
number of assets relative to the number of currencies and not by a change in the dependence structure.

For the deviation measures, most of the small in-sample advantage in risk reduction is realized out of sample, while the larger in-sample
advantage of the tail measures is not. We identify two mechanisms that drive this discrepancy, a tail-fitting mechanism,
in which the joint program aligns the currency weights with the specific worst days of the lookback window, and an
implicit risk-return tradeoff, through which the tail measures reward a high realized mean. Both
mechanisms fail to generalize out of sample. 


Our results agree with the finding of Lucescu and Ulrych \cite{RegularizedMultiCurrencyExpectedShortfallPortfolios} that the benefit of
joint optimization is most pronounced in small universes, which we find for risk minimization, and with the finding of Burkhardt and Ulrych
\cite{SparseAndStableInternationalPortfolioOptimization} that joint optimization outperforms sequential optimization in a
mean-variance setting, which we find in the medium and large universes once a mean term is added to the unregularized
programs.

\section{Limitations and Future Research}\label{sec:conclusion-limitations}

Our analysis covers one base currency (the US dollar), 14 economies, and one out-of-sample period from 2000 to 2023,
so the results reflect the dependence between asset and currency returns for a US investor over this period. For
an investor based in another currency, such as the euro, the Swiss franc, or the Japanese yen, the excess currency returns
and their dependence with the asset returns differ, and repeating the analysis for other base currencies would show how
much the results depend on this structure.

The backtests are also based on simplified trading assumptions, namely constant transaction costs, no market impact, and quarterly
rebalancing, among others.
Since the unregularized and mean-risk joint optimizers have a much higher currency turnover than the overlay optimizers in most cases, higher trading
costs, more frequent rebalancing, and market impact would likely reduce the advantage of the joint optimizers
over the overlay optimizers. A more realistic analysis could include a dynamic transaction cost model and 
multiple rebalancing frequencies, and test the sensitivity of the results to these assumptions.

Three further extensions follow from the results. First, the tail measures are estimated from the empirical distribution
of the lookback window and are sensitive to estimation error. Estimators or formulations that
stabilize the tail, such as the extreme value approach proposed by McNeil and Frey \cite{McNeilFrey2000}, could improve the out-of-sample performance.
Second, we do not test whether the differences in Sharpe ratios between the joint and overlay optimizers 
are statistically significant. Since the return series of the two approaches are highly correlated, applying a
paired test such as that of Ledoit and Wolf \cite{LedoitWolf2008} is a natural next step.
Third, the explanations given for the optimizers' performance, a lower market
exposure, a tilt toward low-risk stocks, and currency carry, could be further investigated by
regressing their returns on market, low-volatility, carry, and other factors.
