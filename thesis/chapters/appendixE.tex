\chapter{Testing Differences in the Sharpe Ratio} \label{app:sharpe_test}

This appendix details the test used in \Cref{sec:joint-returns} to compare the risk-adjusted return of the joint and sequential programs, and reports its results in full. Throughout, $r_{J,t}$ and $r_{O,t}$ denote the realized daily excess returns of the joint and overlay backtests over the same $T$ trading days, net of transaction costs.

\section{The Test and Why It Is Used}\label{sec:sharpe-test-choice}

The quantity of interest is the difference of the two Sharpe ratios,
\begin{equation}
\Delta \coloneqq \mathrm{SR}_J - \mathrm{SR}_O,
\qquad
\mathrm{SR}_i = \frac{\mu_i}{\sqrt{\gamma_i - \mu_i^2}},
\qquad
\mu_i = \mathbb{E}[r_i], \quad \gamma_i = \mathbb{E}[r_i^2],
\label{eq:sharpe-delta}
\end{equation}
under the null hypothesis $H_0: \Delta = 0$.

Two features of this setting rule out the standard tests. First, the two samples are \emph{dependent}: both strategies are evaluated on the same dates, over the same investment universe, from the same price data, and differ only in how the portfolio weights were chosen. Across the $27$ comparisons reported below their daily return series have a correlation between $0.90$ and $0.99$, with a median of $0.98$, so any test that treats the two samples as independent is invalid here. Second, daily equity returns are heavy-tailed and serially dependent, which violates the i.i.d.\ normality assumed by the closed-form test of \cite{JobsonKorkie1981} and its corrected version in \cite{Memmel2003}. \cite{LedoitWolf2008} show that under realistic return distributions this test over-rejects substantially, and propose a studentized bootstrap that is robust to both departures while accommodating dependent samples. It has since become the standard instrument for this comparison, and is what we use.

\section{Implementation}\label{sec:sharpe-test-implementation}

Both Sharpe ratios are smooth functions of the moment vector $\bm{v} = (\mu_J, \mu_O, \gamma_J, \gamma_O)^\T$, so $\Delta = f(\bm{v})$ and the delta method applies. Writing $s_i = \gamma_i - \mu_i^2$, the gradient is
\begin{equation}
\nabla f(\bm{v})
=
\left(
\frac{\gamma_J}{s_J^{3/2}},
\;-\frac{\gamma_O}{s_O^{3/2}},
\;-\frac{\mu_J}{2 s_J^{3/2}},
\;\frac{\mu_O}{2 s_O^{3/2}}
\right)^{\!\T},
\label{eq:sharpe-gradient}
\end{equation}
and the standard error of $\hat\Delta$ is $\widehat{\mathrm{se}}(\hat\Delta) = \sqrt{\nabla f^\T \bm{\Psi} \nabla f / T}$, where $\bm{\Psi}$ is a heteroskedasticity- and autocorrelation-consistent estimate of the long-run covariance of the per-observation contributions
\begin{equation*}
\bm{v}_t = \left(r_{J,t} - \mu_J,\; r_{O,t} - \mu_O,\; r_{J,t}^2 - \gamma_J,\; r_{O,t}^2 - \gamma_O \right)^{\!\T}.
\end{equation*}
We use a Bartlett kernel,
\begin{equation}
\bm{\Psi} = \bm{\Gamma}_0 + \sum_{j=1}^{J} \left(1 - \frac{j}{J+1}\right)\left(\bm{\Gamma}_j + \bm{\Gamma}_j^\T\right),
\qquad
\bm{\Gamma}_j = \frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=j+1}^{T} \bm{v}_t \bm{v}_{t-j}^\T ,
\label{eq:sharpe-hac}
\end{equation}
with the Newey--West bandwidth $J = \lceil 4 (T/100)^{2/9} \rceil$.

The distribution of the studentized statistic is obtained by circular block bootstrap:
\begin{enumerate}
\item Draw blocks of $b$ consecutive observations, wrapping at the end of the sample, until $T$ positions are filled. The \emph{same} index sequence is applied to both return series, so the dependence between the two strategies is preserved under resampling.
\item On replication $m$, recompute $\hat\Delta^{*}_m$ and its standard error $\widehat{\mathrm{se}}^{*}_m$ from \eqref{eq:sharpe-gradient}--\eqref{eq:sharpe-hac}, and form $t^{*}_m = (\hat\Delta^{*}_m - \hat\Delta)/\widehat{\mathrm{se}}^{*}_m$.
\item Report the two-sided $p$-value $B^{-1}\sum_m \mathbf{1}\{|t^{*}_m| \geq |\hat\Delta / \widehat{\mathrm{se}}(\hat\Delta)|\}$ and the interval obtained by inverting the studentized quantiles, $[\hat\Delta - q_{0.975}\widehat{\mathrm{se}}, \; \hat\Delta - q_{0.025}\widehat{\mathrm{se}}]$.
\end{enumerate}
We take $b = 5$ trading days and $B = 4999$ replications, with $T = 5935$. All reported figures are annualized by $\sqrt{252}$.

\section{Results and Interpretation}\label{sec:sharpe-test-results}

The test is applied to every pair of joint and overlay production runs recorded in \texttt{top\_runs.csv}: five risk measures, three universe sizes and both families, giving $27$ comparisons once the three cells whose second-order Expected Shortfall backtests have not landed are excluded.

\emph{No comparison is significant at the $5\%$ level, and none at $10\%$.} The smallest $p$-value is $0.183$ and the median $0.645$; the point estimates are as often negative as positive ($15$ of $27$), with a median difference of $+0.0066$ in annualized Sharpe units. The intervals have a median half-width of $0.073$, so improvements above roughly $0.13$ can be excluded. Repeating all $27$ tests with block lengths from $b = 1$ to $b = 63$ trading days leaves every one of them insignificant.

This is not evidence that the two approaches are equivalent; it is a statement about what the Sharpe ratio can resolve. Over $5935$ trading days the denominator of a Sharpe ratio is estimable to about one percent, while the numerator is not estimable to useful precision at all---paired tests on mean returns bound the difference only to within $\pm 25$ to $\pm 160$ basis points per annum. The volatility reduction the joint program achieves at the top-$10$ universe, between $2.5\%$ and $5.7\%$ in relative terms, implies a Sharpe improvement of approximately $0.003$, two orders of magnitude below what these intervals exclude. An effect of the size actually present could not have been detected however the test were constructed.

Two features distinguish this from a failed test. The same bootstrap applied to the same return series does detect differences in volatility and in Expected Shortfall, with intervals clear of zero at the top-$10$ and top-$50$ universes, so the procedure finds effects in these data when the estimand is one the sample can pin down. And the absence of significance is uniform rather than concentrated---no $p$-value below $0.18$, balanced signs, and no systematic variation across measures, universes or families---whereas a genuine but underpowered effect would at least leave the point estimates sharing a sign. Precision does vary, and unhelpfully: the median interval is $65\%$ wider at top-$10$ than at top-$100$, so the test is least able to resolve a difference in precisely the universe where the joint program's risk advantage survives.
