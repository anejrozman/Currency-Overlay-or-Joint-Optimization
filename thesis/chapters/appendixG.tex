\chapter{Additional Results}\label{app:additional-results}

In this appendix, we report additional results that support the empirical analysis of \Cref{chap:backtest-analysis,chap:joint-vs-sequential}.
In \Cref{app:hyperparameter-sensitivity} we justify the bounded hyperparameter grids over which the regularization
penalties are selected. In
\Cref{app:portfolio-sparsity} we show how many of the available assets and currencies the overlay and joint optimizers
hold and how regularization influences the portfolio construction. In \Cref{app:risk-aversion-calibration} we describe how the risk
aversion coefficients of the mean-risk optimizers are calibrated. \Cref{app:drawdown-analysis} analyzes the drawdowns
of the optimizers, with a focus on the main market crashes of the out-of-sample period, and
\Cref{app:additional-backtest-results} reports the realized values of the risk measures and the expected shortfall
ratios of the optimizers, together with their turnover at each rebalancing date.

\section{Hyperparameter Grids} \label{app:hyperparameter-sensitivity}

The regularized overlay and joint programs have four penalties in the objective, the asset penalties $\lambda_1^a$ and $\lambda_2^a$ and
the currency penalties $\lambda_1^c$ and $\lambda_2^c$, while the asset-only optimizers, Full and Zero, only have the
two asset penalties. In the backtests, the penalties are selected at every rebalancing date by the grid search described in
\Cref{sec:backtesting-framework}. The grid we optimize over is the Cartesian product of the penalty values, with $256$ points for the overlay and
joint programs and $16$ points for the Full and Zero programs. The asset leverage
limit is fixed at $\ell^a = 2$ for all the programs, and the confidence level of expected shortfall and second-order expected shortfall at
$\beta = 0.8$.
 
As the penalty sizes are increased, the optimal weights approach a limiting
allocation, and beyond some threshold a further increase leaves the solution unchanged. A grid whose
largest value lies past that threshold covers the whole range of regularization, and extending it further would only
increase the computational cost of the grid search.

To locate this threshold, we use a similar approach to Lucescu and Ulrych \cite{RegularizedMultiCurrencyExpectedShortfallPortfolios} and analyze each penalty
$\lambda \in \{\lambda_1^a, \lambda_2^a, \lambda_1^c, \lambda_2^c\}$ on its own, holding the other three fixed at zero. We consider an
increasing sequence $0 = \lambda_0 < \lambda_1 < \dots < \lambda_K$.
Let $\bm{x}_t(\lambda_n)$ and $\bm{\psi}_t(\lambda_n)$ denote the optimal asset weights and currency exposures at
$\lambda_n$. The marginal effect of the step from $\lambda_{n-1}$ to $\lambda_n$ is measured in the supremum norm
\begin{equation*} 
\begin{aligned}
    d_n^a &= \left\| \bm{x}_t(\lambda_n) - \bm{x}_t(\lambda_{n-1}) \right\|_\infty
    \quad \text{for } \lambda \in \{\lambda_1^a, \lambda_2^a\}, \\
    d_n^c &= \left\| \bm{\psi}_t(\lambda_n) - \bm{\psi}_t(\lambda_{n-1}) \right\|_\infty
    \quad \text{for } \lambda \in \{\lambda_1^c, \lambda_2^c\}.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation*} 

The two penalty types approach their limits in different ways. Under an $L^1$ penalty, the weights reach their limit
at a finite value of the penalty, a long-only portfolio for the asset weights and a zero overlay for the currency
exposures, and do not change as the penalty is increased further. An $L^2$ penalty
instead shrinks the weights smoothly toward their limit, the equally weighted portfolio for the asset weights and a
zero overlay for the currency exposures, and reaches it only asymptotically, so its marginal effect decays gradually
without vanishing \cite{HastieTibshiraniFriedman2009}. For this reason, we report alongside $d_n^a$ and $d_n^c$ the
portfolio exposure, $\|\bm{x}_t(\lambda_n)\|_1$ for the asset penalties and $\|\bm{\psi}_t(\lambda_n)\|_1$ for the
currency penalties.

The analysis is carried out for the regularized overlay and joint programs of each risk measure on the first lookback
window of the small universe, which spans July 1990 to June 2000 and contains $2{,}610$ daily observations. To account for sampling error, each reported quantity is averaged over five bootstrap replications of this
window, each constructed by resampling its daily observations with replacement.

We visualize the results for the regularized variance programs, whose marginal effects and exposures are shown in
\Cref{fig:hyperparameter-sensitivity-mv-marginal,fig:hyperparameter-sensitivity-mv}. The overlay and joint programs
behave very similarly. The difference between the two penalty types is clearest in \Cref{fig:hyperparameter-sensitivity-mv-marginal}. Once the
weights reach their limit under an $L^1$ penalty, the marginal effect has a sudden drop, to zero for the currency
exposures and by more than two orders of magnitude for the asset weights. 
The $L^2$ penalties do not exhibit such a drop, which is why their largest grid values are interpreted from the
exposure in \Cref{fig:hyperparameter-sensitivity-mv} instead.

For both asset penalties, exposure falls from slightly below the leverage limit to one. 
Once the portfolio becomes long-only, the $L^1$ penalty equals the constant $\lambda_1^a$, so the solution stops changing.
We set the largest grid value to $5 \times 10^{-5}$, by which this has happened.
As discussed in \Cref{app:mv_regularization}, the $L^2$ penalty instead shrinks the covariance estimate toward a scaled identity matrix, since
$\bm{x}_t^\top \bm{\Sigma}_t \bm{x}_t + \lambda_2^a \|\bm{x}_t\|_2^2 = \bm{x}_t^\top (\bm{\Sigma}_t + \lambda_2^a \bm{I}_N)
\bm{x}_t$, so the weights move toward the equally weighted portfolio. We set the largest grid value to $10^{-2}$, by which there are no longer 
any short positions.

The currency exposures carry neither a budget nor a leverage constraint, so $\bm{\psi}_t$ shrinks to zero. Under
$\lambda_1^c$, the currency exposures disappear at $5 \times 10^{-5}$. Under $\lambda_2^c$, they never fully disappear,
so we determine the largest grid value as the point at which the overlay becomes negligible. From $1.7 \times 10^{-3}$
onward, less than one percent of the unpenalized currency exposure remains, and we set the largest grid value to
$2 \times 10^{-3}$.

We perform the same analysis for the regularized overlay and joint programs of the other four risk measures. 
They show the same pattern as the variance
programs. Both asset penalties reduce the asset exposure to one, the currency $L^1$ penalty removes the currency
exposures at a finite value, and the currency $L^2$ penalty shrinks them smoothly toward zero. 

On the basis of this analysis, we construct the grids reported in \Cref{tab:hypergrid}. 
The upper bounds of the asset $L^1$ penalty for variance and
semi-variance are set to $5 \times 10^{-5}$
and $10^{-4}$, while those for mean absolute deviation, expected shortfall, and second-order expected shortfall are set to $2.5 \times 10^{-3}$, $5 \times 10^{-3}$, and $10^{-1}$.
The asset penalty grids
of the variance and semi-variance programs lie one to three orders of magnitude below those of the other risk measures. This 
is because they are measured in squared returns.

\section{Portfolio Sparsity Analysis} \label{app:portfolio-sparsity}

\Cref{fig:sparsity-assets,fig:sparsity-currencies} report the share of
the investable universe in which the unregularized and regularized overlay and joint optimizers hold a position.
\Cref{fig:sparsity-assets} counts the assets whose weight exceeds $10^{-4}$ in absolute value, and
\Cref{fig:sparsity-currencies} the foreign currencies whose currency exposure exceeds $10^{-4}$ in absolute value. On the lines of the regularized
optimizers in \Cref{fig:sparsity-assets}, the markers indicate the rebalancing dates at which the selected asset $L^1$ or
$L^2$ penalties are in the top half of the range.

Regularization lowers the number of assets held in every universe size, and the share held by the regularized optimizers
swings from one rebalancing date to the next due to the selection of the $L^1$ and $L^2$ penalties.
An $L^1$-dominant selection sets weights
to zero and creates a sparse, concentrated portfolio, whereas an $L^2$-dominant selection shrinks all weights toward the equally
weighted portfolio. The swings therefore reflect the optimizers alternating between these two regimes across
the rebalancing quarters, not an instability of the optimizers themselves.

The unregularized optimizers have no such degree of freedom. Besides the budget constraint, their asset weights are only
restricted by the leverage limit $\|\bm{x}_t\|_1 \le \ell^a$, which promotes sparsity in the same way as the $L^1$ penalty. 
As a result, the share of the assets they hold falls with the size of the universe. For variance,
semi-variance, mean absolute deviation, and expected shortfall, it falls from $91$--$99\%$ in the small universe to $60$--$73\%$
in the large universe. For the second-order expected shortfall optimizers, it falls from $70$--$78\%$ in 
the small universe to around $50\%$ in the large universe.

For mean absolute deviation, expected shortfall, and second-order expected shortfall, the unregularized overlay optimizers hold almost no
currency exposure in the medium and large universes, unlike the joint optimizers. With several hundred
assets, the asset step of the overlay program likely already fits the in-sample risk so closely that no currency position can
lower it further. The joint program instead chooses the asset weights and currency exposures together.

\section{Risk Aversion Calibration} \label{app:risk-aversion-calibration}

The mean-risk programs of \Cref{sec:effects-of-adding-a-mean-term} subtract the expected portfolio return, scaled by
$1/\gamma$, from the risk measure, with the same risk aversion coefficient $\gamma$ in the asset and currency steps of the overlay program and in
the joint program. As $\gamma \to 0$, the mean term dominates, and the programs maximize the expected portfolio return
subject to the budget and leverage constraints. The currency exposures then reach the
currency leverage limit $\ell^c = 2$ in the currency whose interest rate differs most from the US interest rate. Without
this limit, which is added to the mean-risk programs in Sections~\ref{app:mean-risk}, \ref{app:mv_mean_risk}, and~\ref{app:mad_mean_risk}, the
currency turnover reaches upwards of $20$, which clearly signals instability. 

The risk aversion coefficient is not selected at each rebalancing date but calibrated once on the first lookback
window of the small universe, as described in \Cref{sec:backtest-configuration}. Like the lookback windows of the
penalty grid search, this window is split into a nine-year estimation window and a one-year validation window. For
every value of $\gamma$ in a grid, the overlay and joint programs of each risk measure are fitted on the estimation
window and scored on the validation window. Unlike the penalties, which are scored by the risk measure, $\gamma$ is
scored by the Sharpe ratio, since the mean term is meant to trade additional risk for a higher expected return. For
each risk measure, we fix the value with the highest validation Sharpe ratio averaged over the overlay and joint
programs and use it at every rebalancing date in all three universes. The resulting values are reported in
\Cref{tab:risk-aversion}. Since the first lookback window ends on the first rebalancing date, 30 June 2000, the
calibration only uses data that is available when the first portfolio is formed and introduces no look-ahead bias into
the backtests.

\toprule

\midrule

The values in \Cref{tab:risk-aversion} are one to two orders of magnitude larger for variance and semi-variance than
for mean absolute deviation, expected shortfall, and second-order expected shortfall. As for the penalty grids in
\Cref{app:hyperparameter-sensitivity}, this is because variance and semi-variance are measured in squared returns.

\section{Drawdown Analysis} \label{app:drawdown-analysis}

\Cref{fig:drawdowns,fig:drawdowns-mean-risk} show the drawdown of the optimizers at each trading day of the
out-of-sample period, that is, the decline of their cumulative wealth from its running maximum, whose largest value is
the maximum drawdown as defined in \Cref{sec:performance-evaluation-metrics}. \Cref{fig:drawdowns} reports the
unregularized and regularized overlay and joint optimizers and the Zero optimizer, and \Cref{fig:drawdowns-mean-risk}
the mean-risk optimizers, each against the equally weighted portfolio. 

To compare the optimizers in the main market
crashes of the sample, we also report their crisis drawdowns, which include the dot-com crash, from the start of the out-of-sample period in July 2000 to June 2003,
the global financial crisis, from 2007 to 2009, and the COVID-19 crash, from January to June 2020. In addition, we
report the recovery time of the maximum drawdown, the time from the peak preceding it until the cumulative wealth
regains that peak.

In the medium and large universes, all optimizers reach their maximum drawdown in the global financial crisis. The
overlay and joint optimizers lose between $24.7\%$ and $38.6\%$, and the mean-risk optimizers between $29.6\%$ and
$41.5\%$, against $55.7\%$ and $56.3\%$ for the equally weighted portfolio. However, they need $7.2$ to $12.1$ years to
regain their pre-crisis peak, against $6.7$ to $6.9$ years for the equally weighted portfolio. Their smaller losses
require smaller gains to recover, between $33\%$ and $71\%$ against $126\%$ and $129\%$, but in the two years after each portfolio's cumulative wealth reaches its lowest point in the
crisis, they gain only $7\%$ to $32\%$, while the equally weighted portfolio gains $86\%$ and $89\%$. The low-risk assets
that limit their losses in the crisis therefore also limit their gains in the recovery. In the dot-com and COVID-19
crashes, they lose $4.7\%$ to $26.3\%$, against $30.2\%$ to $33.9\%$ for
the equally weighted portfolio.

The small-universe backtests differ in two respects. First, the overlay and joint optimizers lose as much in the dot-com crash
as in the global financial crisis, between $26.0\%$ and $36.2\%$ against $23.3\%$ to $34.1\%$, whereas in the medium and
large universes they lose at most $10.2\%$ in the dot-com crash. With ten stocks per economy, there are fewer low-risk assets for the optimizers to move into, as
discussed in \Cref{sec:baseline-backtests}. Second, six of the ten unregularized overlay and joint optimizers and both
second-order expected shortfall mean-risk optimizers reach their maximum drawdown, between $28.2\%$ and $37.1\%$, in
October 2022, and have not regained their early-2020 peak by the end of the sample in April 2023. The equally weighted
portfolio had already regained its pre-COVID-19 peak in January 2021.

Zero leaves the currency exposure of its asset positions unhedged and loses between $33.1\%$ and $44.8\%$ in the global
financial crisis, more than the overlay and joint optimizers of the same risk measure in every universe. Between July 2008 and March 2009, the foreign
currencies lost $16.6\%$ against the US dollar on average, and only the Japanese yen and the Hong Kong dollar
appreciated, so the unhedged portfolio lost on its currency exposure in addition to its equities. The same exposure
helps Zero in the recovery, as $12$ of the $13$ foreign currencies appreciated over the following two years, and in the
medium and large universes Zero regains its pre-crisis peak sooner than 
the overlay and joint optimizers.

\section{Additional Backtest Performance Results} \label{app:additional-backtest-results}

In this section, we report additional out-of-sample performance metrics for the optimizers of
\Cref{chap:backtest-analysis}. \Cref{tab:additional-baseline,tab:additional-regularized,tab:additional-mean-risk}
report, for the baseline, regularized, and mean-risk optimizers, the realized values of the risk measures the optimizers
minimize and the expected shortfall ratio.
\Cref{fig:turnover-asset,fig:turnover-currency} show the asset and currency turnover of the overlay and joint
optimizers at each rebalancing date.

The risk measures are evaluated on the empirical distribution of the realized daily out-of-sample returns, and we report
them as daily values, unlike the annualized metrics of \Cref{chap:backtest-analysis}. When defining the sample estimates of the 
risk measures, we use the notation of
\Cref{sec:performance-evaluation-metrics}. The realized expected shortfall is the average loss on the days with a
loss of at least the realized value-at-risk
\begin{equation*}
    \mathrm{ES}_{\beta,\mathcal P} = -\frac{1}{|\mathcal T_{\mathcal P}|} \sum_{t \in \mathcal T_{\mathcal P}} R_{\mathcal P,t},
    \qquad \mathcal T_{\mathcal P} = \left\{ t \mid -R_{\mathcal P,t} \ge \mathrm{VaR}_{\beta,\mathcal P} \right\},
\end{equation*}
where $\mathrm{VaR}_{\beta,\mathcal P}$ is the empirical $\beta$-quantile of the daily losses $-R_{\mathcal P,t}$. 
The realized
second-order expected shortfall is the sample estimate of \eqref{eq:soes_def}
\begin{equation*}
    \mathrm{SOES}_{\beta,\mathcal P} = \min_{\alpha} \left\{ \alpha + \frac{1}{1-\beta} \left( \frac{1}{T}
    \sum_{t=1}^{T} \left( \left( -R_{\mathcal P,t} - \alpha \right)^+ \right)^2 \right)^{1/2} \right\},
\end{equation*}
and the realized semi-deviation and mean absolute deviation, the sample estimates of
\eqref{eq:svar_def} and \eqref{eq:mad_def}, are
\begin{equation*}
    \mathrm{SD}_{\mathcal P} = \left( \frac{1}{T} \sum_{t=1}^{T} \left( \left( \bar R_{\mathcal P}
    - R_{\mathcal P,t} \right)^+ \right)^2 \right)^{1/2}, \qquad
    \mathrm{MAD}_{\mathcal P} = \frac{1}{T} \sum_{t=1}^{T} \bigl| R_{\mathcal P,t}
    - \bar R_{\mathcal P} \bigr|.
\end{equation*}
The expected shortfall ratio replaces the volatility in the Sharpe ratio with the realized expected shortfall,
annualized like the volatility
\begin{equation*}
    \mathrm{ESR}_{\mathcal P} = 252\, \frac{\bar R_{\mathcal P} - \bar R_f}{\sqrt{252}\, \mathrm{ES}_{\beta,\mathcal P}},
\end{equation*}
and measures the excess return per unit of tail risk.\footnote{In recent literature, this ratio is also referred to as
the STAR ratio (stable tail-adjusted return ratio).}

As for the volatility in \Cref{sec:baseline-backtests}, the optimizers realize much lower values of all four risk
measures than the equally weighted portfolio, and the reduction is larger in the medium and large universes than in
the small universe. Without a mean
term, the overlay, joint, and Full optimizers realize between $30.2\%$ and $45.2\%$ lower ES, SOES, SD, and MAD than the
equally weighted portfolio in the small universe, between $51.4\%$ and $61.8\%$ in the medium universe, and between
$52.4\%$ and $67.8\%$ in the large universe. For the mean-risk optimizers, the reduction is between $26.0\%$ and
$64.1\%$.

Without regularization, the joint optimizers also realize lower risk than the overlay optimizers and, as for the
out-of-sample gaps in \Cref{chap:joint-vs-sequential}, their advantage falls with the size of the universe. Their
realized risk is on average $3.65\%$ lower than that of the overlay optimizers in the small universe, $2.57\%$ in the
medium, and $0.92\%$ in the large universe. With regularization, the joint optimizers realize lower risk in almost all
comparisons, while with a mean term, they do so in all comparisons in the small universe but only in about half of them
in the large universe.

Leaving the currency exposure unhedged raises the risk measures by a similar factor as the volatility. The Zero
optimizer optimizes the same objective as the Full optimizer but is evaluated on unhedged returns. Its expected
shortfall is $1.34$ to $2.33$ times larger than that of the Full optimizer, its semi-deviation $1.34$ to $1.94$ times larger, and its mean
absolute deviation $1.37$ to $2.40$ times larger. The increase in risk when leaving the currency exposure unhedged rises proportionally with the 
volatility. The increase in volatility is $1.35$ to $2.09$ times larger for the Zero optimizer than for the Full optimizer. 

The expected shortfall ratios lead to the same comparison with the equally weighted portfolio as the Sharpe ratios in
\Cref{chap:backtest-analysis}. In the small universe, fewer than half of the unregularized optimizers but most of the
regularized and mean-risk optimizers achieve a higher expected shortfall ratio than the equally weighted portfolio, and
in the medium and large universes all of them do. In every case, these are the optimizers with a higher Sharpe ratio
than the equally weighted portfolio.


Finally, \Cref{fig:turnover-asset} shows that the asset turnover spikes once a year, at the rebalancing date that
follows the annual reconstitution of the investable universe, when the optimizers are forced to rebalance based on the new constituents. 
In \Cref{fig:turnover-currency},
the currency turnover of the joint optimizers exceeds that of the overlay optimizers at $60\%$ to $100\%$ of the
rebalancing dates without regularization, but only at $50\%$ to $74\%$ with regularization. This is in line with the analysis of the average
currency turnover in \Cref{sec:effects-of-regularization}, where the two optimizers differ by at most $7\%$ with regularization.
