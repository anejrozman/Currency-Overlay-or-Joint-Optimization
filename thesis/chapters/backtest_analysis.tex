\chapter{Empirical Analysis}\label{chap:backtest-analysis}

In this chapter, we analyze the out-of-sample performance of the joint and sequential overlay programs across the five
risk measures and three universe sizes introduced in \Cref{chap:optimization-framework,chap:data-and-implementation}.
In \Cref{sec:baseline-backtests} we report the baseline backtests of the unregularized joint and overlay programs,
together with the equally weighted benchmark. In \Cref{sec:effects-of-regularization} we repeat the analysis for the
regularized joint and overlay programs and compare them against two regularized asset-only benchmarks introduced in
\Cref{sec:backtest-configuration}. In \Cref{sec:effects-of-adding-a-mean-term} we add a mean term to the objectives of the unregularized
programs and analyze how it changes their performance.

\section{Baseline Backtest Results} \label{sec:baseline-backtests}

We first report the baseline backtests of the 
unregularized joint and overlay optimizers across the five 
risk measures and three universe sizes considered in the thesis. For Expected Shortfall and second-order Expected
Shortfall, we use the $80\%$ confidence level throughout the chapter.
We compare them against the equally weighted portfolio, which serves as the no-optimization benchmark.

For the small, medium, and large universes, we report in \Cref{tab:baseline} the out-of-sample annualized excess return over the risk-free rate,
annualized volatility, maximum drawdown, Sharpe ratio, Sortino ratio, average asset
turnover, and average currency turnover of the overlay and joint optimizers and of the equally weighted portfolio. All quantities are computed on the out-of-sample excess return 
series produced by the backtest configuration presented in \Cref{sec:backtest-configuration}. We also 
report in \Cref{fig:baseline-top10,fig:baseline-top50,fig:baseline-top100} the 
cumulative out-of-sample returns of the joint optimizers together with the 
equally weighted benchmark, and in the lower panel of each figure the 
cumulative difference between the joint and overlay optimizers for each risk 
measure. The latter helps us visualize the realized return difference  
between the two optimization approaches over time. In \Cref{app:additional-backtest-results}, we also report the
realized semi-deviation, mean absolute deviation, Expected Shortfall, and second-order Expected Shortfall of all
optimizers in this chapter.

\vspace{0.8em}

Across all five risk measures and universe sizes, the overlay and joint optimizers achieve substantially lower
volatility than the equally weighted portfolio. In the small universe, their volatility ranges from $7.71\%$ to $9.17\%$,
against $13.96\%$ for the equally weighted portfolio, a reduction of $34\%$ to $45\%$. In the medium and large 
universes, the reduction is even larger, by up to $59\%$ and $64\%$, respectively. The other four risk measures in
\Cref{app:additional-backtest-results} fall by a similar amount.
The equally weighted portfolio ignores the dependence between asset returns.
Since it already holds a broad cross-section of stocks, most idiosyncratic
risk is diversified away, and further adding stocks does little to reduce
the remaining market risk, which can only be addressed by
taking the correlation structure of the assets and currencies into account. The optimizers instead tilt the portfolio
toward assets with low risk, and a larger universe offers more such
assets to choose from. As a result, maximum drawdowns are
also substantially reduced, by up to $27$ percentage points in the small, $23$ in the medium, and $30$ in
the large universe, relative to drawdowns of $54.77\%$ to $56.26\%$ for the equally weighted portfolio.  Large drawdowns occur
in market crashes, when the correlations between assets rise and diversification offers less protection. The optimizers reduce the portfolio's exposure to these crashes but cannot completely mitigate them, since in a crash even low-risk equities tend to fall together, which is why the drawdowns remain sizeable for all strategies. \Cref{app:drawdown-analysis} shows the drawdowns over the whole out-of-sample period and compares the optimizers in the main market crashes of the sample.

For excess returns, we observe the opposite effect. The overlay and joint optimizers deliver lower excess returns than
the equally weighted portfolio for every risk measure and universe size. This is partially due to the fact that the optimizers are
minimizing risk without any explicit return term in the objective function. We address this in more detail in \Cref{sec:effects-of-adding-a-mean-term}. Secondly, much of the volatility reduction achieved by the risk-minimizing portfolios may come from a
lower exposure to the common movement of the equity markets, in which case the optimizers also earn a smaller share of the equity risk
premium. But excess returns alone say little about performance when the portfolios carry different levels of risk, so we compare them on a risk-adjusted basis.

In the small universe, the Sharpe ratios of the optimizers range from $0.02$ to $0.18$, against $0.13$ for the
equally weighted portfolio, and only the variance and mean absolute deviation optimizers improve on the benchmark. In
the medium and large universes, every optimizer outperforms the equally weighted portfolio, with Sharpe ratios between
$0.41$ and $0.56$ against $0.33$, and between $0.51$ and $0.76$ against $0.37$, respectively.
In the small universe, the optimizers lose more excess return than they reduce
volatility, while in larger universes they can diversify across more low-risk stocks,
and the reduction in volatility outweighs the lost return.
Sortino ratios follow the same pattern, so accounting only
for downside risk does not change the comparison. 

Both the overlay and joint optimizers have much higher asset and currency turnover than the equally weighted portfolio. This is expected since the equally weighted portfolio has fixed target weights and only trades to undo the price drift.
Comparing the asset turnover of the joint and overlay optimizers, we see that it is very similar, differing at most by $6\%$. This tells us that the asset weights are determined primarily by the risk measure being optimized and not by the optimization approach itself. The difference between the two optimizers is in currency 
turnover, where that of the joint optimizer is higher than that of the overlay optimizer in every measure-universe combination, by 
between $11\%$ and $82\%$.  Since returns are reported net of transaction costs, the comparisons above already incorporate these differences. The extra currency turnover does not reduce the risk-adjusted returns of the joint optimizer relative to the overlay optimizer, and the higher turnover of both optimizers does not change their advantage over the equally weighted portfolio.

Finally, comparing the performance of the unregularized joint and overlay optimizers under the same risk measures, across all reported metrics, we see that they perform very similarly.
The joint optimizer does consistently achieve a lower volatility, but the differences shrink as we increase the universe size, from up to a $7\%$ decrease in the
small universe to essentially no difference in the large universe. This is in line with the
joint program being able to exploit the asset and currency dependence structure when the number of assets compared to the number of currencies is smaller. In \Cref{chap:joint-vs-sequential}, we study in more detail how much the joint program reduces the minimized risk measure relative to the overlay program, and why. The other metrics differ by small
amounts, with excess returns differing by at most $0.5$ percentage points, maximum drawdowns by at most $1.96$
percentage points, and Sharpe ratios by at most $0.07$. However, the sign of these differences changes across risk measures within every universe, so one optimization approach does not consistently outperform the other.

\Cref{fig:baseline-top10,fig:baseline-top50,fig:baseline-top100} present the cumulative performance of the overlay and joint optimizers 
alongside the equally weighted benchmark. In each universe, the top panel shows that the joint optimizers perform better 
than the equally weighted portfolio during the first major market crashes in the sample, the dot-com crash and the global financial 
crisis. This advantage is particularly evident in the medium and large universes during the global financial crisis, where the optimizer 
strategies exhibit a much smaller drawdown compared to the equally weighted portfolio. The equally weighted portfolio recovers quickly 
after the COVID-19 market shock and ultimately attains the highest terminal wealth across all three universes, albeit at considerably higher 
volatility and with deeper drawdowns throughout the sample. In contrast, the overlay and joint optimizers follow more stable growth 
paths, consistent with their lower realized volatility reported in \Cref{tab:baseline}.

The bottom panels show the cumulative daily return difference between the joint and overlay optimizers for each risk measure, and confirm that the two optimization approaches track each other closely throughout the sample in terms of returns. The cumulative differences oscillate around zero in every universe size and for every measure, with the exception of second-order Expected Shortfall in the small universe, where the joint optimizer drifts persistently below the overlay optimizer from the early $2010$s onward. 

Overall, we see a consistent pattern across all the considered risk measures. The overlay and
joint optimizers reduce volatility and drawdowns substantially relative to the equally weighted portfolio. The Sharpe comparison depends on the 
universe size. In the small universe, the joint and overlay optimizers do not consistently outperform the equally weighted portfolio, while in the medium and large universes they do. The joint and overlay optimizers produce very similar out-of-sample metrics across all risk measures and universe sizes, with the joint optimizer consistently delivering lower volatility and higher currency turnover but no consistent advantage on risk-adjusted returns.

\section{Regularization} \label{sec:effects-of-regularization}

We now repeat the analysis of \Cref{sec:baseline-backtests} for the regularized versions of the joint and
overlay optimizers. We add $L_1$ and $L_2$ penalties on the asset weights and, separately, on the currency
weights to the objective, following the regularization scheme of \Cref{chap:optimization-framework}. The penalty
weights are selected at every rebalancing date by a grid search over the validation window described in
\Cref{sec:backtest-configuration}. All other aspects of the backtests remain unchanged. We also compare the joint and overlay
optimizers against the regularized Assets-Full (Full) and Assets-Zero (Zero) optimizers, which serve as 
benchmarks for optimizing only the asset weights under the same risk measures, with the currency weights either fully hedged or set to zero, respectively.
\Cref{tab:regularization-sharpe-change} reports the change in the Sharpe ratio between the unregularized and regularized optimizers,
and \Cref{tab:regularized-top10,tab:regularized-top50,tab:regularized-top100} report 
performance metrics of the regularized optimizers and the three benchmarks.

We see in \Cref{tab:regularization-sharpe-change} that regularization improves the Sharpe ratios of the overlay and joint optimizers for most risk measures and universe sizes. 
In absolute terms, the improvement is the largest in the large universe, where the Sharpe ratio
rises by $0.17$ on average, while the effect is modest in the small universe and mixed in the medium universe. Relative
to the low unregularized Sharpe ratios of the small universe, the gain there is substantial as well, $41\%$ on average
for the overlay optimizers and $24\%$ for the joint optimizers, against $28\%$ and $26\%$ in the large universe and at most $4\%$
in the medium universe. The penalties stabilize the weights by limiting the extreme positions that the unregularized programs fit to noise in the
lookback window, which becomes more important when the number of assets increases relative to the length of the lookback
window. The unregularized second-order Expected Shortfall optimizers are the least stable, since the measure is driven by
the squared losses of the few most extreme observations in the lookback window. Accordingly, regularization benefits them the most, raising their Sharpe ratios by $0.38$ in the large universe.

Comparing the performance of the Full optimizer with the overlay and joint optimizers in
\Cref{tab:regularized-top10,tab:regularized-top50,tab:regularized-top100}, we see that actively trading the currencies
reduces risk relative to fully hedging the currency exposure, without giving up risk-adjusted returns. Full solves the
same asset-only problem as the first step of the overlay program, and the regularized overlay and joint optimizers
realize a lower value of the risk measure they minimize than Full for every risk measure and universe size, and a lower
volatility in almost every case (see also \Cref{tab:additional-regularized}). The reduction is substantial in the small
universe, up to $13\%$ for the minimized risk measure and $8\%$ for volatility, but becomes smaller as the universe
grows and stays below $3\%$ in the large universe. At the same time, the Sharpe ratios of the overlay and joint
optimizers are on par with those of Full in every universe, slightly higher for some risk measures and slightly lower
for others. A likely reason why the lower risk does not also raise the risk-adjusted returns is that the objective
contains no mean term, so the currency weights are chosen only to minimize the risk measure and not to earn the excess
currency returns. Looking at the currency turnover, we see that active currency trading is the mechanism through which
the risk reduction is achieved.
In the medium and large universes, the currency turnover of the overlay and joint optimizers is
only $4\%$ to $46\%$ higher than that of Full, so their currency positions deviate only moderately from a full hedge,
whereas in the small universe it is $1.5$ to $2.3$ times as high. 
On the other hand, the Zero optimizer keeps the full currency 
exposure and with it the excess currency returns that the
risk-minimizing optimizers give up, and earns a higher excess return at a much higher volatility. In the small universe
this gives Zero the higher Sharpe ratio for every risk measure, while in the medium and large universes the overlay and
joint optimizers outperform it for every risk measure, by up to 70\%. This performance advantage of the risk-minimizing optimizers over Zero shows the
benefits of active currency management in larger universes, where equity risk is
reduced further and currency risk makes up a larger share of the portfolio's
total risk.

After regularization, the performance of the joint and overlay optimizers remains very similar. Their Sharpe ratios in
\Cref{tab:regularized-top10,tab:regularized-top50,tab:regularized-top100} differ only marginally for every risk measure
and universe size, with the sign of the difference changing across risk measures within each universe. The joint optimizers again
achieve a lower volatility in every measure-universe combination, the only exception being
second-order Expected Shortfall in the small universe, where the overlay optimizer beats the joint optimizer on every metric. The bottom panel of
\Cref{fig:regularized-top10} shows that this happens almost entirely after the onset of the COVID-19 crisis in 2020, so
it is reflective of this period rather than a persistent difference. The bottom panels of
\Cref{fig:regularized-top50,fig:regularized-top100} show that the cumulative return differences in the medium and large
universes remain small for all risk measures throughout the sample. The main effect of regularization on the comparison is on currency
turnover. Without regularization, the currency turnover of the joint optimizer exceeds that of the overlay optimizer by $11\%$ to $82\%$, while with
regularization the two differ by at most $7\%$. The penalties on the currency weights thus shrink the larger currency
positions of the joint program to about the size of those of the overlay program. This does not have an effect on their
risk-adjusted returns, which remain very similar, with no optimizer consistently outperforming the other. 

The results so far share a common feature. All optimizers minimize a risk measure without any return term in the
objective, and while this delivers substantially lower volatility and drawdowns than the equally weighted portfolio, it
also gives up potential excess asset and currency returns. A natural extension is therefore to
add a mean term to the objective, which lets the optimizers trade off expected return against risk. 
A risk-return objective is also where joint optimization could be more beneficial, as the joint program
can account for the dependence structure between asset and currency returns when choosing positions that raise the expected
return.

\section{Mean-Risk Optimization} \label{sec:effects-of-adding-a-mean-term}

In this section, we add a mean term to the objective of the unregularized joint and overlay programs of
\Cref{sec:baseline-backtests}. Each program now
minimizes the risk measure with an added expected portfolio return scaled by $1/\gamma$, where $\gamma$ is a risk aversion
coefficient, as introduced in \Cref{sec:mean-risk-portfolio-optimization}. In the overlay program, the asset step includes the
expected asset returns and the currency step the expected excess currency returns. The expected asset returns are averaged
across the stocks of each economy and shrunk toward zero, while the expected excess currency returns are set to the
negative forward premium, that is, the difference between the foreign and the US interest rate. The choice of $\gamma$ for each risk measure is described in
\Cref{app:risk-aversion-calibration}. All other aspects of the backtests remain unchanged. \Cref{tab:mean-sharpe-change} reports the
change in the Sharpe ratio from adding the mean term, and \Cref{tab:mean-risk} reports the performance
metrics.

Looking at \Cref{tab:mean-sharpe-change}, we see that adding a mean term to the objective raises the Sharpe ratios of the overlay and joint optimizers in 28 of the 30 combinations of risk
measure and universe size. Unlike regularization, which has the highest absolute gain in the large universe, the addition of a mean
term improves the Sharpe ratios by a similar absolute amount in all three universes. Minimizing risk alone ignores expected returns, and the mean term recovers part
of the excess return the optimizers otherwise give up, though we can see that the improvement is very uneven across
risk measures. The Sharpe ratios of the deviation measures rise by $0.16$ to $0.24$ on average, while those of the tail
measures rise by at most $0.10$.

This split is in line with how the risk measures handle the mean of the portfolio return. Expected Shortfall and
second-order Expected Shortfall are translation equivariant, so minimizing them already rewards portfolios with a higher mean. The two tail measures therefore
carry an implicit mean term, which the explicit one only strengthens. The deviation measures are invariant to a shift in
the portfolio return, so for them the addition of a mean term introduces new information in the optimization problem. The average currency turnover is
consistent with this interpretation. In the medium and large universes, the mean term raises the currency turnover of
the variance and semi-variance optimizers to two to three times its previous level, while for the tail-measure
optimizers it remains stable around its previous level. We analyze the implicit risk-return tradeoff of the tail measures in more detail in
\Cref{sec:mechanism-level}.

As we can see in \Cref{tab:mean-risk}, the higher Sharpe ratios come from substantially higher excess returns achieved by the mean-risk optimizers. The excess returns are increased by $1.9$ to $3.6$ percentage points for the variance and semi-variance optimizers and by
$0.8$ to $1.7$ percentage points for the mean absolute deviation optimizers, but by at most $0.8$ percentage points for
the tail measures. This comes at the cost of an increase in volatility, which rises by $0.6$ to $3.4$ percentage points for the
variance and semi-variance optimizers, while for mean absolute deviation
and the tail measures it changes by less than half a percentage point. Even with the increase, the volatility of the variance and
semi-variance optimizers remains at between $50\%$ and $70\%$ of that of the equally weighted portfolio, and the
maximum drawdowns of all mean-risk optimizers stay below $70\%$ of that of the equally weighted portfolio. The mean
term thus raises returns without giving up the lower volatility and drawdowns that distinguish the optimizers from the equally
weighted portfolio. As a result, every mean-risk optimizer outperforms the equally weighted portfolio on a risk-adjusted
basis in the medium and large universes. In the small universe, the deviation measures reach Sharpe ratios of $0.34$ to
$0.38$ against $0.13$ for the equally weighted portfolio, while the tail measures perform similarly at $0.12$ to $0.16$. For the deviation measures, the
underperformance of the pure risk-minimizing optimizers in the small universe is therefore not a failure to reduce
volatility but a consequence of ignoring expected returns. The Sortino ratios show the same pattern, so a
comparison focused on downside risk does not change the conclusion.

The addition of a mean term also changes the comparison between the joint and overlay optimizers. In the previous
sections, neither optimization approach consistently outperformed the other. Now the joint optimizer attains a higher Sharpe ratio than
the overlay optimizer for every risk measure in the medium and large universes, by $0.03$ to $0.17$ and by about $0.07$ on average. The Sortino
ratios confirm this, with the joint optimizer performing better, by $0.02$ to $0.25$. The bottom panels of
\Cref{fig:mean-risk-top50,fig:mean-risk-top100} show that this advantage builds up gradually over the sample rather than in
a single period, with the cumulative return difference reaching about $0.18$ for semi-variance in the large universe and
$0.19$ for second-order Expected Shortfall in the medium universe by the end of the sample. At the same time, the joint optimizer attains
a lower volatility than the overlay optimizer for all risk measures, by up to $0.44$ percentage
points. The lower volatility of the joint optimizers is a persistent finding across all backtests in
\Cref{sec:baseline-backtests,sec:effects-of-regularization}. Intuitively, the joint program can choose assets 
and currencies that offset each other's risk, while the overlay program picks its assets before considering the currencies.
Without a mean term, this flexibility is used only to minimize the risk measure, which lowers
volatility but leaves returns largely unchanged. With a mean term, the joint
program can also weigh the expected returns of assets and currencies against each
other, keeping currency exposure where it is expected to pay and hedging it where
it is not.


\section{Summary} \label{sec:backtest-conclusion}

All optimizers considered in this chapter achieve a much lower volatility and smaller drawdowns than the equally weighted
portfolio. Without a mean term,
the excess return of the joint and overlay optimizers is also lower. They minimize a risk measure without any return term in the
objective and possibly have a lower exposure to the common movement of the equity markets, which would also give them a
smaller share of the equity risk premium. In the medium and large universes, the optimizers achieve a higher Sharpe ratio than the equally
weighted portfolio, but in the small universe they do not outperform it consistently. With fewer stocks to choose from, the optimizers lose more excess return than they
reduce volatility, while a larger universe offers more low-risk stocks, and the
reduction in volatility outweighs the lost return.

Regularization increases the Sharpe ratios, especially in the large universe. The penalties limit the extreme positions
that the unregularized programs fit to noise in the lookback window, which matters more when the number of assets is
large relative to the length of the window. Compared with the Full optimizer, which keeps the currency exposure fully hedged, the regularized joint and overlay
optimizers realize a lower value of the risk measure they minimize, at a similar Sharpe ratio. Without a mean term in the objective, actively trading the currencies therefore
reduces risk relative to full hedging without giving up risk-adjusted returns but does not raise them either, likely
because the currency weights are then chosen only to minimize the risk measure and not to earn the excess currency
returns. Compared with the Zero optimizer, which keeps the full currency exposure, managing
the currencies pays off in the medium and large universes, where equity risk is
reduced further and currency risk makes up a larger share of the portfolio's total risk.

Adding a mean term to the objective raises the Sharpe ratios in almost every case and
by a similar amount in all three universes. The gain comes from higher excess returns,
while the volatility and drawdowns stay well below those of the equally weighted
portfolio. The most significant improvement is for the deviation measures. Since they are
invariant to a shift in the portfolio return, minimizing them ignores expected returns
entirely, and the mean term adds information the optimizers did not use before.
Expected Shortfall and second-order Expected Shortfall are translation equivariant and
already reward a higher mean, so the explicit mean term changes them much less. 

The joint and overlay optimizers perform very similarly when they only minimize a risk measure. The joint optimizer achieves a lower volatility and, without regularization, a higher currency
turnover. With regularization, the currency turnover decreases to about the size of that of
the overlay optimizer. In both cases, neither approach achieves consistently higher risk-adjusted returns. This changes when a mean term is added to the unregularized programs. The
joint optimizer then has higher Sharpe and Sortino ratios than the overlay optimizer for every risk measure in the medium and large
universes, and this advantage is consistent over the sample. Once expected returns enter the objective, the joint program can optimize the expected
returns of assets and currencies against each other, which the overlay program cannot
do since it fixes the asset weights first. This is the setting in which active
currency management adds to the risk-adjusted returns, whereas without a mean term it mainly
reduces risk.

The lower volatility of the joint optimizer is the most consistent difference between the two approaches. We would
expect a difference in this direction, since the joint program can always choose the same portfolio as the
overlay program.
Intuitively, the joint program can choose assets and currencies that offset each other's risk, while the overlay program picks
its assets before considering the currencies, and this matters most in the small universe, where the number of assets is
small relative to the number of currencies. In the next chapter, we therefore focus on comparing the optimizers in terms
of reducing the risk measure they are optimizing for.
