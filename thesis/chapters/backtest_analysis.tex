\chapter{Backtest Analysis}\label{chap:backtest-analysis}

In this chapter, we analyze the out-of-sample performance of the joint and sequential overlay programs across the five 
risk measures and three universe sizes introduced in \Cref{chap:optimization-framework,chap:data-and-implementation}. In \Cref{sec:baseline-backtests} we report the
baseline backtests of the unregularized joint and overlay optimizers, together with the equally weighted benchmark.
We show that the optimized strategies trade excess return for lower volatility and drawdowns, that their
risk-adjusted returns exceed those of the equally weighted portfolio in the medium and large universes but not consistently
in the small universe, and that joint and overlay optimization approaches produce similar realized performance across
measures and universes. In \Cref{sec:effects-of-regularization} we repeat the analysis for the regularized joint and
overlay programs and compare them against the regularized asset-only benchmarks, the fully hedged and unhedged portfolios. 
We show that regularization does not meaningfully change the results, and that the risk-adjusted
returns of the joint and overlay optimizers are not consistently better than those of the asset-only benchmarks. In \Cref{sec:effects-of-adding-a-mean-term} we consider the mean-risk formulation of the optimizers and show that adding a mean term
substantially raises excess and risk-adjusted returns. We further show that the
deviation measures benefit from the mean term more than the tail measures. This split motivates the 
risk-return tradeoff analysis in \Cref{chap:joint-vs-sequential}. The absence of a meaningful difference 
between joint and overlay optimizers on out-of-sample returns motivates the next chapter, where we investigate their performance on risk minimization.

Across the five risk measures, the Overlay and Joint optimizers achieve lower volatility than 
the equally weighted and Zero benchmarks, and comparable to the Full benchmarks. 
In the small universe, the volatility of the Joint optimizers ranges from $7.71\%$ to $8.
72\%$ and Overlay from $7.87\%$ to $9.17\%$, against $13.96\%$ for the equally 
weighted portfolio and $11.70-12.35\%$ for the Zero benchmark, a reduction of approximately $35\%$ and $45\%$, respectively. 
The same holds at 
larger universes, with Joint and Overlay volatility falling to $5.09-5.64\%$ at top-$50$ and 
$4.19-4.66\%$ at top-$100$, again roughly $35\%$ and $45\%$ below the Zero benchmark at each universe size. 
For excess returns, we observe the opposite effect. The Overlay and 
Joint optimizers deliver lower excess returns than the Zero benchmark across 
almost every measure and universe, with the gap widening as the universe 
grows. This reflects the structural trade-off of the Joint, Ovelray and Full optimizers are making. By minimizing 
risk without any explicit return term in the objective function, the 
strategies trade excess return for lower volatility relative to the unhedged benchmarks.

If we look at the Sharpe ratio, we see that the performance of Overlay and Joint optimizers 
improves relative to the benchmarks as we increase the universe size. In the small universe, the Zero 
benchmark outperforms both optimizers across every risk measure, 
with Sharpe ratios roughly $0.1$ to $0.2$ higher. This reverses at the 
medium universe for most measures, and in the large universe the Overlay and 
Joint optimizers outperform the Zero benchmark across every measure except mean 
absolute deviation. This reversal at larger universes reflects the Overlay and Joint 
optimizers volatilty reduction begins to outweigh the excess return advantage of the Zero benchmark. The Full benchmark is harder to beat. 
In the small and medium universes, the Sharpe ratios of the Overlay and Joint 
optimizers are close to those of the Full benchmark, but in the large universe 
the Full benchmark outperforms both optimizers across every risk measure. For example, Full-Semi-Variance at $0.
83$ against Joint-Semi-Variance's $0.66$ and Full-ES at $0.90$ against Joint-ES's $0.76$. 
Sortino ratios track Sharpe 
ratios closely across all strategies and universes, so the 
downside-only risk adjustment does not change the comparison.

Maximum drawdowns of the Overlay and Joint optimizers are substantially lower 
than those of the equally weighted and Zero benchmarks at every universe size, 
and comparable to the Full benchmark. In the small universe the optimizers reduce 
drawdowns by roughly $20$ to $30$ percentage points relative to the equally weighted 
portfolio and by $10$ to $15$ relative to Zero, and at larger universes they are close to the Full benchmark. 
The Overlay and Joint optimizers control drawdowns 
similarly to full currency hedging, without translating this into higher risk-adjusted 
returns relative to Full.

Asset turnover is similar between Joint and Overlay optimizers, which tells us that the asset weights are determined primarily by the risk measure being optimized and not by the optimization approach itself. The difference between the two optimizers is in currency 
turnover, where Joint is higher than Overlay in every measure-universe combination, by 
between $11\%$ and $82\%$.  Since returns are reported net of transaction costs, the comparisons above 
already incorporate this, and the extra currency turnover does not reduce the risk-adjusted 
returns of Joint optimization relative to Overlay.

\Cref{fig:baseline-top10,fig:baseline-top50,fig:baseline-top100} present the cumulative performance of the Overlay and Joint optimizers 
alongside the equally weighted benchmark. In each universe, the top panel shows that the Overlay and Joint optimizers perform better 
than the equally weighted portfolio during the first major market crashes in the sample, the dot-com bust and the global financial 
crisis. This advantage is particularly evident in the medium and large universes during the 2008 financial crisis, where the optimizer 
strategies exhibit a much smaller drawdown compared to the equally weighted portfolio. The equally weighted portfolio recovers rapidly 
after the COVID-19 shock and ultimately attains the highest terminal wealth across all three universes, albeit at considerably higher 
volatility and with deeper drawdowns throughout the sample. In contrast, the Overlay and Joint optimizers follow more stable growth 
paths, consistent with their lower realized volatility reported in \Cref{tab:baseline}.

The bottom panels show the cumulative daily return difference between the Joint and Overlay optimizers for each risk measure, and confirm that the two optimization approaches track each other closely throughout the sample. The cumulative differences remain close around zero in every universe size and for every measure, with the sole exception of second-order Expected Shortfall in the small universe, where the Joint optimizer drifts persistently below the Overlay optimizer from the early $2010$s onward. 

Overall, we see a consitent pattern across all the considered risk measures. The Overlay and 
Joint optimizers reduce volatility and drawdowns substantially relative to the unhedged 
benchmarks, at the cost of lower excess returns. The Sharpe comparison depends on the 
universe size. Unhedged benchmarks perform better in the small universe and the Overlay and 
Joint optimizers perform better in the larger universes. Against the fully hedged benchmark, 
the optimized strategies do not meaningfully improve risk-adjusted returns. Joint and Overlay 
optimizers produce very similar out-of-sample metrics across all risk measures and universe 
sizes, with Joint consistently delivering lower volatility and higher currency turnover but 
no consistent advantage on risk-adjusted returns. 
The underperformance of the Ovelray and Joint  
optimizers relative to the fully hedged Full benchmark, could be attributed to regularization, which we introduce in \Cref{sec:effects-of-regularization} by comparing the regularized 
Overlay and Joint optimizers against the same benchmark. The underperformance relative to the 
unhedged Zero benchmark at small universes points to the optimizers foregoing currency risk 
premia in exchange for lowering the overall risk of the portfolio, which we study in \Cref{sec:effects-of-adding-a-mean-term} by adding a mean term to the objective.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Optimizers vs 1/N} All strategies outperform 1/N on all risk metrics and SR, SOR and ESR in all 3 universe sizes. The risk reduction is larger in ES and Vol than in MD, suggesting that the optimized portfolios are better at controlling tail risk and volatility, but may still be sucseptible to large drawdowns.
  
  \item \textbf{Risk metrics with increasing universe size} 
  We can observe that the risk metrics for the 1/N portfolio do not improve by much as the universe size increases. They do increase substantially for the optimized portfolios. They drop by around 50\% from the small to the large universe, while the 1/N portfolio only drops by around 15\%. This suggests that the benefits of optimization are more pronounced in larger universes, where there is more opportunity to diversify and reduce risk.

  \item \textbf{Joint-Reg vs.\ Overlay-Reg} In the small universe, Joint-Reg is ahead on SR, SOR and ESR  at slightly higher. Still waiting for results on the medium and large universes. Hypothesis: with the large universe there is no more benefit to the joint optimization. 

  \item \textbf{Regularized vs.\ unregularized.} Overlay vs.\ Overlay-Reg and Joint vs.\ Joint-Reg. Regularization does not improve risk metrics but does improve ER, SOR and ESR. It typically reduces turnover, but not in every situation. 

  \item \textbf{Plots} TODO: Compare drawdown depth of the optimizers against 1/N around 2002, 2008--09 and 2020, and compare how that gap changes based on universe size.

\end{itemize}

The backtests of \Cref{chap:backtest-analysis} showed no consistent return advantage of
joint optimization over the sequential currency overlay. Across the five risk measures, the three universe sizes
and both program families, neither approach reliably outperforms the other in terms of raw returns. Both programs are risk minimizers, so the question that remains is whether solving for asset and
currency weights simultaneously delivers a lower value of the risk measure being minimized, and whether that advantage is consistenly realized out-of-sample.
